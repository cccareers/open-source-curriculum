## Goal

Produce a complete, defensible business case for one live opportunity — the artifact this whole course points at. When you are done you will have a document that does two things at once: it argues the deal in the buyer's numbers, and it proves the deal is worth your company's money in yours.

You are working a real-shaped opportunity with real-shaped discovery notes. Nothing is invented for you beyond what appears below. Every figure in your finished case must trace back to this brief, and where it cannot, you must say so on the page.

## The scenario

**Harborview Health Partners** operates 14 outpatient clinics. You sell scheduling and patient-communication software: appointment reminders, automated waitlist backfill, and self-service rescheduling, integrated with the clinic's electronic health record. You have completed three discovery calls.

### Discovery notes — Chief Operating Officer, 3 March

- 41 providers across the 14 clinics
- 128,000 scheduled visits last year
- "Our no-show rate runs about 14%. It has been that way for years."
- "When someone no-shows, we backfill maybe a fifth of those slots. The rest of the hour is just gone."
- 6 schedulers, fully loaded cost $46,000 each
- "A scheduler spends about 11 minutes per rebook attempt — pulling the waitlist, calling down it, updating the chart."
- "We are opening three more clinics next year and adding nine providers. Visits should be up about 22%."

### Discovery notes — Chief Financial Officer, 11 March

- Average net revenue per completed visit: **$148**
- Contribution margin on an incremental visit: **62%**
- "We spend $37,000 a year on an outbound reminder-call vendor. I have never seen a number that says it works."
- "I will look at anything with a payback under a year. Over that, it competes with clinical equipment and it loses."
- Budget for next fiscal year is built in **September**

### Discovery notes — Director of Information Technology, 18 March

- The electronic health record is a major cloud platform with a documented API; integration is supported
- Security review takes **six weeks** and cannot be compressed
- "We are not adding servers and we are not adding an IT headcount for this."
- "The last vendor promised a two-week implementation and it took five months. I will believe a date when I see a staffing plan."

### Your published pricing

| Model | Terms |
| --- | --- |
| Subscription | $95 per provider per month |
| Usage | $0.42 per scheduled visit |
| Hybrid | $3,200 per month including 60,000 visits per year, then $0.28 per visit above the allowance |

Implementation is a one-time **$22,000** in all three models. Terms available are 12, 24, or 36 months; multi-year terms carry a 4% annual uplift on the recurring fee.

### Vendor-published effects

These are your company's marketing figures, drawn from a customer sample. They are the *maximum* claim, not the claim you should make.

- No-show rate falls from **14% to 9%**
- Waitlist backfill of no-show slots rises from **20% to 55%**
- Scheduler time per rebook attempt falls from **11 minutes to 3**
- The outbound reminder vendor is replaced entirely

### Your own economics

- Last quarter, fully loaded sales and marketing cost: **$640,000**, producing **21 new customers**
- Of those, **6 were healthcare**, with **$384,000** of the spend attributed to the healthcare segment
- Company gross margin: **74%**
- Healthcare vertical annual logo churn: **7%**

## What you must deliver

Seven parts, in this order, as one document.

**1. Revenue model recommendation.** Compute Harborview's year-one cost under all three published models at last year's volume, showing the arithmetic. Then recompute all three under the COO's stated growth for next year (three clinics, nine additional providers, 22% more visits). Recommend one model and one term length. Your recommendation must argue both sides: what it does for your company's revenue predictability and growth, and what it does for Harborview's budget risk. Name the contract term you would add to protect whichever side your chosen model leaves exposed.

**2. The baseline.** A current-state table of Harborview's annual costs, one row per line item, with three columns: the figure, the arithmetic that produced it, and the source (which person, which call). Cover at minimum the cost of unfilled no-show slots, the scheduler labor consumed by rebooking, and the reminder vendor spend. Before you use the labor line, check it for internal consistency against the visit volume and say whether it holds.

**3. The conservative future state.** One row per benefit line, with four columns: the value lever (cost reduction, cost avoidance, revenue gain, or risk), the raw benefit implied by the vendor-published effects, the haircut you are applying, and the figure you will actually claim. Every haircut needs a one-sentence reason. Any revenue-side benefit must be converted to contribution margin before it enters the total.

**4. The buyer's numbers.** Year-one total investment, year-one net benefit, year-one return on investment, and payback in months. Then the three-year net at your recommended term. Then a downside case in which every benefit line lands at half your claimed figure, with net, return, and payback recomputed. State explicitly whether the downside case still clears the CFO's stated under-one-year payback bar.

**5. The diagnosis.** A four-column table — stated pain in the speaker's own words, root cause, the capability that addresses it, and the measure that would prove it worked — with at least four rows. One row must be a pain you cannot honestly address, with "we do not solve this" in the capability column.

**6. Your side of the deal.** Using your own economics above: the blended fully loaded CAC and the healthcare-segment CAC; the monthly gross profit on the deal at your recommended model and price; your CAC payback in months; the lifetime value using the simple churn formula, with the annual churn converted to monthly first; and the LTV:CAC ratio. Then recompute payback and LTV:CAC at a 20% discount, and write the two-sentence case you would take to deal desk.

**7. The executive frame.** One page for the CFO. No more than eight lines. Every number traceable to this brief. It must end with the downside case.

## Requirements

- Show your arithmetic for every computed figure. A number with no visible calculation behind it does not count as delivered.
- Attribute every buyer figure to a named source from the discovery notes.
- State every assumption you add on the page, in a clearly labeled assumptions list. You will need at least one — decide what a "lost visit" is worth, whether a freed scheduler hour is a reduction or an avoidance, and what happens to the sixth scheduler.
- Convert revenue benefits to margin. The CFO gave you a contribution margin figure for exactly this reason.
- Use gross-margin payback for your own economics, not revenue payback, and label it as such.
- Present the recommended model in the buyer's units (per visit, per provider, per month) as well as in annual dollars.

## Constraints

- **No number may enter the model that is not in this brief or derived from it.** If you need an industry benchmark, label it as a benchmark on the page and mark that line as low confidence.
- **The vendor-published effects are a ceiling, not a claim.** Every benefit line must be haircut below the published figure, and you must be able to defend each haircut in one sentence.
- **No single benefit line may exceed 60% of your claimed annual total.** If your arithmetic produces one that does, that is a signal about the line, not about the rule — say what you did about it.
- **Respect the constraints the buyer stated.** The security review is six weeks and cannot be compressed. IT will not add servers or headcount. Budget is built in September, which constrains your timeline, not just your model.
- **Do not model headcount reduction the buyer has not agreed to.** Nobody at Harborview said they would cut a scheduler.
- Stay inside the course. Do not build a discount ladder or a negotiation plan; do not design a CRM workflow or a pipeline forecast; do not write a post-sale onboarding program. This is a business case.

## Definition of done

Your case is finished when all of the following are true.

- All seven deliverables are present, in order.
- All three pricing models are computed at both last year's volume and next year's, and one is recommended with a stated term.
- Every baseline line has a figure, arithmetic, and a named source.
- Every future-state line has a lever, a raw figure, a haircut, and a reason for the haircut.
- Year-one net, return, and payback are computed, and the downside case at half benefits is computed alongside them.
- The downside case is explicitly compared to the CFO's under-one-year payback bar, and you say whether it clears it.
- The diagnosis table has at least four rows and at least one honest "we do not solve this."
- Your own CAC, payback, LTV, and LTV:CAC are computed at both list price and a 20% discount, with the annual churn correctly converted to monthly.
- Every assumption you added is listed in one place and labeled.
- No benefit line exceeds 60% of the claimed total, or you have explained what you did about the one that did.
- The executive frame fits on one page in eight lines or fewer and ends with the downside case.
- Someone else could check every figure in your case using only this brief and a calculator.

## Hints

**Start with the arithmetic that will surprise you.** Compute the unfilled no-show slots first: 128,000 visits at a 14% no-show rate, one fifth of those backfilled. Carry that through to revenue and then to contribution margin. The figure you get will be very large relative to the price of the software, and it will be the single most important judgment call in the project. A business case whose headline benefit is twenty times the contract value does not read as compelling; it reads as unserious. Decide how you are going to handle that before you write anything else.

**Check the labor line against the volume line.** Rebook attempts times 11 minutes gives you hours; hours divided by 2,080 gives you full-time equivalents. Compare that to the six schedulers you were told about. If the numbers do not reconcile, the reconciliation itself is a finding worth putting on the page — it means schedulers do more than rebooking, which changes what "freed time" means.

**Be careful about what a freed hour is worth.** Nobody said they would cut a scheduler. But three new clinics are opening. That is the shape of a cost-avoidance argument, and cost avoidance is the honest lever here.

**Two of the three pricing models grow with Harborview and one does not.** The growth scenario is not decoration; it is the argument for your recommendation. Compute all three at 156,160 visits, 50 providers, and see which ones move.

**Your own payback will be uncomfortable.** Healthcare-segment CAC divided into the monthly gross profit on this deal gives a number well over a year. That is the real reason term length matters on this deal, and it is the reason a 20% discount is a bigger decision here than it would be in another segment. Say so in the deal-desk paragraph.

**Watch the churn conversion.** The 7% is annual. The lifetime formula takes a monthly rate. Divide before you divide.

**Write the frame last and cut it hard.** If the CFO reads eight lines and knows the investment, the return, the payback, and the downside, the case has done its job. Everything else is the appendix that proves those four numbers.
