## The renewal is decided long before the renewal

Lesson 4 showed a book of business growing 14% a year while its existing base shrank — a 96% net revenue retention rate propped up by expensive new logos. Nothing in that book was fixable in the ninety days before the renewals came due. By then the champion had already left, the value nobody measured was already unprovable, and the two departments that never onboarded were already the reason the seat count came down.

Retention is the accumulated result of a year of small, deliberate contacts. That is what this lesson builds: a written, dated, twelve-month plan for one account, owned by the person who sold it. The plan has to survive contact with a real week — a rep with twenty-two accounts cannot give every one of them a monthly executive briefing — so it also has to allocate a finite amount of attention on purpose rather than by whoever emails loudest.

One boundary before we start. This is the *seller's* account plan: relationship coverage, value evidence, expansion sequencing, and renewal preparation. The delivery-side motion after signature — onboarding execution, support workflows, adoption programs, and health-score tooling — is the customer success discipline and belongs to se310. You will coordinate with those people constantly. You are not doing their job in this plan.

## What an account plan contains

A usable plan is one page and has six parts. Anything longer stops being maintained by March.

1. **The account snapshot** — current ARR, contract end date, notice period, pricing model, term, and what the renewal mechanics actually are.
2. **The stakeholder map** — who holds influence, in what direction, and where you are exposed.
3. **The value contract** — the measures agreed at kickoff, their baselines, and current actuals.
4. **The touchpoint calendar** — dated contacts for twelve months, each with a participant, a purpose, and an artifact.
5. **The expansion path** — the specific, sequenced growth opportunities with dollar figures and trigger conditions.
6. **The risk register** — what would cost you this account, with an owner and a date for each mitigation.

Everything below fills in one of these six.

## The stakeholder map, and the risk it exposes

Four roles matter, and most lost renewals are a failure of coverage in one of them.

**The champion** wants the product to succeed and will spend their own credibility on it. They are your most valuable asset and your single greatest point of failure. Champions get promoted, reorganized, and recruited away; industry-wide, a meaningful share of surprise churn traces to a champion departure nobody planned for.

**The economic buyer** signs and can cancel. Frequently you meet them once, during the sale, and never again. If the first time they hear from you in eighteen months is a renewal quote, you are a line item, not a partner.

**The user base** determines whether anything gets renewed at all. Utilization is the number that decides seat-based renewals, and it is visible to you continuously.

**The blocker** exists in most accounts: the person who preferred a competitor, owns the system you displaced, or simply believes the money is better spent elsewhere. Blockers do not become supporters by being avoided.

Map each of them with three facts: name and role, their stake in your outcome, and when you last had a two-way conversation with them. The map's job is to surface **single-threading** — the condition where one person's departure removes your entire relationship. The rule of thumb is **three relationships in every account, across at least two functions**, and for a Tier 1 account, one of them must be at the level that signs.

A concrete drill: for each of your top accounts, write down what you would do if your champion resigned tomorrow. If the answer takes more than a sentence, the account is single-threaded and multi-threading it is a task with a date, not an aspiration.

## The value contract: agree what "worth it" means, in writing, at kickoff

The business case from lesson 5 does not stop being useful at signature. It becomes the renewal argument — but only if someone writes down the baselines while they are still measurable.

The kickoff meeting is where you convert the model's fourth column into a value contract: the two to four measures that will define success, their current baseline, the target, and who supplies the actuals. Do it in the first thirty days, because after the system is live the "before" numbers become an argument rather than a fact.

For Meridian Logistics, whose model you built in lesson 5, the value contract reads:

| Measure | Baseline | Year-1 target | Actuals from |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Late-payment penalties | $91,200/yr | $27,400/yr | Controller, GL 6140 |
| Early-payment discount capture | 20% | 65% | Controller |
| Dollars of duplicate payments blocked | not tracked | $24,800/yr | System report |
| Straight-through processing rate | 0% | 78% | System report |

Then you review it on a schedule. Here is Meridian at month nine, on annualized run rates:

| Measure | Baseline | Target | Actual | Realized value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Late-payment penalties | $91,200 | $27,400 | $34,100 | $57,100 saved |
| Discount capture | 20% | 65% | 58% | $68,400 gained |
| Duplicates blocked | 0 | $24,800 | $31,400 | $31,400 |
| Straight-through rate | 0% | 78% | 71% | 1 clerk not hired: $58,000 |

```text
Realized annual benefit = 57,100 + 68,400 + 31,400 + 58,000 = $214,900
Modeled benefit         = $314,640  -> 68% of plan
Annual contract cost    = $65,400
Realized return         = 214,900 / 65,400 = 3.3x
```

That table is worth more at renewal than any relationship. It concedes the two misses honestly — 58% against a 65% discount target, 71% against 78% straight-through — and it still shows three dollars and thirty cents back for every dollar spent. A rep who walks into a renewal with this does not have to defend the price. A rep who never baselined anything is defending it with adjectives.

Two habits make this work. **Baseline in the first thirty days**, before memories soften. And **let the customer's own system be the source** wherever possible; a number from their GL beats a number from your dashboard every time.

## The touchpoint calendar

A touchpoint is a contact with a purpose and an output. "Checking in" is not a touchpoint; it is a request for the customer's time with nothing offered in return, and it teaches people to stop answering.

Every entry gets four fields: **when**, **who** (on their side), **why**, and **what they get**. Here is a twelve-month calendar for a Tier 1 account on a January-to-December term with a 60-day notice period.

| Month | Who | Purpose | Artifact |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Champion, ops owner, delivery lead | Kickoff and value contract: agree measures, baselines, owners | Signed value contract |
| 2 | Ops owner | Deployment checkpoint; confirm baselines were captured | Baseline record |
| 3 | Champion | First actuals against the value contract | One-page scorecard |
| 4 | Ops owner, power users | Adoption review; find the team that has not onboarded | Utilization summary |
| 5 | Champion + economic buyer | Quarterly business review: value to date, roadmap, ask | QBR deck with the scorecard |
| 6 | Blocker or adjacent function head | Discovery in a new department; no selling | Notes and a follow-up |
| 7 | Champion | Expansion conversation one: the AR module, with a mini business case | 1-page case |
| 8 | Ops owner | Usage and capacity trend; flag the overage before finance sees it | Trend chart |
| 9 | Champion + economic buyer | QBR two: realized value, renewal intent, budget cycle timing | QBR deck |
| 10 | Procurement + economic buyer | Renewal terms opened at T-90; multi-year and expansion options | Renewal proposal |
| 11 | Champion | Close gaps; confirm signature path and notice date | Redlines |
| 12 | Full stakeholder set | Renewal signed; reset the value contract for year two | New value contract |

Notice the shape. The heavy contact is in months 1 through 5, when the account is being formed and value is being proven — not in months 10 through 12, when reps who neglected the account suddenly reappear. Notice also that months 6 and 8 are deliberately non-commercial: coverage and early warning, not asks. And notice that the renewal opens at T-90, which is 30 days before the 60-day notice deadline, so the customer never has to make a decision under a clock.

**Anchor the calendar to the customer's cycle, not yours.** If their budget is built in September, your value evidence has to exist in August. A renewal proposal that lands after the budget is set is a proposal asking to displace something.

## Allocating attention across a real book

A rep with twenty-two accounts cannot run twelve-touch plans on all of them. Tiering makes the trade-off explicit.

A mid-market book: **22 accounts, $2,600,000 ARR**.

```text
Tier 1: top 4 accounts      = $1,430,000  (55% of ARR)
Tier 2: next 7 accounts     =   $780,000  (30%)
Tier 3: remaining 11        =   $390,000  (15%)
```

Assume roughly **6 hours a week — about 26 hours a month** — available for account management after new-business work. Assign a cadence per tier and check whether it fits:

```text
Tier 1: 4 accounts x 3 hours/month              = 12.0 hours/month
Tier 2: 7 accounts x 3 hours/quarter = 21/qtr   =  7.0 hours/month
Tier 3: 11 accounts x 2 hours/half-year = 22/6mo=  3.7 hours/month
                                          Total = 22.7 hours/month
```

It fits, with about 3 hours a month of slack for the account that catches fire. If it had not fit, the answer is not to work faster; it is to move an account down a tier, or to hand Tier 3 to a scaled motion, and to say so to your manager with these numbers.

Tier on **revenue at risk plus strategic value**, not on who is pleasant to talk to. Two adjustments are legitimate: an account with unusual expansion headroom can be promoted a tier, and an account with a known risk flag should be promoted temporarily. Everything else is favoritism with extra steps.

## Early warning signs a seller can actually see

You do not need a health-scoring system to spot the following, and each has a defined response.

- **Utilization drift.** Licensed seats flat, active seats falling. This is next year's contraction, visible a year early. Response: find the team that stopped, in person.
- **Champion silence.** Two consecutive touchpoints rescheduled or answered by a delegate. Response: go around, politely and openly, to a second relationship.
- **A reorg or a new executive above your champion.** New leaders review inherited vendor spend. Response: request an introduction within thirty days, with your value scorecard.
- **Support volume shape changing.** Not the count but the type — questions moving from "how do I" to "why doesn't this." Response: a joint review with the delivery team.
- **Value contract went unmeasured for two quarters.** If nobody is producing actuals, nobody believes the actuals matter. Response: rebuild the baseline now, even imperfectly.
- **Procurement contacts you first.** Almost always means a competitive process or a cost-cutting mandate is already underway. Response: treat the renewal as a new sale immediately.

Write the ones that apply into the risk register with an owner and a date. A risk with no date is a note.

## Expansion paths, and when each is honest

Expansion is not a separate activity from retention; it is the same relationship producing more. Four paths, roughly in order of ease.

**More of the same** — seats or usage volume. Easiest, because it needs no new decision, only growth. In a usage model it can happen without you.

**Depth** — a higher tier or an add-on module for the same team solving an adjacent problem they already told you about.

**Breadth** — the same product in a new department, region, or subsidiary. This requires a new discovery cycle and often a new economic buyer, so it is a sale, not an upsell, and it should be planned as one.

**Strategic** — a platform-level commitment across the enterprise. Multi-quarter, executive-sponsored, and not something to attempt before the value contract has two clean quarters behind it.

The gating rule: **expand from proof, not from quota.** An expansion proposal made before the original promise is demonstrably kept converts poorly and damages the relationship you will need at renewal. The month-nine scorecard is the permission slip.

Meridian's year-two expansion plan, sequenced against that rule:

```text
Current ARR (hybrid, 12,000 docs/month)                    $65,400
+ Depth:   accounts receivable module                      +$18,000
+ Breadth: two subsidiaries onto the platform              +$26,000
+ Volume:  20% growth -> 14,400 docs/month
           $3,000 + (14,400 - 5,000) x $0.35 = $6,290/mo
           vs $5,450/mo today = +$840/mo                   +$10,080
= Year-2 ARR                                              $119,480
```

```text
Account-level net revenue retention = 119,480 / 65,400 = 183%
```

That is the mechanism behind lesson 4's arithmetic. A book of accounts behaving like this one carries an NRR well above 100%, and the rep who owns it needs a fraction of the new-logo volume — and a fraction of the acquisition cost — that Rep A needed to reach the same ending ARR.

Two of those three lines carry conditions worth writing down. The AR module depends on the AP results holding through month nine. The subsidiaries depend on a corporate systems decision outside your champion's authority, which means the breadth play needs a second relationship built in months 4 through 8, before it is ever proposed. That is why the calendar has a non-commercial discovery touchpoint in month 6.

## The renewal runway

Work backwards from the notice date, not the contract end date. On a 60-day notice period, the customer's real decision deadline is two months before the term ends.

| Milestone | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| **T-180** | Confirm the exact end date, notice period, and auto-renewal language. Identify who signs now, which may not be who signed before. Confirm their budget cycle. |
| **T-120** | Assemble the realized-value scorecard against the value contract. Identify gaps and start closing them while there is still time to move a number. |
| **T-90** | Open the renewal conversation. Present value first, terms second. Put multi-year and expansion options on the table together so the customer has choices, not an ultimatum. |
| **T-60** | Notice deadline. The renewal should be agreed in principle by now; anything unresolved is escalated today, not next week. |
| **T-30** | Paper: redlines, procurement, signature path, and a named date. |
| **T-0** | Renewed. Reset the value contract for the new term in the same meeting. |

Two failure modes to name. **Arriving at T-90 with no scorecard** turns the conversation into a price negotiation, because value has nothing to stand on. **Discovering the notice period at T-45** is an avoidable self-inflicted wound; that date belongs in the account snapshot from the day the deal closes.

## Practice

You have inherited **Brightline Manufacturing**, an existing account, eight months into its first twelve-month term.

**Account facts:**

- ARR $148,000 on a seat model: 240 licensed seats at $51.39 per seat per month
- Term ends 31 March; notice period 45 days; contract auto-renews unless notice is given
- Active seats have fallen from 218 in month two to 176 today
- Your champion is the Director of Operations, who has now been promoted to VP and has a new director underneath her
- You have never met the CFO, who signed the original contract
- The IT Manager opposed the purchase and preferred an incumbent tool
- Nobody recorded a baseline at kickoff. There is no value contract.
- Two divisions of five were in scope at purchase; a third division has asked for a demo
- Your book: 18 accounts, $2,100,000 ARR; Brightline is your third largest. You have about 5 hours a week for account management.

**Exercise 1 — snapshot and risk.** Write the account snapshot (ARR, dates, mechanics) and compute two figures: Brightline's share of your total book ARR, and the annualized ARR at risk if the seat count is trued down to current active usage at renewal. Show the arithmetic.

**Exercise 2 — stakeholder map.** Map the four stakeholder roles for Brightline. Mark which are covered, which are exposed, and state in one sentence what happens to this account if the promoted champion stops taking your calls. Name the specific single-threading risk.

**Exercise 3 — rebuild the value contract late.** No baseline was captured eight months ago. Propose three measures you would agree with the new director now, where each actual can be sourced from Brightline's own systems, and describe how you would establish a defensible "before" figure this late. State plainly which of the three is the weakest and why.

**Exercise 4 — the calendar.** Build a touchpoint calendar covering the next eight months through renewal and one month past it. Use the four fields — when, who, why, artifact — for every entry. It must include: the executive relationship you do not yet have, a non-commercial coverage touchpoint, a defined response to the utilization drop, and every milestone of the renewal runway placed against the correct notice date.

**Exercise 5 — the time budget.** With 18 accounts, $2,100,000 ARR, and 5 hours a week, propose a three-tier allocation. State the ARR and account count in each tier, the cadence per tier, and prove with arithmetic that your plan fits inside the available hours. Say which accounts lose attention as a result.

**Exercise 6 — expansion, gated.** The third division wants a demo. Write the expansion plan for it: which path it is, the trigger condition that must be true before you propose it, an estimated ARR figure with your assumption stated, and the resulting account-level NRR if it lands and the seat true-down also happens. Then answer in two sentences whether you should pursue this expansion at all right now, and defend the answer with the utilization number.
