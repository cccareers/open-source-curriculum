## Why discovery comes first

The most common mistake business services staff make is opening with the program. You sit down with a plant manager, slide the apprenticeship one-pager across the table, and spend twenty minutes explaining related technical instruction and wage progressions. The manager nods, takes the flyer, and you never hear back.

The problem is not the program. The problem is that you answered a question the employer never asked. Consultative selling flips the order: you spend the first part of the meeting learning what is actually hurting the business, and you only talk about solutions once the employer has said, in their own words, what the problem costs them.

"Talent pain" is the shorthand for that problem. It is the specific, costly people issue the employer already feels: positions open for months, new hires quitting in the first 90 days, overtime bills from short-staffing, a supervisor spending every week training replacements, or an aging workforce with no successors. If you cannot name the pain, you cannot sell the cure.

## The shape of a discovery conversation

A good discovery meeting moves through four layers. You do not need to follow them rigidly, but you should touch each one before you start recommending anything.

| Layer | What you are learning | Sample questions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Situation | The basic facts of their workforce | "How many people work in production? Which roles do you hire for most often?" |
| Problem | Where it hurts | "Which roles are hardest to fill right now? What happens when a new hire doesn't work out?" |
| Impact | What the pain costs | "How long does that position sit open? What does that do to overtime or missed orders?" |
| Future | What success would look like | "If this were solved a year from now, what would be different?" |

Most staff ask plenty of situation questions and almost no impact questions. Impact is where the employer starts to quantify the problem for you, and those numbers become the foundation of the business case you will build in the next lesson.

## Listening techniques that keep them talking

- **Ask open questions.** "Tell me about..." and "Walk me through..." get stories. "Do you have trouble hiring?" gets a yes or no.
- **Follow the energy.** When the employer sighs, laughs, or says "don't get me started," stop and ask, "What happened?" That is where the pain lives.
- **Reflect and confirm.** "So if I'm hearing you right, you've lost three CNC operators this year and each one took about four months to replace?" Confirming shows you listened and corrects your notes before you build on them.
- **Ask for the number.** When they say "a lot of turnover," ask, "Roughly how many people a year?" Estimates are fine; you are not auditing them.
- **Stay quiet.** After a good question, wait. Silence is uncomfortable, and the employer will usually fill it with something useful.

## Worked example: Delta Fabrication

Jamal, a business services representative with Valley Regional Workforce Board, meets Teresa, the operations manager at Delta Fabrication, a 60-person metal shop.

> **Jamal:** Before I tell you anything about us, I'd like to understand your shop. Which roles are you hiring for most often?
>
> **Teresa:** Welders. Always welders.
>
> **Jamal:** Walk me through what happens when you post a welder job.
>
> **Teresa:** We get applicants, but half of them can't pass our weld test. The ones who can usually have offers from three other shops.
>
> **Jamal:** How long does a welder position stay open?
>
> **Teresa:** Three, four months. Meanwhile my guys are on overtime and I'm turning down rush jobs.
>
> **Jamal:** Do you have a sense of what the overtime or the lost jobs cost you?
>
> **Teresa:** Overtime alone is probably a few thousand a month. I don't even want to think about the jobs we've passed on.
>
> **Jamal:** And your current welders, what does the age picture look like?
>
> **Teresa:** Two of my best are retiring in the next couple of years. Honestly, that keeps me up at night.

Jamal has not mentioned apprenticeship yet. But he now knows the role (welder), the problem (unqualified applicants, competitive market), the impact (months-long vacancies, overtime, lost work), and a future risk (retirements). That is everything he needs.

## Capturing what you learned

Write a short discovery note right after the meeting, while it is fresh. Use your board's business services record or CRM if it has a field for this. A simple structure:

```text
Employer: Delta Fabrication (60 employees, metal fabrication)
Contact: Teresa, Operations Manager (decision-maker for hiring: yes)
Priority role: Welder
Pain: Applicants fail weld test; qualified candidates have multiple offers
Impact: Vacancies open 3-4 months; overtime est. several thousand/month; rush jobs declined
Future risk: 2 senior welders retiring within ~2 years
Success looks like: Reliable pipeline of welders who pass the shop test
Next step: Follow-up meeting in 2 weeks with cost numbers
```

## Discovery checklist

- [ ] I asked more questions than I answered in the first half of the meeting.
- [ ] I can name the priority role in one phrase.
- [ ] I have at least one rough number for the impact (time, money, or people).
- [ ] I know who makes the hiring and training decision.
- [ ] I confirmed my understanding back to the employer.
- [ ] We agreed on a specific next step and date.

## Practice

Work in pairs. One person plays the employer using the card below; the other runs a ten-minute discovery conversation without mentioning any program by name. Then switch roles with a new card you write yourselves.

**Employer card:** You are Linh, the HR director at Sunrise Senior Living, which runs three assisted-living facilities. You cannot keep certified nursing assistants; many leave within their first few months for hospital jobs. You rely on a staffing agency to cover shifts, and the agency bill keeps growing. Reveal the agency cost only if asked about impact.

After each round:

1. The interviewer writes a discovery note in the format above.
2. The "employer" scores the interviewer on the checklist and names the one question that got the most useful answer.
3. Discuss as a group: Which impact questions worked best? Where did the interviewer drift into pitching too early?
