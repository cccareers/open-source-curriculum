## Objections are a good sign

When an employer raises an objection, they are thinking seriously about saying yes. The employer who has no questions is usually the one who will never call you back. Your job is not to win an argument; it is to understand the worry behind the objection and answer it honestly.

## A four-step response pattern

Use the same pattern for every objection, so you are never improvising under pressure.

1. **Acknowledge.** Show you heard it and that it is reasonable. "That's a fair concern. A lot of employers ask that."
2. **Clarify.** Find out what is really behind it. "When you say paperwork, is it the setup, the ongoing reporting, or both?"
3. **Respond.** Give a short, honest answer, using their numbers where you can.
4. **Confirm.** Check that it landed. "Does that address it, or is there more to it?"

The clarify step is the one people skip. "It costs too much" might mean "I have no budget this quarter," "I don't believe the return," or "I'm not the one who decides." Each needs a different answer.

## The five objections you will hear most

| # | Objection | What is usually behind it | Core response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | "It costs too much." | Fear of paying full wages for low productivity; no budget line for training | Return to the business case: compare the cost of vacancies and turnover with apprentice wages and available offsets |
| 2 | "The paperwork is a nightmare." | Bad past experience with government programs; no HR capacity | Explain who does what, and that intermediaries and your team can carry much of the setup |
| 3 | "I'll train them and they'll leave." | Past poaching by competitors | Progressive wages and a credential tied to *their* shop give a reason to stay; compare with the turnover they already have |
| 4 | "We don't have time to mentor." | Supervisors already stretched | Mentoring is structured and shared; it replaces the informal, repeated training they already do for every new hire who quits |
| 5 | "We already train our own people." | Pride in an in-house system; seeing apprenticeship as outside interference | Build on what they have: their existing training can become the on-the-job portion, with structure and recognition added |

## Sample responses

These are starting points. Put them in your own words, and never promise something you have not confirmed.

**1. Cost.**
"You mentioned about $30,000 a year in overtime from open welder positions. An apprentice starts at a lower rate and your supervisors are already training new hires anyway. If your new hires are eligible, an OJT contract can offset part of their wages during training. I'll confirm the exact rate under our board's policy before you commit to anything."

**2. Paperwork.**
"Let's separate it out. Registering a program means writing standards once. If you join an existing program run by an intermediary, they hold the standards and handle much of the reporting. On our side, I'll handle the WIOA eligibility and contract paperwork with you. What part worries you most?"

**3. Poaching.**
"Right now you're losing welders to shops paying a little more. An apprentice sees a raise every few months on a schedule, and finishes with a credential earned at your shop. That's a reason to stay that a job posting can't match. You're already training people who leave. This gives them a reason not to."

**4. Mentor time.**
"How many hours did your lead spend last year training people who didn't stay? A mentor plan puts that time into fewer, better-matched people. We can also look at whether incumbent worker training funds could help prepare your lead as a mentor; I'd need to check our board's current rules."

**5. We train our own.**
"That's a strength. Your current training could become the on-the-job part of the program. What apprenticeship adds is a written skills list, scheduled raises, classroom instruction, and a recognized credential. Would it help to map what you already do against a sample work process?"

## What not to do

- Do not argue or correct the employer's experience. If a past program was painful, believe them.
- Do not overpromise funding, timelines, or approval. Say "I'll confirm" and then do it.
- Do not answer an objection you have not clarified. You may solve the wrong problem.
- Do not bury the employer in regulation. Plain answers build trust.

## When the honest answer is "not right now"

Sometimes the objection is real and decisive: the employer is cutting staff, has no supervisor who could mentor, or needs a fully skilled worker next week. Say so. Offer a smaller next step, such as a referral of job-ready candidates or a check-in next quarter, and record it. A business services representative who tells the truth gets the next call.

## Practice

Form groups of three: employer, representative, observer.

1. The employer draws one of the five objections (write them on cards) and adds a personal detail, for example, "We tried a state program in 2019 and the reporting buried my office manager."
2. The representative responds using all four steps: acknowledge, clarify, respond, confirm.
3. The observer notes whether each step happened and whether the representative promised anything that would need confirmation.

Rotate until each person has handled at least two objections. Then discuss as a full group: Which objection was hardest? What did the clarify question uncover that changed the answer?
