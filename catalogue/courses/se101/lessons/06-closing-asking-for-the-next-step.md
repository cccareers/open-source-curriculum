## The last ninety seconds

Most sales conversations end badly, and not in a dramatic way. They end like this: "Great, this has been really helpful — I'll send over some materials and follow up in a week or so." Everyone says goodbye feeling positive. Nothing has been agreed. The materials get skimmed, the follow-up email goes unanswered, and three weeks later the representative writes "unresponsive" in a note and the deal quietly stops existing.

The fix is not a clever technique. It is the discipline of never ending a conversation without a specific, dated commitment from both sides. That is the whole objective of this lesson, and the last ninety seconds of a call are where it lives or dies.

## Advance versus continuation

Two ways a call can end.

A **continuation** ends with warmth and no commitment. "Sounds great, let us think it over." "Send the deck." "I'll loop in my colleague at some point." Nothing has a date, and nothing is owed by the customer.

An **advance** ends with an action, owned by a named person, on a stated date, that moves the deal to its next stage. "Thursday the 14th at 10, you, me, and your IT lead, and I'll send three sample records for us to look at together." The customer has agreed to spend something — time, attention, an introduction — and that expenditure is the actual evidence of interest. Words are cheap; a diary entry is not.

Judge your own calls on this single measure. It is uncomfortable at first, because a large fraction of calls that felt wonderful turn out to have ended in continuations.

Note that an advance does not have to be a signature. Advances are proportional to where you are: after a first conversation the right advance is a discovery call with the person who owns the process. After discovery it is a demonstration with their data. After a demonstration it is a review with the person who approves the spend. Asking for a signature at the end of a first call is not bold, it is a mistake — you are asking for a commitment the customer has no basis to make, and the refusal costs you the smaller ask you could have got.

## The short recap that earns the ask

Before you ask for anything, spend thirty seconds making the case. This is what a "pitch" is in a real conversation — not a presentation, a paragraph. Three parts, in this order.

**Their problem, in their words.** "Month-end runs three days over because reconciliation is manual."

**What changes, specifically.** "The piece that matters to you is automatic matching — the reconciliation stops being a manual pass."

**Why they should believe it.** "The way to prove that is one month of your real data, not a demo of ours."

That third part is doing more than it looks. It converts your claim into a proposed test, and a proposed test is already most of a next step. Most good closes are simply the recap with the last sentence turned into a question.

Keep it short and keep it theirs. If your recap contains a phrase they never used, it is your recap and not theirs, and it will not persuade.

## Asking well

Six properties of a good ask.

**Specific.** Name the activity. "A working session" beats "a follow-up."

**Dated.** Offer two concrete options rather than an open field. "Would Tuesday morning or Thursday afternoon work better?" An open "when suits you?" hands the customer an unbounded task, and unbounded tasks get postponed.

**Named.** Say who needs to be there. "You, me, and whoever owns the data on your side."

**Two-sided.** State what you will do as well. "I'll have the sample records ready the day before." A commitment you also carry is far easier to accept.

**Proportional.** One step, not three. Asking for a demo, a security review, and pricing approval in one breath produces a no to all of it.

**Then silent.** Ask, and stop talking. This is the hardest habit in the lesson. The pause after an ask feels much longer to you than to the customer, and the reflex to fill it — by softening the ask, offering an escape, or adding a fourth reason — is what turns advances back into continuations. Ask, then wait, however long it takes.

## When the answer is not yes

**"Let me think about it."** Almost always means there is an unresolved concern you have not heard. Treat it exactly as you learned to treat an objection: acknowledge, then ask one clarifying question. "Of course. So I send the right thing over — is it the fit you're weighing, or the timing?" Then handle what comes back and ask again for a smaller step.

**"Send me the pricing and I'll take it from there."** The deal is leaving your hands. Attach a date to the handoff: "Happy to. Can we put twenty minutes in for the Thursday after, so I can walk your director through it rather than leaving them with a PDF?"

**"I need to check with my manager."** A stakeholder problem, not a decision problem. The ask becomes an introduction: "Makes sense. Would it be easier if I joined that conversation, or would you rather I give you a one-page summary you can forward?" Both options are advances.

**A genuine no.** Take it cleanly, thank them, and ask one question: what would have had to be different? Then agree when it would be sensible to talk again, and write it down. Representatives who take a no gracefully get called back; representatives who negotiate against a no do not.

## Write it down, same day

An advance that lives only in memory is half an advance. Send a short recap within a few hours: what you understood the problem to be, what you agreed to do, what they agreed to do, and the date. Four or five lines, no attachments unless something was promised.

This is not administrative box-ticking. It gives the customer something to forward internally, it lets them correct your understanding in writing before you build on it, and it makes the commitment real to both of you. It is also the artifact that a colleague inherits if the deal changes hands.

## Worked example

The end of a discovery call with Priya, the support manager.

> **Rep:** Before we run out of time — let me summarize where I think we are. First response is breached about eleven times a quarter, and it happens when tickets land during a handover and sit in the shared inbox. That number goes in front of your director every quarter, which is the part that actually costs you. *(their problem, their words)*
>
> **Priya:** That's right.
>
> **Rep:** The piece of ours that speaks to that is automatic assignment on arrival, so nothing sits unowned during a handover. I don't want to just assert that, though. The way to test it is to take last quarter's eleven breaches and walk through what would have happened to each one. *(what changes, and the proof)*
>
> **Priya:** That would be interesting, actually.
>
> **Rep:** Then here's what I'd suggest. Forty-five minutes with you and whoever can pull that ticket history — is that Sam? I'll prepare the walkthrough beforehand. Would Tuesday the 12th in the morning work, or is Thursday better? *(specific, named, two-sided, dated, two options)*
>
> *(silence)*
>
> **Priya:** Thursday. Sam's off Tuesdays.
>
> **Rep:** Thursday it is. I'll send an invite this afternoon and a note confirming what we'll need from Sam.

Total elapsed time: under two minutes. There is nothing clever in it. It is a summary, one capability, a proposed test, and a dated ask followed by silence.

## Practice

1. Write the thirty-second recap — problem, what changes, why believe it — for the field-service scenario you have been working with. Read it aloud and time it. If it runs over forty seconds, cut it, and cut adjectives before you cut facts.
2. Draft three different advances for the same conversation: one appropriate after a first call, one after discovery, one after a demonstration. For each, write it as a single sentence containing an activity, the people, and two date options.
3. In pairs, deliver the recap and the ask. The customer's only instruction is to say nothing for a full ten seconds after the ask. The representative must not speak first. Do this three times; it stops being unbearable around the third.
4. Run the same close again with the customer responding "let me think about it." Handle it in no more than four sentences and land a smaller dated step.
5. Write the same-day recap email for whichever round went best: problem, your commitment, their commitment, date. Five lines maximum. Have your partner read it and tell you what they would have to do next — if they cannot say without asking you, rewrite it.
