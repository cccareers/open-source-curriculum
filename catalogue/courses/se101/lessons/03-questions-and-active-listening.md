## The job of this conversation

In a discovery conversation your goal is not to be interesting. It is to leave with an accurate, confirmed picture of what the customer needs — accurate enough that they would recognize it if you read it back, and confirmed because you actually did read it back.

That is a narrower goal than it sounds. New representatives consistently leave discovery calls with a page of notes and no idea what the customer actually needs, because they collected topics instead of needs. "They mentioned reporting" is a topic. "Their controller spends the last three days of every month reconciling numbers by hand and misses the board deadline about half the time" is a need. The difference is entirely a function of the questions you asked and whether you listened to the answers or waited for your turn.

This lesson is about producing that difference. It has two halves, and they are inseparable: the questions that open a customer up, and the listening that turns what they say into something you can confirm.

## Open questions and closed questions

A **closed question** can be answered with yes, no, or a single value. *Do you use a spreadsheet for this? How many people are on the team? Is this a priority this quarter?*

An **open question** cannot. It requires the customer to construct an answer. *Walk me through how that works today. What happens when it goes wrong? What have you already tried?*

Both are useful, and the common advice to "always ask open questions" is wrong. Closed questions are how you confirm a specific fact and how you close a loop. The failure mode is not using closed questions; it is using *only* closed questions, which turns a conversation into an interrogation and — critically — limits every answer to information you already thought to ask about. Open questions are the only kind that can surprise you, and the whole point of discovery is to be surprised.

A practical ratio for an entry-level call: mostly open, with closed questions used deliberately to pin down a number, a name, or a date.

Some open forms that reliably produce real answers:

- **Process:** "Walk me through what happens from the moment a request comes in."
- **Frequency and volume:** "How often does that happen in a typical week?"
- **Failure:** "What does it look like when that goes wrong?"
- **Impact:** "Who feels it when it goes wrong, and what do they do about it?"
- **History:** "What have you already tried?"
- **Consequence of doing nothing:** "If this stays exactly as it is through next year, what happens?"

That last one does a great deal of work. It is not a pressure tactic; it is a genuine question, and sometimes the honest answer is "nothing much," which tells you this is not a deal worth six weeks of your time.

## Questions that damage the conversation

**Leading questions.** "So it sounds like accuracy is really important to you, right?" You have just told the customer the answer and invited them to agree. They will agree, out of politeness, and you will have learned nothing. Ask "what goes wrong when the numbers are off?" instead.

**Stacked questions.** "How many people touch the process, and how long has it been that way, and who owns it?" The customer answers the last one and you lose the first two. Ask one question and stop.

**Questions you could have answered yourself.** Asking what the company does, when it is the first line of their homepage, spends your credibility to save you five minutes.

**Premature pitching disguised as a question.** "Would it be helpful if you could see all of that in one place?" This is a product claim wearing a question mark. Everyone recognizes it. Save the recommendation for after you understand the problem — that is the next lesson's work, and it depends entirely on this one.

## Active listening is a set of visible behaviors

"Listening" is not a state of mind you can claim. It is a set of things you do that the customer can observe. Four of them matter most.

**Silence.** When the customer stops talking, count two beats before you speak. This is genuinely uncomfortable on a video call, and it is the highest-yield habit in this lesson. The most valuable sentence in a discovery call is very often the one the customer adds into the pause — the qualification, the caveat, the thing they were not sure they should say.

**Minimal encouragers.** "Mm-hm." "Right." "Go on." Small verbal nods that cost nothing and keep the other person talking. On a call with the camera off, these are the only signal that you are still there.

**Note-taking, out loud.** Say "let me get that down" and type. It signals that what they said mattered, and it slows the conversation to a pace where you can actually think. Capture their words verbatim where you can — "a nightmare at month end" is far more useful later than your paraphrase "reporting inefficiency."

**Following the thread.** Build your next question out of the last thing they said rather than the next item on your list. A prepared list of questions is a safety net, not a script; if you never depart from it, you are conducting a survey.

## Confirming: the part people skip

Surfacing a need is only half the competency. The other half is confirming it, out loud, before you move on. The mechanic is a **playback**: a short summary in the customer's own language, ending with an explicit invitation to correct you.

> "Let me make sure I have this right. Exception handling is manual today — a dispatcher retypes each one into a spreadsheet. That's about ninety minutes a day each, and it's worst on Mondays. The real cost isn't the typing, it's that customers don't get called back and you hear about it from the account team. Have I got that right, or did I miss something?"

Three things make that playback work. It uses her words, not yours. It separates the mechanism (retyping) from the consequence (missed callbacks), which is what she actually cares about. And it ends with a real question, phrased so that correcting you is easy.

Play back at natural breaks — after a major topic, and again at the end of the call. When a customer corrects your playback, that is the conversation working exactly as designed. An uncorrected playback that was wrong is far more expensive: everything you propose afterwards will be built on it.

## Worked example

A short exchange, annotated. The representative is talking to Priya, who manages a support team.

> **Rep:** Walk me through what happens when a customer emails support on a Monday morning. *(open, process)*
>
> **Priya:** It goes into a shared inbox, and whoever's on rota picks it up. Usually.
>
> **Rep:** *(two-beat pause)*
>
> **Priya:** ...I say usually, because if it comes in during a handover it can sit there for a few hours. That's where we get in trouble.
>
> **Rep:** Tell me about "in trouble." *(open, follows her word)*
>
> **Priya:** We have a four-hour first response commitment with our enterprise accounts. We breached it eleven times last quarter.
>
> **Rep:** Eleven — is that counted somewhere, or is that from memory? *(closed, pins down a fact)*
>
> **Priya:** It's in the quarterly review deck. My director presents it.
>
> **Rep:** So the misses are visible to your director every quarter. What happens in that meeting when the number goes up? *(open, impact)*

Notice what the representative has not done: no product mentioned, no solution offered, no leading question. Notice also that the single most important fact — eleven breaches, visible to a director — arrived because of a pause and a follow-up on one of Priya's own words.

## Practice

Do this with a partner playing the customer. Give them this card and do not read it yourself: *You run scheduling for a home-services company with 30 field technicians. Jobs are booked by phone and written onto a whiteboard, then texted to technicians each morning. Roughly twice a week a job is missed or double-booked. Your owner has told you to "sort it out" before the summer. You will only mention the missed jobs if asked directly about what goes wrong.*

1. Run a ten-minute discovery conversation. Rules: no product talk of any kind, at least six open questions, and you must pause a full two beats after every answer.
2. Take notes in two columns — the customer's exact words on the left, your interpretation on the right. Keep them separate.
3. In the last two minutes, deliver a playback of no more than four sentences using their words, ending with "have I got that right, or did I miss something?" Write down verbatim how they corrected you.
4. Debrief with your partner on one question only: at what point in the conversation did they first feel understood? Compare that to the point where you thought they did.
5. Re-run the same scenario with a different partner and one constraint: you may ask only five questions total. Notice which ones you keep.

## Check your understanding

1. Rewrite this leading question as an open one: "So reporting is a real headache for you, right?" *(One good answer: "What happens when a report is late or wrong?")*
2. When is a closed question the right tool? *(Answer: to pin down a specific fact — a number, a name, a date — or to close a loop, as in "Eleven — is that counted somewhere?")*
3. What are the three properties of a good playback? *(Answer: it uses the customer's words, separates the mechanism from the consequence, and ends with an easy invitation to correct you.)*
