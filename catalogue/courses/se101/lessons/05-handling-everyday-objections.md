## An objection is not a rejection

"It's too expensive." "We're happy with what we have." "Send me some information and I'll circulate it." Every new representative hears these in their first week and reads them as the end of the conversation. They are usually the opposite. A person who has decided not to buy generally says nothing at all; they go quiet, or they are politely unavailable. Someone who takes the trouble to tell you why they hesitate is still in the conversation.

That reframe matters because it changes your body language and your tone before it changes your words. If you hear an objection as an attack, you defend. If you hear it as information, you get curious. Buyers can tell the difference immediately, and it is most of the battle.

The objective of this lesson is narrow and behavioral: respond to a routine objection without discounting and without arguing. Both of those are reflexes, both feel productive in the moment, and both cost you the deal in different ways.

## Why not discount

The reflex to drop the price when someone says "too expensive" is enormously strong, because it appears to solve the problem instantly. It does three bad things.

It answers a question that was probably not asked. "Too expensive" almost never means "the number is arithmetically too large." It usually means "I don't yet see enough value to justify this number," or "I have no budget line for this," or "I have nothing to compare it to." A discount addresses none of those.

It teaches the customer what pushing gets them. If mild pushback produced fifteen percent, firmer pushback in the next conversation will be tried. You have trained them, and you will be negotiating against yourself for the rest of the deal.

And it quietly says your price was never real. Whatever confidence you had built in the recommendation goes with it.

Pricing strategy and commercial negotiation are a later course in this pathway. At this stage the rule is simple: you do not have the authority to discount, you do not offer it, and when price comes up you treat it as a value question until you have evidence otherwise.

## Why not argue

The other reflex is to correct the customer. They say your product is missing something; you say it isn't. They say a competitor is cheaper; you explain why the comparison is unfair.

You may be entirely right, and it still loses. Arguing frames the conversation as you against them, and a customer who feels they have to win an argument to protect their position will simply stop having the argument — by ending the call, or by circulating your proposal with a note saying the vendor was defensive.

There is also a practical problem. When you rush to correct, you respond to the objection you *assume* they made rather than the one they actually made. "It's missing approval workflows" might mean anything from a genuine gap to a colleague's secondhand impression from two years ago. Answering before you know which is a coin flip.

## Sort what you just heard

Before responding, decide which of three things you are holding.

A **brush-off** is a polite way of ending a conversation the customer has no interest in. "Send me some information." It usually arrives early, it is generic, and it is not attached to anything specific about their situation. The response is to test whether interest exists at all, gently, and to be willing to hear no.

An **objection** is a real concern about a real thing, raised by someone still engaged. It is specific. "We tried something like this in 2022 and adoption was terrible." This is the category this lesson is about, and it is by far the most common.

A **blocker** is a fact that ends the deal for now regardless of your skill. No budget until the next fiscal year; a signed three-year contract with a competitor; a policy against cloud storage of that data type. The correct response is to stop selling, confirm the fact, and agree when it would make sense to talk again. Trying to talk a customer out of a blocker is how you become the vendor nobody wants to call back.

Most of the damage new representatives do comes from treating blockers as objections and objections as brush-offs.

## The five common objections

| Type | Sounds like | What it usually means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Value / price | "That's more than we expected." | The impact of the problem hasn't been made concrete |
| Timing | "Let's revisit next quarter." | Something else is more urgent, or nobody feels a deadline |
| Authority | "I'd have to run it past my director." | You haven't met the person who decides |
| Status quo | "What we have works fine." | The cost of the current way is invisible or borne by someone else |
| Trust / risk | "How do we know this will actually work?" | They need evidence, not enthusiasm |

Notice that four of the five point backwards to earlier work — to discovery, to impact, to stakeholders. A large share of objections are simply your earlier shortcuts arriving late.

## A response pattern that works

Five steps. Practice them until they are automatic, then stop thinking about them.

**1. Pause.** One beat. It stops the defensive reflex and signals that you took the concern seriously.

**2. Acknowledge, without agreeing or disagreeing.** "That's fair." "I hear that a lot, and it's a reasonable thing to ask." You are validating that raising it was legitimate — not conceding the point.

**3. Ask one clarifying question.** This is the step people skip and the one that does the work. "When you say too expensive — is it the total number, or is it that there's no budget line for it this year?" You cannot answer well until you know which objection you actually have.

**4. Respond with evidence, briefly.** Point at something concrete: their own numbers from discovery, a comparable customer, a specific product behavior, a pilot you can run. Short. Two or three sentences. Long answers read as anxiety.

**5. Confirm and move.** "Does that address it?" Wait for the answer, then return to what happens next. An objection you did not confirm as resolved is still open, and it will come back later with more force.

## Worked examples

**Status quo.**

> **Customer:** Honestly, the spreadsheet works fine for us.
>
> **Rep:** *(pause)* That's fair — and if it's genuinely working, changing it would be a waste of your time. Can I ask, when you said earlier that month-end runs three days over, is that connected to the spreadsheet or separate from it? *(clarify, using their own words)*
>
> **Customer:** ...it's the reconciliation, yes. But that's just how month-end is.
>
> **Rep:** That's the part I'd want to test. The two companies I've worked with in your sector took month-end from three days over to same-day, and the mechanism was exactly this one. I'm not asking you to believe that — I'd rather run one month of your real data through it and see. Worth a look? *(evidence, then confirm)*

No argument, no discount, and the customer's own number is doing the persuading.

One caution about the evidence step. The rep above can cite "two companies I've worked with in your sector" only because that is true. If you are new and have no comparable customers of your own, do not borrow someone else's story as if it were yours — use a published case study your company has approved, the customer's own numbers, or an offer to test on their data. A reference the customer later finds you exaggerated costs far more than the objection did.

**Price.**

> **Customer:** That's a lot more than we budgeted.
>
> **Rep:** *(pause)* Understood. Is it that the number is higher than you expected, or that there isn't a line for it this year? *(clarify — these need completely different responses)*
>
> **Customer:** Both, if I'm honest. Nothing allocated.
>
> **Rep:** Then the number is the second problem, not the first. If we're right that this is worth about six hours a week of your bookkeeper's time, who would need to see that case for a line to exist? *(routes to the real issue — an authority and evidence problem)*

**Blocker, handled correctly.**

> **Customer:** We signed a three-year deal with someone else in January.
>
> **Rep:** Then this isn't the right time and I won't push it. Two things, if you'll allow: what made you choose them, and when in the cycle would you normally start looking again? I'll come back then.

That is not a failure. That is a well-kept relationship and a calendar entry.

## Practice

Work in pairs, five minutes per round, swapping roles each time. The customer plays a facilities manager at a mid-size manufacturer who has been shown a maintenance-scheduling product.

1. Run the five objections in the table above, one per round. The customer delivers the line and then answers the representative's clarifying question honestly but briefly. Hard constraint: the representative may not mention price flexibility, and may not use the words "actually" or "but."
2. After each round, the customer states out loud which of the three categories they *intended* — brush-off, objection, or blocker. Track how often the representative read it correctly.
3. Take the round that went worst and write out the four-sentence response you wish you had given: acknowledgement, clarifying question, evidence, confirmation.
4. Now do one round where the customer plays a genuine blocker of their own invention. The representative's job is to stop selling within thirty seconds and leave with a specific date to return.
5. Write down the two objections you personally find hardest to hear and one sentence on why. That is your drill list for next week.

## Check your understanding

1. Brush-off, objection, or blocker? (a) "Just send me a brochure." early in a first call. (b) "Our data policy forbids storing client records outside our own servers." (c) "We tried a tool like this two years ago and nobody used it." *(Answers: (a) brush-off; (b) blocker, if the policy is real and current — confirm it; (c) objection.)*
2. Which step in the five-step pattern do people most often skip, and why does it matter? *(Answer: the clarifying question — without it you answer the objection you assumed rather than the one the customer actually has.)*
3. Give two reasons a discount is the wrong first response to "too expensive." *(Any two: it answers a question that was probably not asked; it teaches the customer that pushing works; it signals your price was never real.)*
