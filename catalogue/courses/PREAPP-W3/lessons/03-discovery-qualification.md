## Discovery is not a pitch with questions in it

The most common failure in a first sales conversation is that the seller spends it talking. They open with a summary of what their company does, ask "does that sound like something you'd be interested in," and treat the answer as data. It is not data. It is politeness.

Discovery is the conversation where you find out whether there is anything real here — for both of you. Done well, it ends with one of two honest outcomes: a clear next step, or a clean disqualification. A disqualification is a *good* outcome. It costs a rep far less to learn in twelve minutes that a prospect has no budget and no timeline than to learn it after six weeks of follow-up.

You are going to run one of these live this week, recorded, with an industry practitioner playing the buyer. This lab is how you prepare for it.

## The framework: need, authority, timing

You will hear branded acronyms for this — most sales organizations have adopted one. Underneath the branding, nearly all of them are asking the same three questions. Learn the three; the branding is easy to pick up later at whatever company hires you.

**Need.** Is there a problem worth solving, and does the person you are talking to feel it? Not "could this be useful" — is something currently costing them time, money, risk, or headcount. A need you cannot describe in the prospect's own words is not a need you have found.

**Authority.** Who decides? Almost never one person. The realistic version of this question is: who else has to agree, who signs, and who can kill it. A prospect enthusiastically agreeing with you while having no ability to act is the single most common way a pipeline fills with deals that never close.

**Timing.** Is there a reason to act now rather than next year? A budget cycle, a contract renewal, a regulatory date, a system being retired, a new hire starting. Without one, even a real need with real authority sits still.

Everything else — budget, competition, current tooling, success criteria — hangs off these three.

## Question shape matters more than question count

Open questions start with what, how, why, when, or "walk me through." They cannot be answered with yes or no, and they hand the floor back to the prospect. Closed questions confirm a specific fact and should be used sparingly, to nail something down after it has been surfaced.

Compare these, aimed at the same information:

| Weak (closed, leading, or pitching) | Strong (open, neutral) |
| --- | --- |
| "Are you having problems with your current process?" | "Walk me through how your team handles that today." |
| "Would faster onboarding be valuable?" | "What happens when onboarding takes longer than you planned?" |
| "Are you the decision maker?" | "Who else would be involved if you decided to move forward?" |
| "Do you have budget for this?" | "How does your team usually fund something like this?" |
| "Is this a priority this quarter?" | "What has to happen before the end of the quarter?" |

The right column takes no more time to ask and returns several times as much information. Notice also that none of the strong questions mention a product.

Two techniques carry disproportionate weight:

**The follow-up.** The first answer is a headline; the second is the story. When a prospect says "onboarding is slow," the useful move is "slow how — where does it stall?" then "what does that cost you?" Three layers deep is where a real need lives. New reps ask one question, get a surface answer, and move to the next item on their list.

**The confirm-back.** Every few minutes, summarize what you heard and let them correct you: "So if I have this right — new hires wait about two weeks for system access, that's roughly a week of lost productivity each, and it got worse after the reorg. Is that fair?" This does three things at once: it proves you were listening, it corrects your misunderstandings early, and it produces a sentence you can quote later in your notes.

## Running the call

A twenty-minute first call has a shape. Keep it in your head, not on the screen in front of you.

1. **Open (1 min).** Confirm the time you have, and state the purpose in one line: "I've got twenty minutes on the calendar — my goal today is to understand how your team handles X and figure out whether there's a reason for us to keep talking. Fair?"
2. **Context (3-4 min).** Their role, their team, how the relevant work happens today.
3. **Need (6-8 min).** Where it breaks. Follow up twice on every answer that matters. Quantify if they will let you.
4. **Authority and timing (3-4 min).** Who else, what process, why now.
5. **Confirm-back (1-2 min).** Summarize. Let them correct you.
6. **Next step (2 min).** Propose something specific with a date, or agree explicitly that there is not a fit. Never end on "I'll send some information."

The single hardest habit to build is silence. After you ask a real question, stop talking. Count to five in your head if you have to. New reps fill the pause with a second question or an explanation, and the prospect answers the easier one.

## Scoring your pipeline against criteria

Discovery produces information. Qualification is the decision you make with it. You need explicit criteria written down *before* you look at your pipeline, or you will rationalize every contact you like into staying.

Use a simple, defensible scheme. Score every contact in your pipeline 0-2 on each of the three:

- **Need** — 0: none identified. 1: a plausible need you inferred. 2: a need they described in their own words.
- **Authority** — 0: unknown. 1: they can influence or refer. 2: they can decide, or you know exactly who does.
- **Timing** — 0: no event or date. 1: a vague window ("sometime this year"). 2: a specific event or date.

Total 0-6, then act on the total:

- **5-6 — Advance.** Book the next step now, with a date.
- **3-4 — Nurture.** One specific follow-up with a reason to reconnect, scheduled, then out of your active list.
- **0-2 — Disqualify.** Mark it closed in your CRM with a reason. Stop spending time.

Write the reason down every time. A pipeline where nothing is ever disqualified is not a pipeline; it is a list of people you feel bad about. Your CRM — whichever one your cohort uses — should carry three fields for this: score, decision, and reason. Text fields are fine. The discipline is what matters, not the tool.

## Practice

These four parts produce the week's milestone deliverable: a recorded mock discovery call.

1. **Build your question bank.** Write twelve open questions for a real target account from your Week 2 pipeline: four for need, four for authority, four for timing. Every question must start with what, how, why, when, or "walk me through," and none may name a product or service. Have a peer read them and cross out any that can be answered yes or no. Rewrite those.

2. **Rehearse in pairs.** Run a ten-minute practice round with a classmate playing your target. The observer tracks two numbers only: your talk-time percentage, and how many times you asked a follow-up to an answer instead of moving on. Target under 40% talk time and at least four follow-ups. Swap roles and repeat.

3. **Run the recorded mock call.** Twenty minutes with the industry practitioner as the buyer, recorded with their permission. Follow the six-part call shape. End with either a specific next step and a date, or an explicit statement that there is no fit and why. Within an hour, watch your own recording and write three things: the best follow-up question you asked, the moment you started pitching instead of asking, and the piece of need, authority, or timing you failed to surface.

4. **Score the pipeline.** Take every contact in your Week 2 pipeline and score each 0-2 on need, authority, and timing. Record the score, the decision (advance / nurture / disqualify), and a one-line reason in your CRM. Actually disqualify the 0-2s — change their status. Bring the count of each decision to Lesson 04; you will need it for your funnel analysis.
