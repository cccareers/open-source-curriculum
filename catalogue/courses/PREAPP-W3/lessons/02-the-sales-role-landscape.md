## The work you have been doing has a job title

For the last two weeks you researched target companies, wrote cold outreach, followed up, and booked conversations with people who did not know you. On a technical sales team, that is not a side task someone squeezes in — it is a full-time role with a title, a manager, a comp plan (the written rules for how the person is paid, usually a base salary plus commission for hitting targets), and a number attached to it.

This lab is where you put names to the roles and, more importantly, to the numbers. Every behavior you will see on a sales floor traces back to what that person is measured on. If you understand the metric, you can predict the behavior. That is true when you are working alongside a sales team, when you are interviewing for one of these jobs, and when you are on the receiving end of a cold call yourself.

Today you will build a role map from primary sources — real job postings — and then reason about what each role's metric makes people do.

## Three roles, one deal

Most technology sales teams split a single deal across specialists rather than asking one person to do everything. The split is not universal, and small companies collapse it, but the pattern is common enough to be worth learning as a default.

**SDR / BDR — Sales Development Representative / Business Development Representative.** This is the top of the funnel. The SDR's job is to find and open conversations with people who have never spoken to the company: building target lists, researching accounts, sending sequences of email, LinkedIn messages, and calls, and running a short qualifying conversation. The output of the role is a *qualified meeting handed to someone else*. The SDR does not close. The distinction between SDR and BDR varies by company; the most common convention is that SDRs work inbound leads (people who raised a hand) while BDRs work outbound (cold), but plenty of companies use the two titles interchangeably. Do not assume — read the posting.

This is the role your Week 2 work most closely resembles, and it is the most common entry point into technology sales for someone without a degree in the field.

**AE — Account Executive.** The AE owns the deal from the qualified meeting to a signed agreement. That means running the full discovery conversation, building a business case, coordinating everyone on the buyer's side who has to say yes, negotiating terms, and closing. An AE typically carries a *quota*: a dollar amount of closed business they are responsible for in a quarter or a year. AEs are usually promoted from the SDR seat after four to eighteen months, which is why the SDR job is worth taking seriously as a path rather than a destination.

**Sales Engineer / Solutions Engineer / Solutions Consultant.** The technical specialist on the deal. When the buyer asks "will this actually work with the systems we already run," the SE answers it — running demos, scoping integrations, responding to security questionnaires, and building proof-of-concept environments. The SE is a credibility role: they are trusted precisely because they are not the one who gets paid to close. SEs typically pair with one or more AEs and share in the deal outcome without carrying an individual closing quota.

There are adjacent roles you will see in postings and should be able to place: **Account Manager** or **Customer Success Manager** (owns the customer *after* the sale — renewal and expansion), and **Sales Manager** (manages a team of reps and is measured on the team's aggregate number).

## The metrics, and who owns which one

The fastest way to understand a sales organization is to ask each person what number their manager asks them about on Monday morning.

| Metric | What it counts | Who owns it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Activity (calls, emails, touches) | Raw outreach volume | SDR/BDR |
| Meetings booked | Conversations scheduled with a prospect | SDR/BDR |
| Meetings held / accepted | Meetings that actually happened and the AE accepted as legitimate | SDR/BDR |
| Pipeline created | Dollar value of new qualified opportunities | SDR/BDR, credited to AE |
| Conversion rate by stage | Percentage moving from one stage to the next | Everyone; managers most |
| Quota attainment | Percent of closed-won dollars against target | AE |
| Win rate | Deals won ÷ deals worked | AE |
| Sales cycle length | Days from opportunity created to closed | AE, Sales Manager |
| Technical win rate | Evaluations passed | Sales Engineer |
| Net revenue retention | Renewal + expansion of existing customers | AM / CSM |

Two things about this table matter more than memorizing it.

First, **metrics cascade**. Pipeline created feeds quota attainment. Meetings held feed pipeline created. Activity feeds meetings held. When an AE misses quota, the diagnosis usually walks backward up that chain rather than blaming the last conversation.

Second, **metrics shape behavior, including badly**. A rep measured purely on meetings booked has an incentive to book meetings that will never convert — a low-quality meeting still counts. This is exactly why most companies moved to measuring *meetings held and accepted*, or pipeline created, instead: those metrics only pay out if the meeting was real. A rep measured only on activity volume will send a hundred low-effort emails rather than twenty researched ones. When you read a comp plan or a job posting, ask what behavior the number rewards, and what behavior it quietly punishes.

This is also a useful lens on your own Week 2 pipeline. If you had been paid on meetings booked, would your outreach have looked different? Would it have been better?

## Reading a job posting like a practitioner

Job postings are the most reliable public description of these roles, and they are free. When you read one, extract five things and ignore the rest:

1. **The real title and level** — "SDR I," "Enterprise AE," "Associate Solutions Consultant."
2. **The stated metric** — look for phrases like "responsible for booking X qualified meetings per month," "carry an annual quota of," "generate $X in pipeline."
3. **The comp structure** — base salary plus variable/commission, usually expressed as "OTE" (on-target earnings: what you make if you hit 100% of your number). A posting with a wide gap between base and OTE is a heavily commissioned role.
4. **Who they hand off to and who hands off to them** — this tells you where in the funnel the role sits.
5. **The actual stated requirements** — note how often "prior SDR experience" is listed versus a degree requirement.

Ignore the culture paragraph. It tells you nothing you can act on.

## What good looks like in the room

Your instructor block this week includes an industry practitioner. The highest-value question you can ask is not "do you like your job." It is a question only a person doing the work can answer:

- What number does your manager ask you about first?
- What percentage of your outreach gets a reply, honestly?
- What does a meeting have to contain before an AE will accept it?
- What did you not know when you started that cost you your first quarter?

Write your questions before the session. Practitioners answer specific questions well and vague ones badly.

## Practice

Work through all four parts. Parts 3 and 4 feed the week's milestone.

1. **Build the role map.** Find three real, currently posted jobs — one SDR or BDR, one AE, one Sales Engineer or Solutions Consultant — at companies in a technology sector that interests you. For each, record in your notes: company, exact title, the stated metric or quota (quote it), the comp structure including OTE if listed, the stated experience requirement, and one sentence on who the role hands work to. Save the posting links.

2. **Diagram the handoff.** Using your three postings, write a short paragraph tracing one hypothetical deal from first cold touch to signed contract, naming which of your three roles owns each step and what has to be true for the work to pass to the next person.

3. **Predict the behavior.** For each of your three postings, write two sentences: one describing a behavior the stated metric rewards, and one describing a way a rep could hit that metric while producing no real value for the company. Bring these to the practitioner session and ask whether they have seen it happen.

4. **Map yourself onto the funnel.** Open your pipeline — the one you built in Week 1 and worked through Week 2. Count your totals: contacts researched, contacts messaged, replies received, conversations held. Label which of this week's three roles each of those activities belongs to, and write one honest paragraph on which of the three roles your Week 2 work most resembles and which one you would actually want. Keep this file — you will compute conversion rates from these same counts in Lesson 04.
