## The Landing Page Is Part of the Ad

Every hour you have spent on keywords, match types, bids and copy has been spent on one outcome: getting a person to click. The moment they do, you have paid. The auction is over, the money is gone, and whether that click becomes a lead is now entirely a question of what happens on the page.

That is why the landing page belongs in a paid advertising course rather than being handed off to a web team. It is the last third of the ad, and it is the third with the most leverage per hour of work.

Run the numbers on Northgate's biggest non-brand ad group. AC Repair spends $3,200 a month and buys 390 clicks at an average cost per click of $8.21. Hold both of those constant. Only the page changes.

```txt
Same media buy, three different landing pages
  Spend $3,200.00, Clicks 390, Avg CPC $8.21

  Page converting at 8%
    Leads         390 x 0.08 = 31.2  ->  31 leads
    CPA           $3,200 / 31 = $103.23
    Gross profit  31 x $108 = $3,348.00
    Contribution  $3,348.00 - $3,200.00 = $148.00

  Page converting at 11%
    Leads         390 x 0.11 = 42.9  ->  43 leads
    CPA           $3,200 / 43 = $74.42
    Gross profit  43 x $108 = $4,644.00
    Contribution  $4,644.00 - $3,200.00 = $1,444.00

  Page converting at 14%
    Leads         390 x 0.14 = 54.6  ->  55 leads
    CPA           $3,200 / 55 = $58.18
    Gross profit  55 x $108 = $5,940.00
    Contribution  $5,940.00 - $3,200.00 = $2,740.00
```

Look at the 8 percent row. A cost per acquisition of $103.23 sits below the $108 break-even, so the account is technically not losing money, and it is nowhere near the $60 target. The whole month produces $148 of contribution, which is less than one hour of a technician's time. The same media budget at 14 percent produces $2,740. Nothing about the auction changed. Northgate did not outbid anyone, did not find better keywords, did not write better ads. The page got better.

Now consider what it would take to reach a $74.42 cost per acquisition by bidding instead. Cost per acquisition is cost per click divided by conversion rate. At an 8 percent conversion rate you would need an average cost per click of 0.08 x $74.42 = $5.95, down from $8.21. That is a 27.5 percent reduction in click cost, in an auction where your competitors set the price and where cutting bids that hard costs you impression share and therefore volume. You would be buying fewer, cheaper clicks and hoping the mix does not degrade.

The page route is cheaper, more reliable, and compounds. A headline rewrite is a day of work, applies to one hundred percent of the traffic that page will ever receive, does not decay when a competitor raises their bids, and multiplies with any bidding improvement you make later. This is the money argument for page work and you should be able to make it out loud to a client in thirty seconds.

One scope note. This lesson is about landing pages that receive paid traffic, which is a narrow and specific problem: one page, one audience, one intent, arriving already primed by an ad. Broad conversion rate optimization and user experience research methodology, which cover whole-site journeys, qualitative research programs, and personalization, live in the dedicated CRO course later in the pathway. Stay on this side of the line.

## Message Match Is the First and Biggest Lever

Someone types "ac not blowing cold columbus" at 2pm on the first hot Tuesday in June. They see this ad:

```txt
Headline 1:  Same-Day AC Repair in Columbus
Headline 2:  Tech at Your Door in 90 Minutes
Headline 3:  Licensed, Insured, 24/7
Description: Emergency AC repair across the Columbus metro. Real technicians,
             upfront pricing, no overtime charges. Call now or book online.
```

They click. Here is the page they land on today:

```txt
[full-width rotating carousel: three stock photos of houses]

  Welcome to Northgate Heating & Air

  Founded in 1987, Northgate Heating & Air has proudly served central
  Ohio families for three generations. Our commitment to craftsmanship
  and community sets us apart...

  [ Learn About Us ]   [ Our Services ]   [ Careers ]   [ Blog ]
```

The ad made a specific promise about a specific emergency and the page answered with a company history. The visitor's question was "can you get here today?" and the page's first sentence is about 1987. They are back on the results page in four seconds and Northgate paid $8.21 for it.

Here is the same page doing its job:

```txt
  Same-Day AC Repair in Columbus
  A licensed technician at your door in 90 minutes, or we tell you
  before you book.

  [ CALL (614) 555-0142 ]     [ BOOK A VISIT ]

  Open now - 7:00am to 9:00pm, seven days.
  Ohio HVAC Contractor License #HV-0041882
```

The headline echoes the query and repeats the ad's promise almost word for word. The subhead makes the promise falsifiable, which is more persuasive than making it bigger. The two actions are the two things an emergency customer wants to do, and they are the first thing on the page.

The rule is short: **the page headline should echo the query and confirm the ad's promise.** Someone should be able to read the search term, the ad headline, and the page headline in sequence and feel that all three are the same conversation. When you cannot do that because one page is serving eleven different ad groups, that is a structural problem, and the fix is the next section.

## Dedicated Pages, Not the Homepage

The homepage is built to serve everyone: existing customers looking for a phone number, job applicants, people comparing services, someone checking whether you are still in business. It is a directory. Paid traffic does not want a directory, it wants the one thing the ad promised, and every additional option on the page is a chance to leave without doing anything.

Send paid traffic to a dedicated page per service at minimum. Northgate needs at least an AC repair page, a furnace repair page, a system replacement page, and a maintenance plan page.

Beyond that, go one page per ad group where the volume justifies the maintenance. The test is whether the ad group produces enough traffic that you could ever tell a difference, and whether the intent is genuinely distinct from its neighbors.

| Ad group | Monthly clicks | Dedicated page? | Reasoning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Non-brand - AC Repair | 390 | Yes | Highest volume, distinct emergency intent |
| Non-brand - Furnace Install | 178 | Yes | Different funnel entirely, $432 lead value |
| Non-brand - Maintenance Plans | 142 | Yes | Subscription offer, nothing else on the page applies |
| Brand - Northgate | 316 | Yes | Already knows the brand, wants the phone number fast |
| "AC not cooling" symptom terms | 34 | No | Too little traffic to maintain; send to AC Repair page |

Thirty-four clicks a month cannot support its own page. You will never accumulate enough data to know whether the page is working, and you will pay for the upkeep forever. Route it to the closest good page.

## The Anatomy of a Page That Converts Paid Traffic

Work down the page and give a reason for every section. If you cannot state what a section is doing for the conversion, it is a candidate for deletion.

**Headline.** Echoes the query and the ad promise. One line, plain language, no cleverness. Cleverness costs a beat of comprehension and you do not have a beat.

**Subhead.** Adds the specific, checkable detail the headline could not carry. "A licensed technician at your door in 90 minutes" is better than "fast, friendly service" because the first one can be wrong and the second one cannot.

**The offer.** State exactly what the visitor gets if they act, and what it costs them. "Same-day diagnostic visit, $79, applied to the repair if you book it" is an offer — and it uses the same $79 the ads promise, because the page has to keep the ad's number. "Contact us for a free quote" is a form. The offer is the thing you are actually selling in the next sixty seconds, and it is not the repair, it is the visit.

**Primary action above the fold, and repeated.** On an emergency page the two primary actions are call and book. They belong in the first screen and again after the proof section and again at the bottom. A visitor who has just been convinced should not have to scroll to act on it.

**The phone number as a tap target on mobile.** Not text, not an image, a real `tel:` link with a touch area large enough to hit while standing in a hot house holding a toddler. On the mobile viewport, a sticky bottom call bar is one of the highest-return single changes available on a page like this.

**Proof.** This is the section where most contractor pages are thin and it is the one that carries the objection. Concrete proof beats adjectives every time:

- Three recent reviews with a first name, a neighborhood, and a date, not a rotating carousel of five-star quotes with no attribution.
- The state license number, written out. It signals you can be checked.
- Years in business, stated once, as a number.
- Photographs of the actual technicians in actual branded shirts next to actual vans. Stock photography of a smiling model in a hardhat reads as stock photography, and it costs you credibility rather than adding it.

**Trust signals adjacent to the form.** The moment of hesitation is the moment before the submit button. Put the license number, the "no overtime charges" line, and a short privacy assurance ("we call you back, we do not sell your details") immediately beside or beneath the form, not in the footer where nobody reads them.

**What to cut.** The site navigation menu, or at least reduce it to a logo and a phone number. The hero carousel. The company history. The blog links. The social media feed. The cross-sell to unrelated services. The PDF brochure. Every one of those is an exit, and the visitor arrived through a paid click with one intent. A dedicated paid landing page is allowed to be a dead end in every direction except the conversion.

## Friction Analysis

Friction is anything between intent and completion. On a lead-generation page it is overwhelmingly the form.

Every field you add costs completions. Here is what Northgate's own form data looks like across its service pages over two quarters, measured as the share of people who started the form and finished it.

| Fields | Fields asked | Form start to submit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2 | Name, phone | 78% |
| 3 | Name, phone, ZIP | 74% |
| 4 | + email | 66% |
| 5 | + service needed | 61% |
| 7 | + street address, preferred time window | 47% |
| 9 | + system age, system brand, how did you hear about us | 33% |

Now apply it. The AC Repair page currently runs a seven-field form. Assume 36 people start it in a month, which is 9.2 percent of the 390 clicks, and that the rest of the leads arrive as phone calls.

```txt
Current 7-field form
  Form starts        36
  Completion         47%
  Form leads         36 x 0.47 = 16.9  ->  17
  Phone leads                            26
  Total leads                            43
  CPA                $3,200 / 43 = $74.42

Cut to a 3-field form (name, phone, ZIP)
  Form starts        36   (held constant - conservative)
  Completion         74%
  Form leads         36 x 0.74 = 26.6  ->  27
  Phone leads                            26
  Total leads                            53
  CPA                $3,200 / 53 = $60.38
  Gross profit       53 x $108 = $5,724.00
  Contribution       $5,724.00 - $3,200.00 = $2,524.00
```

Removing four fields moves cost per acquisition from $74.42 to $60.38, which lands on Northgate's $60 target, and it adds $1,080 of monthly contribution. That estimate is deliberately conservative because it holds form starts constant, when in practice a shorter form also raises the number of people willing to begin.

Three rules follow.

**Only ask for what you need to make the next call.** Northgate needs a name, a phone number, and a ZIP code to confirm the visit is inside the 25-mile service radius. Everything else is a question a dispatcher can ask in the same phone call that they were going to make anyway. System age and brand are useful, but they are useful to the technician standing in the basement, not to the form.

**Do not ask for what you can look up.** The street address can be captured on the confirmation call. The "how did you hear about us" field is a worse attribution source than the tracking you built in the previous lesson, and it costs you completions to collect data you already have, more accurately, for free.

**Required versus optional is a real choice.** An optional field still costs attention and still lengthens the form visually, but it costs far fewer abandonments than a required one. If a field is genuinely nice-to-have, make it optional or remove it, and be suspicious of any stakeholder who insists that every field is mandatory.

Two more friction levers worth knowing. **Phone versus form preference** shifts hard by urgency: for an emergency repair, most people would rather call, and the page should make calling at least as prominent as the form. For a $8,500 replacement quote, many people would rather not talk to a salesperson yet, and the form should lead. Do not apply one layout to both. **Multi-step forms** help when the total information required is genuinely large and the first step is trivially easy, because starting is the hardest commitment and a two-field first step gets a lot of people started. They hurt when they are used to disguise a nine-field form as a three-field one; people notice by step two and abandon with more annoyance than they would have felt at the start.

## Speed and Mobile Are Conversion Features

Treat page speed as a conversion lever with a dollar figure attached, not as an engineering hygiene task somebody will get to.

Northgate's AC Repair page currently takes 5.8 seconds to render its main content on a mid-range phone over mobile data, mostly because of the hero carousel loading three full-width uncompressed images. Assume, conservatively, that 12 percent of paid clicks abandon before the page becomes usable.

```txt
Cost of a slow page, AC Repair, one month
  Clicks paid for              390
  Abandoned before render      390 x 0.12 = 46.8  ->  47 clicks
  Money spent on those clicks  47 x $8.21 = $385.87
  Leads those clicks would
    have produced at 11%       47 x 0.11 = 5.2  ->  5 leads
  Gross profit forgone         5 x $108 = $540.00
```

That is $385.87 of media purchased and thrown away, plus $540 of gross profit that never happened, every month, from image compression that would take an afternoon. Say it in those terms when you are asking for developer time.

Mobile is not a segment on this account, it is the account. Emergency search happens on a phone, standing in the room with the broken equipment. Look at what the segmented data says.

| Device | Clicks | Conversions | Conversion rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mobile | 304 | 28 | 9.2% |
| Desktop | 74 | 13 | 17.6% |
| Tablet | 12 | 2 | 16.7% |
| Total | 390 | 43 | 11.0% |

Seventy-eight percent of the traffic is converting at roughly half the desktop rate. If mobile merely matched desktop, the page would produce 304 x 0.176 = 53.5, call it 53 mobile leads plus 15 from the other devices, for 68 total and a cost per acquisition of $3,200 / 68 = $47.06. That gap is the single largest number on this page, and you would never have seen it in the blended 11 percent.

## Finding the Problem Before You Propose a Fix

The failure mode of everyone new to this work is proposing changes before diagnosing anything. You will be asked "what would you change on this page," and the professional answer starts with "here is what the data says is wrong."

Five instruments, each with a real weakness.

**Segmented conversion rate reporting.** Break the page's conversion rate by device, campaign, ad group, geography and hour of day. Good at telling you *where* the problem is, cheaply and quantitatively, as the mobile split above just demonstrated. Bad at telling you *why*, and easily misread when a segment has too few clicks to mean anything.

**Scroll depth and click behavior.** Aggregate maps showing how far people get and what they tap. Good at revealing that the form nobody fills in sits below the point where 70 percent of visitors stop scrolling, or that people are repeatedly tapping a non-clickable image expecting it to be the phone number. Bad at causation; a hot spot tells you what was touched, not what was wanted, and these maps mislead badly on responsive pages where the layout differs by viewport.

**Form field analytics.** Per-field drop-off, time spent in each field, and error rates. Good at pinpointing exactly which field kills the form, which is otherwise almost impossible to know. On Northgate's seven-field form, "preferred time window" is where a third of abandonments occur. Bad at anything outside the form, and useless if the problem is that people never reach it.

**Session replay, watched with a specific question.** Recordings of individual sessions. Good at showing you the thing you would never have hypothesized: the sticky cookie banner covering the call button on a small viewport, the ZIP validation rejecting valid Ohio codes. Bad if you watch it aimlessly, which is how most people use it; an hour of replay with no question is entertainment. Watch ten mobile sessions that reached the form and did not submit, and nothing else.

**A five-user hallway test.** Put the page in front of five people who are not in the industry and ask them to do a task out loud. Good at catching comprehension failures that no analytics tool can see: nobody knew what "diagnostic visit" meant, three of five could not tell whether Northgate served their suburb. Cheap and fast. Bad at quantifying anything; five people cannot tell you a conversion rate and you must not let anyone present it as if they could.

Use them in that order. Quantitative to find where, qualitative to find why, then propose.

## Writing a Conversion Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a claim specific enough to be wrong. Use this form and fill in every slot:

**Because [evidence], we believe that [change] will cause [measurable effect] for [segment], measured by [metric].**

Here are four for Northgate's AC Repair page, of deliberately varying quality.

**H1.** Because segmented reporting shows mobile converting at 9.2 percent against 17.6 percent on desktop, and eight of ten mobile session replays show visitors scrolling past the phone number without pausing, we believe that adding a sticky tap-to-call bar pinned to the bottom of the mobile viewport will increase leads per click by at least 2 percentage points for mobile AC Repair traffic, measured by conversion rate on the AC Repair landing page, mobile segment only.

**H2.** Because form field analytics show 33 percent of abandonments occur on the "preferred time window" field, and dispatch confirms they call every lead within ten minutes anyway, we believe that removing "preferred time window", "street address", "email" and "how did you hear about us" will increase form completion from 47 percent to approximately 74 percent for all AC Repair traffic, measured by form-start to form-submit rate.

**H3.** We believe a new design will improve conversions.

**H4.** Because the page looks dated, we believe changing the primary button from grey to orange will increase conversions by 30 percent, measured by leads.

H1 and H2 are usable. Each names the evidence and where it came from, names one change, predicts a specific effect with a number, restricts itself to a segment, and specifies the exact metric that will settle it. Each is also falsifiable, which is the whole point: if mobile conversion rate does not move, H1 was wrong, and you learned something.

H3 fails on every slot. There is no evidence, no segment, no metric and no predicted effect. Worse, "a new design" is not one change, it is thirty changes shipped together, so even if conversions improve you will not know which of the thirty did it or whether some of them made things worse and were masked by the rest. Redesigns are the most common way organizations spend six months and learn nothing.

H4 is more interesting because it looks like a hypothesis. It names a specific change and a metric, so it is at least testable. It still fails in three ways. The evidence, "the page looks dated," is an aesthetic opinion, not a measurement, and it does not connect to the change at all. The predicted effect of 30 percent from a button color is not plausible; effects that large come from message and offer, not chrome, and predicting one that big usually means nobody has thought about the mechanism. And "measured by leads" is ambiguous: leads on which page, over what period, including calls or not. Rewrite it with real evidence and a plausible effect, or drop it below more promising work.

Write hypotheses in this form even when nobody asks you to. It is the discipline that stops you from confusing a preference with a plan.

## Prioritizing the Hypotheses

You will always have more ideas than time. Score them rather than argue about them. Rate each candidate 1 to 5 on impact (how much it could move the metric), confidence (how much the evidence supports it), and ease (how cheap it is to build), then multiply.

| Candidate change | Impact | Confidence | Ease | Score |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rewrite headline and subhead to match the ad promise | 5 | 5 | 5 | 125 |
| Add a sticky tap-to-call bar on mobile | 4 | 4 | 5 | 80 |
| Cut the form from 7 fields to 3 | 5 | 4 | 4 | 80 |
| Add three attributed local reviews and the license number by the form | 3 | 4 | 5 | 60 |
| Compress hero images and lazy-load below-fold assets | 4 | 3 | 3 | 36 |
| Replace the hero carousel with a single static image | 2 | 3 | 4 | 24 |

The resulting order is headline, then the two tied at 80, then reviews, then speed, then the carousel. Break the tie on ease: the call bar is a smaller build than a form change that touches the CRM integration, so ship it first and keep the momentum.

Two cautions about scoring frameworks. They are a conversation tool, not an oracle; the numbers are your judgment, made visible so that someone can disagree with a specific cell rather than with the whole plan. And confidence should be driven by the strength of your evidence, not by how much you like the idea. If you find yourself scoring confidence at 5 on something with no data behind it, you are using the framework to launder a preference.

## Safe Wins Versus Cosmetics

Some changes are reliably worth doing. Fixing a broken message match, cutting unnecessary form fields, making the phone number tappable on mobile, putting the primary action above the fold, adding concrete and attributed proof, and making a slow page fast are all changes that move real numbers on real accounts across many industries. If a page has any of those problems, fix them before you get creative.

Some changes are usually cosmetic. Button color, minor copy polish on secondary text, swapping one stock photograph for another, font choices, small spacing adjustments, and adding a badge nobody recognizes. They are not always worthless, but their effects are small, and small effects need very large samples to detect, which means you will spend weeks of test capacity learning almost nothing. Spend your traffic on changes big enough to be worth measuring.

## A Proposal Is a Hypothesis, Not a Fact

Everything you have produced in this lesson is a belief supported by evidence. It is not yet a result.

Shipping an untested change to a page carrying real paid traffic is a decision with a cost, and you should price it. If a redesign of the AC Repair page turned out to reduce conversion rate from 11 percent to 8 percent, and it ran unnoticed for a month, that is 43 leads down to 31: twelve leads at $108 of gross profit each, or $1,296 gone. If it ran for a quarter before anyone segmented the data, it is nearly $3,900. Nothing about the page would look broken. It would just quietly earn less.

Sometimes shipping without a test is still the right call. If the page is obviously broken on mobile, or the headline contradicts the ad, or the form asks for a Social Security number, you fix it and you do not run a test to confirm that broken is worse than not broken. But when the change is a genuine judgment call, and especially when someone senior is confident about it, the correct move is to measure. How to design that measurement properly, how much traffic you need before a difference means anything, how long to run it, and why "variant B is ahead after 40 clicks" is noise rather than news, is the subject of the next lesson. Do not settle these arguments with opinion when you are one lesson away from being able to settle them with evidence.

## The Ethical Line on Urgency

Urgency and scarcity work, which is exactly why they get abused. The rule is simple and it is not negotiable: **a claim on a landing page must be true.**

"Only 2 appointment slots left today" is a fine line when dispatch really has two slots left and the page is reading the schedule. It is a lie when it is hardcoded and says the same thing every day, including at 6am. A countdown timer that resets when you reload the page is a lie. "47 people booked this week" with an invented number is a lie. Review counts that include reviews you wrote are a lie.

Beyond the obvious legal and advertising-policy exposure, this is bad business for a contractor whose customers live in the same 25 miles and talk to each other. And practically: a false urgency claim raises the lead count and lowers the lead quality, which means your conversion tracking now reports an improvement that your booked-job rate contradicts. You will have optimized toward a number that is not connected to money. Truthful urgency, "our last two same-day slots for today close at 3pm," does the same persuasive work and survives a phone call.

## Practice

You are auditing an underperforming Northgate landing page and proposing a change. Do all of this on a copy of the page, a staging build, or a wireframe. Do not modify a live page, do not change any live ad settings, and do not spend any ad budget.

**The page as it exists.** The AC Repair landing page at `northgateheatingair.com/ac-repair` currently has: a full-width three-slide rotating carousel of stock house photos; the headline "Welcome to Northgate Heating & Air"; a paragraph about being founded in 1987; the full site navigation across the top with eight links including Careers and Blog; the phone number rendered as part of a header image; a "Request Service" form placed below three screens of scrolling, asking for name, email, phone, street address, service needed, preferred time window, and how did you hear about us; a footer strip with a five-star graphic and the text "Trusted by thousands"; and a main content render time of 5.8 seconds on mobile data. It receives all 390 monthly clicks from the AC Repair ad group. Segmented data, form drop-off data, and the field-count table are in this lesson.

1. **Run the diagnostic stack.** Work through all five instruments against the page and the supplied data. For each one, write down what you would look at, what you actually found or would expect to find, and one limitation of that instrument on this specific page. Note explicitly where two instruments disagree and how you would resolve it.

2. **Write at least five hypotheses.** Use the required form, with every slot filled: because, we believe, will cause, for which segment, measured by. Each must trace back to a specific piece of evidence from step 1. At least one must address the mobile gap and at least one must address form friction.

3. **Score and rank them.** Build the impact, confidence, ease table, multiply, and produce an ordered list. Write one sentence per row explaining the confidence score in particular, since that is the one you will be challenged on. Where two scores tie, state your tie-break and why.

4. **Write the top hypothesis up as a change spec.** One page containing: the current state, the proposed state described precisely enough that someone could build it without asking you a question, what is explicitly not changing, the expected conversion rate lift as a number with the reasoning behind it, and the full cost-per-acquisition arithmetic that lift would produce at 390 clicks and $3,200 of spend. Show the division. State the contribution in gross profit before and after.

5. **Price the downside.** Calculate what it costs Northgate per month if your change turns out to move conversion rate 2 percentage points in the wrong direction and nobody notices for six weeks. Use the $108 repair lead value.

6. **Write the measurement note.** In three or four sentences, state how you would find out whether your change actually worked, and be honest about what you cannot yet determine. You will build that measurement properly in the next lesson.

7. **Ethics pass.** Review your own proposed page for any claim that would not survive a phone call to the dispatcher: urgency, scarcity, review counts, guarantees, response times. List every claim you are making and, next to each, the source that makes it true. Delete or rewrite anything with no source.
