## Goal

Take one rep's territory data, diagnose it, and produce a changed plan.

The deliverable is a short analysis document that ends in a decision: one change, one measurable target, one review date. Not a summary of the numbers, not a dashboard, not five recommendations hedged against each other. A decision, with the arithmetic that forces it.

This is the artifact the whole course points at. Everything in lessons 2 through 5 — metric selection, cohort conversion, pricing a leak, discriminating checks, tile specifications — is a tool you will use here. The territory below is not the one you worked with in class, and the answer is not the same one.

## What you must deliver

Six parts, in this order, as one document.

**1. The metric card.** Dana's baseline, each line showing the arithmetic. At minimum: win rate by count and by value; mean and median won deal size; mean and median won cycle and mean lost cycle; quota attainment and the annual gap; Q4 coverage as reported and after cleaning; break-even coverage at her win rate; expected value of the clean Q4 pipeline against the Q4 gap; and pipeline velocity in dollars per day, with the sanity check from lesson 2 comparing the opportunity count the velocity model implies against the count she actually created.

**2. The cohort table.** Opportunities reaching each stage, share of the 115, and step conversion, reconciled against her win rate. Show the reconciliation explicitly. Add the deaths table alongside it and verify that the weighted mean days-alive across the four death rows reproduces the 71-day mean lost cycle.

**3. The priced leaks.** For every step where Dana is below the team median, compute the additional annual won ACV she would have produced if that one step had matched the median and everything downstream had stayed at her own rates. Then price the deal-size gap the same way, as a separate line. Rank all of them by dollars. State plainly which is largest and by how much, and note where two candidates are close enough that the ranking is not decisive.

**4. The causes and the checks.** At least four candidate causes for the leak you ranked first. At least one must be a measurement cause — something about how Dana records or stages opportunities rather than how she sells. For each cause, one discriminating check: a report she could run in her CRM, stated precisely enough to build, together with the result that would make you believe the cause and the result that would make you abandon it. Then say which causes the data in this brief already supports, which it already rules out, and which remain open.

**5. The changed plan.** One change. A measurable target expressed as a specific movement in a specific metric, with a starting value and an ending value. A review date and the exact metric you will read on that date. What Dana stops doing to make room for the change. And the expected value of the change, computed the same way as part 3, stated as a range rather than a point if your evidence does not support a point.

**6. The dashboard spec and the manager brief.** A maximum of six tiles, each specified as decision, question, metric, filter, threshold, action, with at least one tile that would detect the change failing. Then one paragraph, no more than 150 words, that Dana could send to her manager: the diagnosis, the change, the target, the date, and the one figure she does not trust.

## Requirements

- Show the arithmetic for every computed figure. A number with no visible calculation behind it does not count as delivered.
- Every figure must trace to a table in this brief or to a calculation on one.
- State sample size next to every rate you use to make an argument.
- Name at least one figure in the brief you do not trust, say why, and say what you would need to see to trust it.
- Rank leaks by dollars, not by percentage points, and show both numbers so the difference is visible.
- Distinguish clearly between what the data shows and what you infer from it. Any sentence that asserts a cause must be marked as an inference.
- Your plan must be executable by a rep. Every action in it must be something Dana can do herself in the next ninety days.

## Constraints

- **No number may enter your analysis that is not in this brief or derived from it.** If you want an industry benchmark, label it as an assumption on the page and mark the line that depends on it as low confidence.
- **Native CRM reporting only.** No SQL, no data warehouse, no business-intelligence tool, no spreadsheet model that could not be reproduced as a standard grouped report or cross-tab. If a check cannot be built from the opportunity record, it is not a check you may propose.
- **Do not propose CRM configuration or automation work.** New fields, validation rules, workflow automation, and stage redesign are somebody else's project and a different course. A change to Dana's own recording habits is in scope; a change to the system is not.
- **Do not redesign the forecasting process.** Forecast calls, commit categories, and roll-up rituals are out of scope. You are producing a rep's analysis, not a management process.
- **No statistical modelling.** No regression, no significance testing, no predictive scoring. Arithmetic, ratios, and explicit sample-size caution are the entire toolkit.
- **One change, not a programme.** If your plan contains three changes, you have not finished the ranking in part 3.
- **The plan must end with a date.** An analysis without a review date cannot be wrong, which is why it is worthless.

## Definition of done

- All six parts are present, in order.
- Every blank cell in the regional reference table is filled with a computed figure and its arithmetic.
- The cohort's cumulative conversion equals the computed win rate, and the check is shown.
- The weighted mean of the four death rows reproduces the 71-day mean lost cycle, and the check is shown.
- Q4 coverage is computed twice — as reported and cleaned — and both are compared to break-even coverage at Dana's win rate and to the Q4 gap in dollars.
- Pipeline velocity is computed and explicitly labelled as a capacity ceiling, with the implied-opportunity-count sanity check beside it.
- Every below-median step and the deal-size gap are priced in annual ACV and ranked.
- At least four candidate causes are stated, at least one of them a measurement cause, each with a discriminating check and its pass and fail results.
- The plan names one change, a starting value, an ending value, a review date, and something Dana stops doing.
- The dashboard has no more than six tiles, every tile has all six specification lines, and at least one tile would detect the change failing.
- The manager paragraph is 150 words or fewer and names the figure you do not trust.
- Another rep could check every number in your document with this brief and a calculator.

## Hints

**Compute the metric card before you form any opinion.** Two of the figures will surprise you and one of them will overturn the first theory you have. Resist writing part 4 in your head while you are still doing part 1.

**The percentage gaps and the dollar gaps rank differently.** Two of Dana's four steps are at or above the team median, so only two can leak at all. One of those two is barely below the median and worth very little; the other is nearly seventeen points below and worth roughly three quarters of her entire annual quota gap on its own. Rank on the dollars, and show the percentage points beside them so a reader can see why the two orderings differ.

**Deal size is not a leak in the funnel, but it competes with one.** Price it the same way you price a conversion step and put it in the same ranking. When you do, you will find two candidates within a few thousand dollars of each other, and part 3 has to say so rather than pretending the ranking is clean. Deciding between two nearly-tied candidates on evidence rather than preference is most of the work in this project.

**Before blaming discounting for the deal-size gap, look at the segment table.** Win rate by segment is roughly flat while the volume is very lopsided. That pattern points at one explanation and away from another. Compute both win rate and share of opportunities for each segment before you decide which.

**Sixty-one proposals produced thirty-eight negotiations.** Set that beside the team median of 42 proposals and the 19 days from first meeting to proposal against a median of 34. Then look at the mean days alive for a proposal-stage death. Three facts, one behaviour.

**Her losses die faster than her wins close.** That is the reverse of the pattern you saw in class, where losses lingered. Work out what that reversal implies about where her time is going, and be careful — it is not automatically good news.

**Watch the coverage arithmetic.** Cleaned coverage against break-even coverage at her win rate is not a near miss. Say what the shortfall is in dollars and say what is and is not available to a rep with a 104-day mean cycle and four weeks left in the quarter.

**The seven negotiation-stage losses are your untrustworthy figure, or one of them.** Anything you conclude about the last step of Dana's funnel rests on seven deals. Say so.

**Write the manager paragraph last and cut it hard.** If the manager reads 150 words and knows what is broken, what Dana is changing, what number will move, and when they will find out, the analysis has done its job.
