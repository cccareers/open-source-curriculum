## The gap between a reply and a conversation

Lessons 02 and 03 produced replies. This lesson is about the thing that happens to most of them: nothing.

The pattern is easy to recognize once you have seen it. Someone writes back, "Sure, happy to chat sometime — just let me know!" You write back, "That's great, thank you so much! Whenever works for you." They are busy. You are polite. Three days pass. The thread is warm, friendly, and dead.

An open-ended thread is not a booked conversation. It is a decision neither of you made. Your milestone this week is 2+ conversations booked and one informational interview completed and debriefed — and the only reliable way to get there is to treat a positive reply as a scheduling task with a deadline, not as a relationship to nurture.

## Converting a positive reply into a calendar event

The move is the same every time: **reply fast, propose concretely, confirm in writing.**

**Fast** means within a few hours, same business day at the outside. Interest decays. The person who said yes on Tuesday morning has forgotten who you are by Friday.

**Concretely** means you do the scheduling work, not them. Never send "whenever works for you" — that hands a busy person an open-ended task. Send two or three specific windows and a calendar link — a **booking link** is a page from a scheduling tool (most calendar apps and several free services offer one) that shows your open slots and lets the other person pick one, which then lands on both calendars:

```text
Thanks Dana — that's generous of you.

Would either of these work for 20 minutes?
  Thursday 4/11, 2:00-2:20pm ET
  Friday 4/12, 10:00-10:20am ET

If neither fits, here's my calendar and anything on it is open:
<your booking link>

Either way I'll send an invite once you pick, so it's on your calendar.
```

Three things are doing work there. **Twenty minutes** is named explicitly, which makes yes cheap. **Two concrete windows** convert a decision into a pick. **The calendar link is the fallback, not the lead** — leading with a bare link can read as making them do the work, but offered after two options it is a courtesy.

**Confirm in writing** means you send the calendar invite yourself, immediately, with the video link or phone number in it, your name and the program in the title, and a one-line agenda in the body. An unconfirmed verbal yes is not booked. In your CRM, the contact does not move to a "meeting booked" stage until an invite exists and has been accepted.

Two more patterns worth having ready:

**The soft yes.** "Things are crazy right now, maybe in a few weeks." That is a real yes with a date attached to it. Reply, thank them, and propose a specific week: "Totally understood — would the week of the 29th be better? I'll reach back out that Monday." Then log the task in your CRM for that Monday and actually do it.

**The no-reply after a yes.** They said sure, then went quiet. One follow-up, four to five business days later, short, no guilt, and it re-proposes times rather than asking whether they are still interested: "Circling back with two more windows in case last week was busy — Tuesday 11am or Wednesday 3pm?" One. Then let it rest and work the other contacts in that account.

## What an informational interview actually is

An informational interview is a short, structured conversation in which you learn about someone's work, team, and path. It is **not** a job interview and it is **not** a disguised job ask. Say so explicitly when you book it, and then honor it.

That constraint is not politeness, it is what makes the conversation work. People are guarded when they suspect they are being asked for something they cannot give. They are generous when asked about their own experience by someone who did their homework. The professional outcomes — being remembered, being referred, being thought of when a role opens — come from being genuinely useful to talk to, not from converting the call.

Twenty minutes is the standard for this program. It is short enough to be an easy yes and long enough to be worth both people's time. Respect it exactly.

## The five phases of a 20-minute interview

Structure is what separates a conversation that produces intelligence from a pleasant chat you cannot remember on Friday. Run these five phases in this order.

**1. Rapport and frame — 2 minutes.** Thank them for the time. Restate the frame in one sentence: who you are, that you are training with CCC, and that you are here to learn about their work, not to ask about a role. Confirm the time box out loud: "I've got us down for twenty minutes and I'll keep us to it."

**2. Prepared questions — 12 minutes.** Six to eight questions, written in advance, ordered from broad to specific. You will not get through all of them and that is fine — the list is insurance against dead air, not a script to complete. Good questions for this stage:

- How did you get into this work, and what did the first year actually look like?
- What does a normal week on your team look like right now?
- What are the skills someone joining your team at entry level is genuinely expected to have on day one?
- What do people underestimate about the work?
- How does your team think about bringing in career-changers or apprentices?
- What is changing in the role that would not be obvious from a job posting?

Avoid questions whose answers are on the careers page, anything that is a job ask in costume ("what would make someone a strong candidate for the role you posted?"), and yes/no questions. Follow the interesting answer over your list — a real follow-up question is worth three prepared ones.

**3. Insight capture — throughout.** Take notes live, or record with explicit permission asked at the top. Capture their language verbatim where you can: the words a practitioner uses for the tools, the problems, and the seniority ladder are exactly what you will reuse in later outreach and interviews.

**4. Next-step ask — 2 minutes, at the 18-minute mark.** One ask, small, and named plainly. The strongest and most common is the referral: "Is there anyone else on your team or in your network you think I should be talking to?" Others: permission to follow up when you finish a project, a resource they would point you at, or a question about the hiring process if a recruiter is on the call. One ask, not three.

**5. Close and thank-you — 1 minute, plus 24 hours.** End on time, visibly. Then send a thank-you within 24 hours that includes one specific thing you took from the conversation and, if they gave you a name, confirmation that you are reaching out to that person. That note is what makes you memorable; the conversation alone rarely does.

## The debrief: turning a conversation into intelligence

An interview you do not debrief is entertainment. Debrief within 30 minutes of hanging up, before the details blur, and write it into the CRM against the contact and the account.

Capture, at minimum:

- **Facts about the role and team** — stack, team size, how work arrives, what entry level really means there.
- **Their language** — the actual words they used for tools, titles, and problems.
- **Hiring reality** — timing, process, whether apprentices or career-changers have come through before, who owns the decision.
- **Names given** — every person mentioned, with the context they were mentioned in.
- **Signal on the account** — did this raise or lower the account's priority, and why?
- **Your commitments** — anything you said you would do, with a due date.
- **What you would do differently** — one line on your own performance. Which question landed? Where did you talk too much?

Then act on it the same day: log the follow-up tasks, update the contact's tier and the account's priority in line with what you learned, and send the outreach to any name you were given while the referral is fresh — using the warm-referral variant you drafted in Lesson 03.

Three or four debriefs into the program, read them side by side. The patterns across accounts — what every team says entry level means, which skills keep coming up unprompted — are the most valuable career intelligence you will generate this term, and they only exist if you wrote the debriefs down.

## Practice

This block delivers the Week 2 milestone: 2+ conversations booked, first informational interview completed and debriefed.

1. **Clear the reply queue.** Go through every reply currently in your inbox or LinkedIn. Sort into positive, soft yes, and no. Nothing stays unanswered at the end of this exercise.
2. **Send concrete times.** For every positive reply, send a scheduling message with two specific 20-minute windows plus your calendar link as fallback. Set up the booking link now if you do not have one.
3. **Book two.** Confirm at least two conversations with a calendar invite you sent, containing a video link and a one-line agenda. Move those contacts to your CRM's booked stage — invite accepted, not verbal yes.
4. **Handle the stalls.** Send one re-proposing follow-up to any yes that went quiet four or more business days ago. Log a dated task for every soft yes.
5. **Build the question set.** Write 6-8 prepared questions for your first interview, ordered broad to specific, tailored to that person's actual role and company. Have a peer strike any question that is a job ask in costume or answerable from the careers page.
6. **Rehearse the phases.** In pairs, run a timed 20-minute mock with a peer playing your contact and an observer timing each phase. Debrief the mock on: did you hold the frame, did you hit the next-step ask by minute 18, did you end on time?
7. **Run the real interview.** Conduct your first live informational interview. Keep to 20 minutes. Make one next-step ask.
8. **Debrief within 30 minutes.** Write the full debrief into the CRM using the categories above. Send the thank-you note within 24 hours with one specific callback to the conversation.
9. **Report out.** Bring to the closing lab: conversations booked, interview completed, names collected, and the one thing you would do differently in the next one.
