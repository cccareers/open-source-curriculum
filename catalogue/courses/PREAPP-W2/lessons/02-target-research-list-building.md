## The problem with a list that is only long

By the end of Week 1 you had fifty or more contacts initiated. That number was the right goal for a first week, because the only way to find out whether you can actually send is to send. But a list built for volume has a predictable shape: a wide spread of companies, one contact per company, and no stated reason why any given name is on it. Work that list for another week and your reply rate will not improve — you will just be tired.

The fix is not to send less. It is to stop spending equal effort on unequal prospects. Every hour of outreach you have is finite, and this lesson is about deciding, before you spend it, which names deserve it. Two moves do most of the work: writing down what a good contact actually looks like so you can qualify against it, and going deeper into a small number of priority organizations instead of one-and-done across a wide one.

## Writing an ideal contact profile

An **ideal contact profile** (ICP) is a short, written description of the person most likely to have a useful conversation with you. Written is the operative word. Held in your head, it drifts to fit whoever you happened to find; on paper, it makes you either accept or reject a prospect on stated grounds.

A workable profile for a pre-apprentice has three parts.

**Role criteria.** What is this person's job, and does that job put them near the decision or the work you care about? For an apprenticeship-track conversation, that usually means engineering managers, team leads, senior individual contributors on the team you want to join, technical recruiters, and talent or early-career program owners. It usually does not mean a CEO of a 4,000-person company, a sales director with no line into engineering, or a job title you cannot explain.

**Organization criteria.** What kind of employer? Size, industry, location or remote posture, and — the criterion that outranks all of them this week — whether they are a CCC employer partner. Partner organizations have a warm relationship with the program already. That is an unfair advantage and you should use it.

**Relevance criteria.** Is there a specific, findable reason this person would reply to *you*? A recent post, a role they are hiring for, a shared background, a project they shipped, a school or city in common. "They exist and have a LinkedIn account" is not relevance.

Write yours as three or four sentences, not a paragraph of hedges. A good one is concrete enough that another person in the cohort could use it to sort your list the same way you would. For example:

```text
ICP v1 — Marcus T.
Role: engineering manager, team lead, or senior developer on a web/data team;
      OR technical recruiter / early-career program owner.
Org:  regional employer, 50-2000 people, hybrid or remote-friendly, metro area
      or adjacent. CCC employer partners rank above everything else.
Relevance: must have at least ONE findable hook — open junior/apprentice-track
      role, a post in the last 90 days, a shared school/city/prior industry,
      or a shipped project I can name specifically.
Disqualify: no engineering function, C-suite at 1000+ headcount, no hook found
      after 5 minutes of research, contact already worked by a cohort peer.
```

Note the last block. A profile that only says yes is half a profile. Naming your disqualifiers in advance is what stops you from talking yourself into a bad prospect at 4:45 on a Thursday because you want the number to go up.

## Qualifying prospects against the profile

Qualification is the act of running each name past the profile and recording the verdict. Do it in your CRM, as a field, not in your memory.

Add three properties to your contact records this week if they do not exist yet: a **fit tier**, a **hook**, and a **disqualified reason**. Tier is a small scale you will actually use — A, B, C is plenty:

- **A** — meets role, org, and relevance criteria, and is at a priority or partner employer. Personalized outreach, multi-threaded, followed up deliberately.
- **B** — meets role and org but the hook is thin, or the employer is not a priority. Worth a good message, not worth an hour of research.
- **C** — fails a criterion. Not worked this week. Keep the record; do not delete history.

Fit tier is not the same field as the Week 1 **priority tier** (1, 2, 3), and you keep both. Priority tier describes the *employer* — partner, target, or other. Fit tier describes *this person* against your ICP. A contact at a Tier 1 partner employer can still be a C if they have no line into the work and no findable hook; the right move there is usually to map a better contact at the same employer, not to message the weak one harder.

The hook field is the discipline that makes tier A mean something. If you cannot type a specific sentence into it — "hiring two junior data analysts, posted 11 days ago" or "posted about mentoring career-changers on March 4" — the contact is not an A no matter how much you want them to be. This one field is also what Lesson 03 will draw on when you write message variants, so a vague hook now costs you twice.

Time-box the research: five minutes per prospect is the standard in this lab. Past five minutes with no hook, the answer is C and you move on. Prospecting fails far more often from over-researching twenty people than from under-researching two hundred.

A word on sources. Public, professional, verifiable: the person's LinkedIn profile and recent activity, the company careers page and site, published news or press, public GitHub or conference talks. If a piece of information would feel strange to cite out loud in the first meeting — "I saw your neighborhood on a public records site" — it does not belong in your CRM or your message. Professional relevance only.

## Multi-threading: 2-3 contacts inside each priority account

Now the second move, and the one that most often separates a pipeline that produces conversations from one that produces silence.

Single-threading is having exactly one contact inside an organization. It feels efficient and it is fragile. That one person can be on leave, drowning in a launch, three levels away from anything you want, or simply gone — and when they do not reply, the whole account is dead and you have learned nothing about why.

**Multi-threading** means deliberately mapping two to three relevant people inside each priority organization and working them as one account rather than three unrelated names. It works for reasons that have nothing to do with pestering people:

- **Redundancy.** Three people means three chances that one of them has a free twenty minutes this month.
- **Different doors.** A recruiter, a hiring manager, and a senior engineer on the team each know different things and can each do different things for you. The recruiter knows the process and the timeline. The manager knows what the team actually needs. The engineer knows what the work is really like and is often the most willing to talk, because nobody is asking them for a job.
- **Internal referral.** The most common good outcome of a well-run account is not a direct yes. It is one contact saying "you should talk to Dana, she runs that team" — a warm internal handoff that no cold message can buy.
- **Signal.** If all three go quiet, that tells you something real about the account. If one replies warmly, that tells you where the door is.

Mapping an account is a short, structured exercise. Pick a priority employer. Identify the function you want to be part of. Then find one person in each of these lanes, using the company's people page, LinkedIn's company view, the careers page, and public engineering or team content:

1. **The practitioner** — a developer, analyst, or specialist doing roughly the work you are training for.
2. **The decision-maker** — the team lead or engineering manager that person likely reports to.
3. **The process owner** — a technical recruiter, talent partner, or early-career program lead.

Two of the three is a legitimate map when the third genuinely is not findable; do not invent a name to fill a slot. Record all of them in the CRM against the same company, with the account marked as a priority, and log the reasoning: why this org, why these three, and which one you are approaching first.

Order matters. Approach them in a staggered sequence over several days, not all three within an hour. Lead with the lane most likely to reply — for most cohort members that is the practitioner, because the ask is smallest and the flattery is real. Never imply to one contact that you have not spoken to their colleague. If two of them reply, be straightforwardly honest with both: you reached out to a couple of people on the team, and you are glad to talk to either or both.

## What "good" looks like at the end of this lesson

Your CRM should now have a written ICP attached to it, every contact tiered, an A-tier that you can defend contact by contact, and your priority employer-partner accounts each carrying two to three mapped contacts with a stated approach order. The list is probably shorter than it was on Monday in terms of names you will actually work. That is the point. Next lesson you will write to these people, and you will finally have something specific to say.

## Practice

Work in your live CRM. All of this feeds the Week 2 milestone.

1. **Write ICP v1.** Draft your ideal contact profile in the format above: role criteria, organization criteria, relevance criteria, and an explicit disqualify list. Keep it under 150 words. Post it where your instructor can see it and take one round of feedback.
2. **Qualify 30 existing contacts.** Add `fit_tier`, `hook`, and `disqualified_reason` fields to your CRM. Run 30 contacts already in your pipeline past ICP v1 and tier each one A, B, or C. Enforce the five-minute research cap with a timer.
3. **Defend three calls.** Pick one A, one B, and one C from the batch and write a single sentence for each explaining the tier in ICP terms. Trade with a partner in the lab and have them try to overturn one of your calls. Revise ICP v1 if they are right — that is a successful exercise, not a failed one.
4. **Map three priority accounts.** Choose three priority employer-partner organizations. For each, map 2-3 contacts across the practitioner / decision-maker / process-owner lanes, log them in the CRM against the same company, and record why each person is in the map.
5. **Set the approach order.** For each mapped account, write down which contact you will approach first, on which day, and what the hook is. You will send these messages in Lesson 03, so leave the hook field filled in and specific.
6. **Report your numbers.** At the end of the block, report to the lab: contacts qualified, A-tier count, accounts mapped, total mapped contacts. Bring the A-tier list to the next session.
