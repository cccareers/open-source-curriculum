## Closing is a plan, not a moment

The folklore version of closing is a moment of theater: the right words, the pen slid across the table, the assumptive question that traps the buyer into agreement. That model was built for one-call transactional sales and it is actively harmful in a B2B software deal, where the person you are talking to cannot buy anything alone. Your champion does not sign. They convene, they justify, they route through security review and procurement and finance, and they answer questions from people you may never meet.

So the useful definition is this: **closing is converting a decision into a dated plan with named owners on both sides.** The plan is the deliverable. If a buyer says yes and there is no plan, you have an enthusiastic contact and no deal. If the plan exists, is agreed in writing, and both sides are executing it, the deal is closing whether or not anybody has said an inspiring sentence.

That is why this lesson gives the closing techniques about a third of its space and the mutual action plan the rest. The techniques are how you ask; the plan is what you get.

## Before you can close: the three conditions

Three things must be true before a close is a close rather than a hope. Check them explicitly, out loud, and be honest when one is missing.

**The value is agreed and quantified by them.** Not by you, and not in your slides. Somewhere in the record the buyer has stated what the problem costs and what solving it is worth. If you cannot quote them saying it, go back — that is a discovery gap, and closing pressure will not fill it.

**The commercial package is settled.** Price, term, and the non-price items are agreed, or at least the open items are named and few. Closing over an unresolved commercial question invites the last-minute squeeze.

**The path is known.** You know every step between here and a signature — who approves, what security review requires, whether legal is queued, what the procurement process is, and how long each takes. Most deals that "go quiet" have not gone anywhere; they are sitting in a step nobody told you about.

The third condition is where most cycles fail, and the mutual action plan exists to make it impossible to fail there quietly.

## Asking, plainly

The single most under-used closing technique is asking a clear question and then not talking. Everything else is refinement.

**The direct ask.** "Are we doing this?" or "Do you want to go ahead?" It is startling how rarely it is said. Direct asks are respectful of a buyer's time, they surface hesitation immediately, and they produce a real answer where softer framing produces a polite one.

**The summary close.** Recap what they said they needed, what was agreed, and what the outcome is, then ask. It works because it forces you to have listened — the recap has to be in their words. *"You needed the escalation count trending down before the next launch, you needed finance comfortable with a one-year payback, and you needed it live before September. All three are in this. Shall we get it started?"*

**The alternative close.** Two acceptable options rather than yes-or-no: "Do you want to start with the two regions, or go company-wide from day one?" It works when the decision is genuinely made and the buyer is stuck on how rather than whether. It is manipulative — and transparent — when whether is still open.

**The conditional close.** "If I can get the uplift cap approved, are we in a position to sign this month?" This is the honest version of an if-then and it is essential in negotiation: it ensures anything you go and fight for internally buys a commitment rather than another round.

**The trial close.** Not an ask, a temperature check, used throughout: "How does that sit with you?" "On a scale of one to ten, where are you on this?" A seven is the most useful answer in sales — always follow with "what would make it a nine?"

**The negative close.** Used when a deal has gone quiet: "I get the sense this has slipped down the list, and that's completely fine — should I stop chasing you?" It is the Sandler instinct applied at the end, and it either revives the deal or frees your quarter. Deliver it sincerely or not at all.

Two prohibitions. Do not use urgency you invented — a fake expiring discount destroys trust with the exact buyer who checks. And do not close on a person who cannot buy; asking a stakeholder with no authority for a commitment they cannot make produces a meaningless yes and wastes a cycle.

## The commitment ladder

Closing is not one event; it is a series of escalating commitments, each of which should be slightly harder for the buyer to give than the last. Thinking in terms of a ladder does two things: it gives you a specific, appropriate ask for the end of every conversation, and it produces an honest reading of where a deal actually is.

| Rung | What you ask for | What it proves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | A second meeting with a stated agenda | Mild interest |
| 2 | Access to someone they have not yet involved | You are worth their political capital |
| 3 | Their data, or time from their team | Real evaluation, real cost to them |
| 4 | A named, dated evaluation criterion | They are building a decision, not browsing |
| 5 | An agreed mutual action plan | The organization is engaged, not just a person |
| 6 | Commercial terms agreed | Decision effectively made |
| 7 | Signature | — |

Two rules. **Ask for one rung, not three** — a request that skips levels gets refused and costs you the smaller commitment you would have got. And **grade the answer honestly**: a buyer who will happily take another meeting but will not give you thirty minutes of an engineer's time is telling you the evaluation is not real yet, however warm the meetings feel.

The most common self-deception in a sales pipeline is counting rung-one commitments as evidence of a rung-five deal. Meetings are cheap. Introductions, data, and dates are not, and only the expensive commitments predict anything.

## The mutual action plan

A mutual action plan (also called a close plan or joint execution plan) is a shared, dated schedule of every step between today and go-live, with a named owner on each side for each step. It is built collaboratively, agreed in writing, and reviewed on every call.

It does five things at once, and this is why it is the best single artifact in enterprise selling.

**It exposes the real timeline.** A buyer who says "we want this by September" and then sees that security review takes six weeks and procurement adds four discovers, in front of you, that September requires a decision this month. That realization is more motivating than anything you could say.

**It surfaces hidden steps and hidden people.** Building the plan forces the question "who else has to touch this?" — and the answer routinely includes a security architect, a data-protection officer, or a divisional director nobody had mentioned. Every one of those is a person who can kill your deal in week nine if you meet them for the first time in week nine.

**It makes commitment mutual and visible.** Your side has tasks too: reference calls to arrange, questionnaires to complete, an implementation staffing plan to produce. A buyer watching you hit your dates is a buyer accumulating evidence about what you will be like as a vendor.

**It converts vague stalls into specific, discussable facts.** Without a plan, silence is ambiguous. With a plan, "the security review was due Tuesday and hasn't started" is a concrete conversation you can have without nagging.

**It is a qualification instrument.** A buyer who will not build one with you is telling you something true. Not necessarily no — but not now, or not with this person. That information is worth more in week two than in week twelve.

### What a plan contains

- **The goal, with a date.** Not "sign the contract" — the buyer's outcome. "Support team live on the new escalation flow before the November launch."
- **Backwards-planned milestones** from that date, not forwards from today.
- **One named owner per step, on each side.** Names, not departments. "Security" is not an owner; "Ravi Menon, Security Architect" is.
- **Dependencies made explicit.** Which steps cannot start until another finishes.
- **Known duration for each step**, taken from the buyer's own experience of their process.
- **Decision points**, marked clearly — the moments where a person says yes or no.

### A worked plan

The deal from earlier lessons: a support platform, target go-live 1 November, today is 14 July. Buyer contact is Dana, Director of Support.

| # | Step | Owner (them) | Owner (us) | Date | Depends on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Technical session with engineering managers | Dana | You | 22 Jul | — |
| 2 | Reference call with comparable customer | Marcus Webb, VP Eng | You arrange | 29 Jul | 1 |
| 3 | Business case reviewed with CFO | Dana | You supply model | 5 Aug | 2 |
| 4 | Security questionnaire returned | Ravi Menon | Our security team | 12 Aug | — |
| 5 | Security review complete | Ravi Menon | — | 23 Sep | 4 (6 weeks) |
| 6 | Commercial terms agreed | Dana + Procurement | You | 15 Aug | 3 |
| 7 | MSA to legal both sides | Their counsel | Our counsel | 18 Aug | 6 |
| 8 | Legal complete | Their counsel | Our counsel | 12 Sep | 7 |
| 9 | **Decision / signature** | Dana + CFO | You | 26 Sep | 5, 8 |
| 10 | Implementation kickoff | Dana | Implementation lead | 3 Oct | 9 |
| 11 | Pilot team live | Dana | Implementation lead | 17 Oct | 10 |
| 12 | Full go-live before launch | Dana | Implementation lead | 1 Nov | 11 |

Read what the plan reveals. The critical path is not commercial — it is the six-week security review, which must start by 12 August or November is impossible. Ravi Menon has never been in a meeting with you. And the signature date is 26 September, which means every "let's talk again after the summer" is a plan to miss the launch. None of this is an argument you have to make; it is arithmetic on a page the buyer helped write.

### Building it in the conversation

The plan is built *with* the buyer, on a call, out loud. Doing it alone and emailing it produces a vendor document nobody owns.

> **You:** You've said November for the launch, and I'd like to work backwards from that rather than forwards from today, because I've seen this go wrong twice this year on security review timing. Can we build it now, on the call? *(Frames it as protecting their date, not chasing your deal.)*
>
> **Dana:** Sure.
>
> **You:** If go-live is 1 November, and I'd want your pilot team on it two weeks before that — call it 17 October — how long does your implementation window realistically need before that?
>
> **Dana:** Two weeks, if we've got people. Say kickoff early October.
>
> **You:** Then signature has to be late September at the latest. Working back from there: what has to happen before a signature that I might not know about? *(The question that finds hidden steps.)*
>
> **Dana:** Legal, obviously. And security have to sign off — that's a whole thing.
>
> **You:** How long does security take, from when they receive the questionnaire?
>
> **Dana:** Six weeks. They don't move.
>
> **You:** Six weeks from 23 September is 12 August — that's four weeks away, and it's the step that decides whether November happens. Who owns it on your side? *(Names the critical path, asks for the name.)*
>
> **Dana:** Ravi Menon.
>
> **You:** Can we get the questionnaire to Ravi this month, and can I meet him? If he raises something structural I'd rather find out in July than in September. And what does he need from us to start? *(Meets the hidden stakeholder early, which is the deal-saving move.)*
>
> **Dana:** He'll want the SOC 2 report and the data-flow diagram.
>
> **You:** I'll have both to you Thursday — that's my task, 17 July. And the CFO conversation for the business case: is that before or after commercial terms? *(Takes a dated task, keeps building.)*
>
> **Dana:** Before. She'll want to know the number's real before we haggle.
>
> **You:** Then that's 5 August. Last one, and it's the awkward one: on 26 September, whose signature is on it — yours, hers, or both? *(Establishes the actual decider before the plan is agreed, not at signature.)*
>
> **Dana:** Hers, over my recommendation.
>
> **You:** Then I'd like her to have met me before she's asked to sign something I sold to you. Not a pitch — fifteen minutes after the business-case review. Can you make that happen? *(Multithreading, justified by the plan rather than by wanting a meeting.)*
>
> **Dana:** That's reasonable.
>
> **You:** I'll write all of this up this afternoon with owners and dates and send it over. If anything's wrong, change it and send it back — it's as much yours as mine. And can we spend two minutes on it at the top of every call from here? *(Written confirmation, shared ownership, recurring review.)*

Notice what closed here. No pressure, no technique, no urgency. What happened is that a plan now exists in which the buyer has committed dates, produced two new stakeholders, and discovered a deadline of her own.

### Working the plan

**Send it the same day**, as a table in the email body or a shared document — not an attachment nobody opens. Ask for corrections; corrections are engagement.

**Review it at the top of every call.** Two minutes. What moved, what slipped, what changed.

**When a date slips, ask what it means rather than chasing the task.** "Security hasn't started — does that mean November is off, or does something else compress?" A slipped date on a shared plan is a legitimate conversation about their goal, not a vendor nagging.

**Use it to multithread.** Every step with an owner you have not met is a warranted introduction request.

**Keep your own dates religiously.** The plan is the only evidence a buyer has of what your company will be like after the money changes hands.

## Where closes fail

**Closing a champion instead of a decider.** Your contact says yes with real enthusiasm and no authority. The plan prevents this by forcing the question "whose signature is on it?" early, while there is still time to meet that person.

**A close with no written confirmation.** A verbal agreement that is never summarized will be remembered differently by each party within two weeks. Whoever writes the first summary sets the terms of the memory; be that person, the same day, every time.

**Pressure substituting for a plan.** Follow-up cadence, urgency language, and expiring offers are what representatives reach for when they have no visibility into the buyer's process. They produce short-term motion and long-term avoidance. The cure is not more pressure; it is asking what the actual next step is and who owns it.

**Treating "yes" as the end.** In a subscription deal the yes is roughly the halfway point of the elapsed calendar. Security review, legal, and procurement all sit after the decision, and every one of them can add weeks. A plan that stops at "decision" is a plan that misses its go-live date.

**Vague next steps.** "We'll reconnect in a couple of weeks" is not a next step, it is the absence of one. Every conversation ends with a named action, an owner, and a date, or it ends with an honest acknowledgment that there isn't one — which is itself useful information.

**Never asking.** The most common failure of all, and the least discussed. A deal that has been worked properly still requires somebody to say "shall we do this?" out loud. If you find you consistently reach the end of calls without asking, the problem is not technique — it is that you are not sure you have earned it, and the fix is upstream in discovery.

## The end game

Two situations recur. Handle both from the plan.

**The last-minute demand.** At signature, procurement asks for one more thing. It is a nibble, and it is answered exactly as the previous two lessons taught: re-open the package, or trade. The plan helps because signature is a dated commitment they made, so the response has a natural shape: *"I can look at that, and if I do we'll need to re-agree the package, which puts the 26th at risk. Is that the trade you want?"*

**The silent buyer.** The plan converts silence into a specific missed step, which gives you something to say other than "just checking in." If two review points pass with nothing moving, use the negative close: name what you observe, offer to stop, and mean it.

Finally, the handoff. When it is signed, the last item on the plan is the transition to whoever owns the customer afterwards — introduce them against the plan, with the buyer's stated goal and dates intact, so nothing established in the sale is lost. Everything past that point belongs to the retention course in this pathway, not to you.

## Practice

Work in pairs with an observer. Around two hours; the plan-building is most of it.

1. **Write three asks.** For one live or realistic opportunity, write a direct ask, a summary close in the buyer's own words, and a conditional close tied to something you would need approved. Read all three aloud and time the silence after each — anything under four seconds needs another run.

2. **Trial-close drill.** Ten rapid rounds. Your partner gives a number one to ten in response to "where are you on this?" and you must respond with one question that finds the gap. Any response that pitches instead of asking is a fail.

3. **Build a plan live.** Your partner plays a buyer with a target go-live date and three hidden process steps written on a card (for example: an architecture review board that meets monthly, a data-protection assessment, a divisional VP whose sign-off is required). You must build the plan backwards from the date, on the call, and surface all three. Score how many you found.

4. **Find the critical path.** Take the plan you just built and identify the single step whose duration decides whether the date is achievable. Write the sentence you would say to the buyer about it — one sentence, no drama.

5. **Name the owners.** Go through your plan and replace every departmental owner with a person's name. For each name you cannot supply, write the exact request you would make to get introduced, justified by the plan rather than by your interest.

6. **Write the recap.** Produce the confirmation email: goal, table with owners and dates, your committed tasks, the two-minute review agreement, and a request for corrections. Under 200 words. Have your partner mark anything ambiguous — ambiguity here becomes a stall in six weeks.

7. **The negative close.** Two review points have passed and the buyer has gone silent. Deliver a sincere negative close in two sentences. Your partner responds however they genuinely feel like responding; debrief on whether it read as honest or as a technique.
