## The negotiation is won on paper, before it is spoken

A price conversation you have not planned is a price conversation you will lose, because the person on the other side has planned theirs. Procurement professionals negotiate all day, every day, against vendors who each negotiate a handful of times a quarter. They have a target, a script, a set of standard moves, and an internal scorecard measured in savings percentage. If your preparation consists of knowing your list price and hoping, you are the amateur in the room.

The good news is that the asymmetry is entirely fixable with about ninety minutes of preparation per deal, and the preparation is a small number of numbers written down in advance. This lesson builds that page: your four price points, your concession ladder, and what each rung costs you.

Two boundaries before we start. First, this lesson is about **money** — list price, discount, the shape of the discount curve, and the discipline of making the buyer pay for a lower number. The specific inventory of non-price terms you can trade, and how each one is negotiated, is the next lesson; here they appear only as the currency a concession is bought with. Second, you are negotiating **commercial terms**. Contract language — indemnities, liability caps, data-processing clauses — is not yours to negotiate and never becomes yours to negotiate, however reasonable the request sounds in the moment.

## The four numbers

Before any pricing conversation, write down four numbers. Not three. Not "a rough idea."

**Your opening.** The number you will put in front of the buyer first. It is not your list price if list is a fiction nobody pays; it is a number you can state with a straight face and defend with a rationale.

**Your target.** The realistic, good outcome. This is the number your plan is designed to land on, and it should be based on comparable closed deals, not on optimism.

**Your walk-away.** The point below which the deal is worse for your company than no deal. Genuinely worse — accounting for support cost, implementation cost, the precedent it sets with this buyer's peers, and the renewal you will now be negotiating from a lower base.

**Your BATNA.** Your best alternative to a negotiated agreement: what you actually do if this deal dies. "Nothing, my quarter is destroyed" is a weak BATNA and you will negotiate like it. "I have two deals of similar size at the same stage" is strong. The point of writing it down is calibration — a weak BATNA is a reason to be *more* careful about opening high and conceding slowly, not less.

Two further quantities make the ladder meaningful.

**The cost of a discount point.** Compute it once for your product and never forget it. Suppose a deal has a list of $200,000 annual and a gross margin of 78%. A 10% discount removes $20,000 of revenue and $20,000 of margin — because discount comes entirely out of margin — so it removes about 13% of the deal's margin. To recover that margin at the same discount you would need to sell roughly 15% more volume. Say that out loud a few times. A discount that feels like a small courtesy is a large fraction of the profit.

**The precedent cost.** Whatever you give this customer, you have given their renewal, and you have very likely given the next three customers who talk to them at a conference. In subscription businesses the discount is not a one-time cost; it is applied to every year of the term and it becomes the baseline the renewal negotiates *down* from.

## Anchoring

The first number in a negotiation exerts a gravitational pull on every number that follows. This is one of the most robustly replicated findings in behavioral research, and it survives even when everyone in the room knows about it.

**Should you go first?** Yes, when you have a reasonable read on the buyer's budget and value — which, if you ran the earlier lessons of this course properly, you do. Going first sets the frame. Wait for the buyer's number only when you genuinely have no idea of scale, and understand that you are then negotiating inside *their* frame all the way down.

**Anchor with a rationale, not just a number.** A bare number invites a bare counter. A number attached to a mechanism invites a discussion about the mechanism. "Two hundred thousand" versus "for 400 seats at your tier with the integration package, it's 200,000 a year — that's 500 a seat, and it's priced against the 300k of leakage you measured." The second is much harder to counter with an arbitrary percentage because the arbitrary percentage now has to argue with a reason.

**Anchor high but defensible.** An anchor so far outside the plausible range that the buyer disengages costs you credibility, and credibility is what you are trading on. The upper bound of defensible is roughly: the highest number you could justify to this buyer, in their terms, without embarrassment.

**Anchor in the right unit.** You control the denominator. Per seat per month sounds smaller than annual contract value; annual contract value sounds smaller than three-year total. Choose the unit that makes the number comprehensible next to the value you established, and be prepared for procurement to convert it into whichever unit sounds largest.

**Present a range or a menu, not a point.** Offering two or three shapes — a lean package, a standard, and a full — does three things: it moves the conversation from *whether* to *which*, it reveals the buyer's priorities by what they gravitate to, and it gives you a legitimate way to reduce price later by reducing scope.

### Defending against their anchor

When procurement opens with "we have 120,000 budgeted" or "we need 30% off," three moves in sequence.

**Do not counter immediately.** An instant counter-number legitimizes theirs as one end of a range and lands you at the midpoint, which is a mechanically bad place if their anchor was extreme.

**Reject the frame, calmly, and ask where it came from.** *"That's a long way from where this sits. Can I ask how you got to 120 — is it a budget you've had approved, or a target?"* You have learned whether it is a real constraint (which you can design around) or an opening position (which you should ignore).

**Re-anchor with the value, then re-state your number.** *"Here's my problem with 120: at 400 seats, that prices this below what you told me the leakage costs you in a single quarter. Two hundred is where it sits. What I can do is talk about the shape — term, timing, what's in scope — and there's room in those."* You have held the number and signaled that trades exist. That signal is the entire subject of the next section.

**Never split the difference reflexively.** Splitting is a habit, not a strategy. If you are at 200 and they are at 120, meeting at 160 rewards an unreasonable anchor and teaches them to open further apart next time.

## The concession ladder

A concession plan is the sequence of price moves you are willing to make, in order, each with a defined size and a defined price the buyer pays for it. Write it before the call.

Four rules govern it.

**1. Every concession is bought, never given.** The sentence you should be able to say in your sleep: *"I can do X, if you can do Y."* Never "let me see what I can do" — that phrase concedes without receiving and trains the buyer that asking is sufficient.

**2. Concessions shrink.** 8%, then 3%, then 1%. A decreasing pattern signals you are approaching a limit. An increasing or flat pattern (5%, 5%, 5%) tells the buyer there is always another 5% and guarantees another round. This is the most commonly violated rule and the most expensive.

**3. Concessions are conditional and revocable.** A concession is offered as a package: *"At 24 months with annual prepay, the number is 178. If the term goes back to 12, the number goes back to 200."* Everything you gave is attached to something you got, and it evaporates if that thing does.

**4. Concessions are labeled and made to cost you something visibly.** Announce the effort. "I had to take this to my VP and I had to justify it with the term commitment." A concession that appears effortless is worth nothing and implies more behind it. Do not fabricate the approval — most companies genuinely have discount thresholds, and using the real one is both honest and effective.

A worked plan, for the $200,000 deal above with a walk-away at $160,000:

| Rung | You move to | Discount | You require in return | Margin impact |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Opening | 200,000 | 0% | Nothing | Baseline |
| 1 | 184,000 | 8% | 24-month term, signature this quarter | -8k margin/yr, +1 yr locked |
| 2 | 178,000 | 11% | Annual prepay instead of quarterly | -3k, cash forward, churn risk down |
| 3 | 174,000 | 13% | Named reference + one case study | -2k, marketing asset |
| Floor | 168,000 | 16% | All of the above, plus exec sponsor call | Approval required at VP |
| Walk-away | 160,000 | 20% | Not available at this scope | Reduce scope instead |

Read the last row carefully. Below the floor you do not keep discounting — you **reduce scope**. That is the release valve that keeps price integrity intact: the buyer can have a smaller number, but for less. Fewer seats, fewer modules, a shorter term, a lower support tier. "I can get you to 150, but not for this — for this we'd take the analytics module out" is a completely different message from "fine, 150," and it protects both your margin and your list price's meaning.

Notice too that each rung buys something of real value: term length reduces churn and increases lifetime value; prepayment moves cash forward and reduces collection risk; a reference has genuine pipeline value. You are not inventing tokens — you are trading assets. The full inventory of what you can ask for, and how to negotiate each item, is the next lesson.

## Discount governance is a tool, not an obstacle

Most companies set discount authority in tiers: the representative can approve a small band alone, a manager or VP a larger one, and anything beyond that requires a deal desk or an executive. New representatives experience this as bureaucracy. It is in fact the most useful structural asset you have in a negotiation, for three reasons.

It gives you a **real** higher authority, so you never have to invent one. "I can do 8% on my own signature; past that I have to write a justification to my VP explaining what we got in return" is true, verifiable, and it makes the requirement for a trade a matter of policy rather than of your personality. Buyers argue with people; they rarely argue with process.

It **slows the negotiation down at exactly the right moment**. A concession that requires a day and an internal conversation reads as expensive, which is the impression you want. A concession granted in the same breath as the request reads as available all along.

And it **creates a legitimate reason to ask for something**. If the approval genuinely requires a written justification of what was received, then asking for a term commitment or a reference is not a negotiating flourish — it is the thing that makes the approval possible.

Two disciplines follow. Know your exact authority before every pricing call, because discovering it mid-conversation makes you hedge. And never approach your VP without the trade already in hand; a representative who requests approval on the strength of "they're asking for it" is asking their company to fund their discomfort.

## A worked pricing exchange

Same deal: 400 seats, opening $200,000, target around $180,000, floor $168,000, walk-away $160,000, ladder as above.

> **Buyer:** So where does this land?
>
> **You:** For 400 seats on the tier you need, with the integration package, it's 200,000 a year — 500 a seat. I've priced it against the 300,000 of under-billing you measured last year, because that's the thing it's supposed to stop. *(Anchor first, with a rationale, in the unit that sits next to their number.)*
>
> **Buyer:** *(sharp intake of breath)* That's well above what we had in mind.
>
> **You:** *(silence — four seconds. The flinch is theater; the next person to speak concedes.)*
>
> **Buyer:** We were thinking 130.
>
> **You:** That's a long way apart. Can I ask how you got to 130 — is that an approved budget, or a target you've been given? *(No counter-number. Find out whether the constraint is real.)*
>
> **Buyer:** It's what we spend on the current stack.
>
> **You:** That's useful, and it's the comparison I'd push back on — the current stack is what produced the 300,000. Two hundred is where this sits, and I'm not going to move it just because it's uncomfortable. What I can do is talk about the shape of the deal, and there's genuine room there. If you can go to twenty-four months and get it signed this quarter, I can do 184. *(Holds the anchor, re-frames the comparison, then rung one — 8%, with both halves of the trade in one sentence.)*
>
> **Buyer:** Twenty-four months is a big ask. What if we did twenty-four but paid quarterly?
>
> **You:** Then 184 isn't the number. The 8% is partly funded by the cash — quarterly billing takes that away. At twenty-four months and quarterly, I'm back at about 192. If you can do annual in advance, I can go to 178, and that's a bigger move than the last one looks. *(Revocable and conditional: the concession evaporates when its payment is withdrawn. Note the decrement has shrunk from 16k to 6k — the shape of the ladder is itself a message.)*
>
> **Buyer:** Let me take 178 to my director. I think she'll want to see it start with a 16.
>
> **You:** I'd rather she saw a number I can actually stand behind. There's one more move in this and it isn't free: 174, if you'll be a named reference after six months and do one case study. Below that I'd have to take it to my VP, and she'll ask what we got — so if 168 is genuinely where this has to land, tell me what else is available and I'll go and make the case. *(Rung three, 2k decrement, real authority named, and the request for payment made before the approval is sought.)*
>
> **Buyer:** And if none of that works?
>
> **You:** Then we're not discounting further, we're buying less. I can get you to 150 by taking the analytics module out and starting at 320 seats. It's a smaller deal and a smaller result, and it's a real option — but it isn't 400 seats and analytics for 150. *(The scope-reduction valve. Price integrity intact, and the buyer now has to choose between value and number rather than simply pushing.)*

Trace the decrements: 16k, 6k, 4k. Each one bought something. The floor was never crossed, the list price still means something, and the alternative below the floor is a different product rather than the same product cheaper.

## Their playbook, and your counters

Procurement moves are standardized. Recognize them by name and they lose most of their power.

**The flinch.** A visible, audible reaction to your number — the sharp intake of breath. It is theater, and it works because silence is uncomfortable. *Counter:* say nothing. Let the flinch sit. The next person to speak usually concedes.

**The nibble.** Small additional asks after agreement is essentially reached, each too minor to fight over — an extra ten seats, a training day, an extra environment. Individually trivial, collectively expensive, and they never stop while they keep working. *Counter:* re-open the whole package. "I can add that, and if I do, we're back to looking at the total. Shall we do that, or shall we keep what we agreed?" One application usually ends nibbling for good.

**Higher authority.** "I'd love to, but the committee won't approve above X." Sometimes true, often a device to extract concessions without reciprocating. *Counter:* mirror it — "I have the same constraint; anything past 12% needs my VP and she'll ask what we got for it" — and ask to meet the authority. If they will not produce the committee, treat the constraint as soft.

**The deadline.** "Our board meets Thursday, we need the revised number by Wednesday." Manufactured urgency transfers pressure to you. *Counter:* accept the date, attach your own condition to it. "I can get approval by Wednesday if I can tell my VP the signature date is the 30th."

**The auction / phantom competitor.** "Your competitor is 25% cheaper." Sometimes true, often not, and rarely like-for-like. *Counter:* ask what is in their number. Scope, term, support tier, implementation, and included volumes are almost never identical. "Happy to look at it — does their number include implementation and the 24-month price hold, or is that year one only?"

**The salami.** Slicing the negotiation into item-by-item concessions so you never see the cumulative total. *Counter:* insist on negotiating the package. "Let's not do these one at a time — put everything on the list, and I'll come back with one answer to all of it." This single move is worth several points of margin over a career.

**The end-of-quarter squeeze.** Buyers know your fiscal calendar. Waiting until day 89 to demand a concession is not cynicism, it is competence on their part. *Counter:* structure timing incentives *early*, and make them expire. A signature-date discount offered in week two is a legitimate trade; the same discount extracted from you on the last day is a defeat you set yourself up for.

## Discipline in the moment

Preparation fails at three predictable points.

**The silence after your number.** Say the price, then stop. Any sentence you add after a price is a discount in waiting — "...but there's some flexibility" is the most expensive clause in sales.

**Negotiating against yourself.** The buyer says nothing, or says "that's high," and you produce a better number unprompted. If you have made two consecutive moves without them moving, you are bidding against yourself. Stop and ask what they can do.

**Conceding to end discomfort.** Most unplanned discounts are given to make an awkward moment stop. Knowing this about yourself is most of the fix. The written ladder helps because it converts a moment of social pressure into a lookup: *is this rung on the sheet, and have they paid for it?*

Finally, keep two administrative disciplines. Confirm every agreed package in writing the same day — "as discussed: 178, 24 months, annual prepay, reference agreed" — because an unwritten concession will be remembered generously by whoever benefits. And log what you gave and what you got, per deal. Over a quarter that log will tell you which of your concessions the buyer actually valued, which is the only reliable way to improve your ladder.

## Practice

Work in pairs; an observer with a calculator is useful. Around two hours.

1. **Compute your discount arithmetic.** For a product you know, write down list price, gross margin, and then the margin cost of 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20% discounts on a representative deal, both for year one and across a three-year term. Write the volume increase required to recover each. Keep this card.

2. **Build a prep page.** For a specific opportunity, write the four numbers: opening, target, walk-away, BATNA. For each, write one sentence justifying it. If your walk-away is not genuinely worse than no deal, it is not a walk-away — fix it.

3. **Build the ladder.** Three to four rungs, decreasing in size, each with a specific requirement attached and its margin impact. Then write the scope-reduction option that sits below your floor. Have your partner attack it: are the decrements really decreasing? Is any rung purchasable with something worthless?

4. **Anchor, live.** Deliver your opening number with rationale in under twenty seconds. Partner flinches theatrically. You may not speak for five seconds. Run it three times until the silence is comfortable.

5. **Defend against an aggressive anchor.** The buyer opens at 40% below your opening. Run the three-move sequence: no counter-number, ask where it came from, re-anchor on value and signal that trades exist. The observer's job is to catch you if you name any number before the third move.

6. **Tactic recognition round.** The buyer runs four of the six plays above in a fifteen-minute negotiation without announcing them. The seller must name each one aloud immediately after it happens ("that's a nibble") and then respond. Score the recognition and the response separately.

7. **Debrief on paper.** Write down the final package and compare it against your ladder. Which rung did you land on? What did you receive for it? Any concession you cannot name a payment for was given away, and that is the number to bring to the next round.
