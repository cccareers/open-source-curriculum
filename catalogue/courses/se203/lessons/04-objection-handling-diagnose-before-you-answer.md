## The answer is the easy part

By now you can acknowledge an objection without arguing and without discounting. That is the floor. The reason experienced representatives still lose deals to objections is not that their answers are bad — it is that they answer accurately and *fluently* the wrong objection.

Consider a single sentence: **"Your price is higher than we expected."** Here are six things it can mean.

1. The number exceeds an allocated budget line that genuinely exists.
2. There is no budget line at all, and the size of the number is irrelevant.
3. A competitor quoted lower and the buyer is establishing a reference point.
4. The buyer has not internalized the value and is expressing that as price.
5. Procurement has instructed this person to say it to every vendor, always.
6. Someone senior who is not on this call said it, and your contact is relaying.

Every one of those needs a different response, and four of the six get *worse* if you respond to price. Answer (2) with a discount and you have made an unbudgeted purchase slightly cheaper, which changes nothing. Answer (5) with a discount and you have taught a professional negotiator that one sentence is worth 10% — and they have four more sentences.

So the skill this lesson builds is not a response library. It is the discipline of spending one question, sometimes two, on diagnosis before you spend anything else, and then choosing a response that keeps the deal on merit — decided on whether your product solves an expensive problem — rather than on who blinks.

## Four axes of diagnosis

Diagnose along four axes. In a live call you will run them in a second and a half, which is achievable because each has a small number of possible values.

### Axis 1: What kind of gap is this?

| Type | Underneath it | What resolves it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Skepticism | Believes you *could* solve it, doubts that you *will* | Proof: reference, pilot, data, contractual commitment |
| Misunderstanding | Believes something untrue about your product | Correction — gently, and check where the belief came from |
| Drawback | A real gap; you genuinely don't do the thing | Acknowledge, then re-weight against what you do do |
| Indifference | Sees no problem worth solving | Not an objection response at all — go back to discovery |

Indifference is the misdiagnosis that wastes the most time. "We're fine as we are" is not a thing to overcome with a better answer; it means the earlier conversation did not establish a consequence worth acting on. The correct response is to stop handling and start asking.

A drawback is the one representatives handle worst, because the instinct is to fudge. Do not. "No, we don't do that today, and it isn't on the roadmap this year" buys enormous credibility, and it is the only honest position from which to ask the follow-up that matters: *"How much does that one weigh against the escalation problem you described?"* Buyers routinely rank a missing feature far lower than you fear once they are asked to weigh it explicitly.

### Axis 2: When in the cycle did it arrive?

The same words mean different things at different stages.

**Early** objections are usually qualification signals. "We already have a tool for that" in call one is mostly a request for a reason to keep talking. Treat early objections as cheap information and do not over-invest.

**Mid-cycle** objections are usually real evaluation content: integration concerns, adoption fears, comparisons. These deserve genuine proof.

**Late** objections — after the proposal, near signature — are almost never about their surface content. Late objections are about **risk and commitment**. A buyer who has been enthusiastic for six weeks and suddenly raises a functionality concern at signature is usually experiencing decision fear, or has been ambushed internally by someone you never met. Answering the functionality question technically will not resolve it. Asking "what changed?" often will.

### Axis 3: Whose objection is it?

Is the person speaking the person who holds the concern? Very often not.

- **Owned:** "I'm worried my team won't adopt it." Handle directly.
- **Proxy:** "Our CFO will say the payback is too long." You are hearing a rehearsal of someone else's argument. The response is not to rebut the CFO in absentia — it is to arm your champion or get in the room. *"When she says that, what's the number she'll compare it to? And would it be easier if I made that case directly, or would that be unhelpful?"*
- **Institutional:** "Procurement requires three bids and a 10% concession." Nobody in the room believes it is about you. Treat it as a process to navigate, not a persuasion problem.

Misreading a proxy objection as an owned one produces the classic failure where you win the argument with your champion, who then loses it upstairs.

### Axis 4: Is it an objection, a stall, or a condition?

An **objection** is a stated concern from an engaged buyer; it is answerable. A **stall** is a delay with no stated content — "circle back next quarter," "we're heads-down right now"; it hides something and must be surfaced before it can be answered. A **condition** is a fact that ends or defers the deal regardless of your skill: a signed competitor contract with eighteen months left, a hiring freeze, a compliance regime you do not meet. Conditions are respected, dated, and left alone; trying to talk a buyer out of a condition is how you lose the next cycle too.

## The diagnostic moves

Four moves cover nearly everything. Each costs one sentence.

**The split.** Offer two plausible readings and let the buyer choose. *"When you say the price is high — is it that the total is more than you have, or that you don't yet see what makes us worth more than the other quote?"* Buyers answer this readily because it feels like being understood rather than being probed, and their choice collapses six possible meanings into one.

**The isolate.** Establish whether this is the only thing. *"Setting that aside for a second — if we had a good answer on it, is there anything else that would stop you moving forward this quarter?"* This is the single highest-value sentence in the lesson. If the answer is yes-there's-more, you have just saved yourself from solving the decoy. If the answer is no, you have converted a vague concern into a closing condition, and you should confirm it: *"So if we solve the integration question, we're go."*

**The origin.** Find out where the belief came from. *"Can I ask what's driving that — did something like this go badly before, or has someone on the team seen us somewhere?"* Origin tells you whether you are facing a misunderstanding (correctable), a scar (needs evidence and empathy), or a competitor's talk track (needs a different approach entirely).

**The weigh.** Force an explicit comparison against the pain you already established. *"That's fair, and we don't do it. How does it stack against the 300k of under-billing — is it a deal-breaker or an irritation?"* Notice this only works if the earlier discovery produced something to weigh against. Objection handling is downstream of discovery in every direction.

Use one or two. Three diagnostic questions in a row starts to feel like avoidance, and the buyer will say "you're not answering the question."

## Keeping the deal on merit

"On merit" means the decision rests on whether your product solves an expensive problem better than the alternatives, at a price that is defensible. Every objection response either strengthens that footing or erodes it.

Three responses erode it. **Discounting reflexively** converts a value question into a price negotiation you did not plan and moves the entire deal onto commercial ground where the buyer has more practice than you. **Over-promising** — "that's on the roadmap for Q3" when you have no such commitment — buys a week and loses the renewal. **Volume** — answering a thirty-second concern for four minutes — reads as anxiety and tells the buyer they have found something soft.

Four responses strengthen it.

**Return to their own numbers.** The strongest objection response is usually a sentence the buyer said earlier. "You told me the drift cost 300k last year. This is a 90k subscription." Their arithmetic, not yours.

**Substitute proof for assertion.** Skepticism is answered by evidence, ranked roughly: a pilot on their data > a reference call with a comparable customer in their sector > a documented case with numbers > a contractual commitment (SLA, ramp, opt-out) > your assurance. Move down that list only when the rung above is impossible.

**Re-weight rather than rebut.** Against a genuine drawback, do not argue the drawback away; put it on the scale next to what you uniquely do. Buyers make trade-offs constantly and are perfectly capable of accepting one — but only if you name it honestly first.

**Trade, don't concede.** When an objection legitimately warrants a commercial move, that move must be paid for. Not "I'll ask for 10%," but "if the issue is that this year's budget is short, there's a version where we ramp — and I'd need something in return." You are not doing the pricing negotiation here; you are refusing to hand it over for free. The mechanics of anchors and concession ladders are the next lesson's subject, and the specific non-price levers you can trade are the one after. What matters right now is the reflex: **an objection is never, by itself, a reason to reduce your price.**

## A response decision table

| You diagnosed | Do this | Not this |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Indifference | Go back to implication and consequence | Any objection response at all |
| Misunderstanding | Correct in one sentence, then ask where it came from | A five-minute clarification |
| Skepticism | Offer the highest rung of proof you can execute | Assurance and enthusiasm |
| Drawback | Name it plainly, then weigh it | Fudging, or "it's on the roadmap" |
| Proxy | Arm the champion, or get in the room | Rebutting an absent person |
| Institutional / procurement | Navigate the process; hold price until you know the rules | Pre-emptive discounting |
| Stall | Surface the content, invite the no | Cheerful follow-up sequences |
| Condition | Confirm, date it, exit gracefully | Persuasion |
| Late-stage risk objection | Ask what changed; look for the unmet stakeholder | Technical answers |

## Objections about your company rather than your product

A category that catches technical representatives off guard, because it cannot be answered with product knowledge. *"You're a lot smaller than the incumbent." "What happens to us if you get acquired?" "We'd be one of your first customers in this sector."* These are risk objections about the vendor, and they are entirely rational — the buyer is being asked to bet a business process on a company.

Diagnose them the same way. The gap type is nearly always skepticism, so the response is proof rather than reassurance: named customers of comparable size, retention numbers, a support model with an SLA behind it, financial backing you are permitted to describe, contractual protections such as escrow or transition assistance. What does not work is denial ("we're very stable!") or turning it into a virtue speech about agility.

Two moves help specifically. **Concede the frame and reprice the risk:** *"You're right that we're a fifth of their size. The question I'd ask is what the exposure actually is — the data's exportable, the term's twenty-four months, and here are three customers your size who made the same bet three years ago."* And **offer the structural answer**: risk objections are frequently solved with terms rather than words — a shorter initial term, a defined pilot with an exit, a phased rollout. Note that these are commercial structures, so you plan them like any other concession rather than blurting them in the moment.

## Pre-empting: raise it before they do

If an objection appears in the majority of your deals, it will appear in this one, and there is a well-established advantage to raising it yourself. A concern you introduce is a concern you frame; a concern they introduce is a concern you defend. Raising it first also transfers credibility, because a seller who volunteers a weakness is read as trustworthy on everything else.

The pattern is three sentences: name it, size it honestly, and move to the weigh. *"Before you find it yourself — our mobile app is thinner than theirs. If your field team lives on phones, that matters, and I'd tell you so. For an office-based support team it has never come up. Which are you?"*

Pre-empt selectively. Two per deal is candor; five is a confession, and it suggests you do not believe in what you are selling. Choose the ones that are certain to surface and that you can weigh favorably.

## Keep an objection log

Objection handling improves through record-keeping more reliably than through practice, because the same six objections make up most of your losses and you will not remember which responses actually worked.

Keep four columns: the objection verbatim, your diagnosis, what you said, and what happened next. Review it monthly. Three things fall out of it within a quarter. You discover the objection you are worst at, which is usually the one you answer fastest. You discover which objections are systematically arriving *late*, which is a sign your earlier discovery is skipping something. And you build a bank of responses in your own voice, which is far more usable under pressure than anything from a training deck.

Cross-reference the log against your losses. If a particular objection precedes half your closed-lost deals, it is not an objection-handling problem — it is a targeting problem or a product gap, and the useful action is to tell someone rather than to get better at deflecting it.

## Worked examples

**A proxy objection at the business-case stage.**

> **Champion:** I like it. But our CFO is going to say a two-year payback is too slow.
>
> **You:** That's useful — is that a rule, or her instinct? *(Origin: is this policy or preference?)*
>
> **Champion:** She's said "under a year" out loud in the last two budget meetings.
>
> **You:** Then let's not send her a two-year case. Two questions. First, when she says a year, does she mean cash payback or does she count the avoided headcount? Because those are very different numbers here. Second — if the case landed under twelve months on her definition, is there anything else in the way? *(Split, then isolate.)*
>
> **Champion:** She means cash out the door versus cash saved. And no, if the number works she'll approve it.
>
> **You:** Then I'll rebuild it on cash-out versus the under-billing recovery only, and leave the headcount out entirely — it's the weaker half of the argument anyway. Would you rather present that, or would it help if I walked her through where the numbers came from? *(Arms the champion, offers the room, no price move.)*

The price never came up, the objection was never rebutted, and the deal is now on merit — on whether the recovery number survives a CFO's scrutiny.

**A late-stage risk objection dressed as functionality.**

> **Buyer (three days before signature):** We've been going back and forth on the mobile experience. I'm not sure it's there yet.
>
> **You:** Can I ask a slightly awkward question? Mobile hasn't come up once in eleven weeks. What's changed? *(Late objections are about risk. Ask directly.)*
>
> **Buyer:** ...our regional director sat in on the demo recording. He wasn't impressed.
>
> **You:** That's the more useful problem. Has he seen what the field team actually does in it, or just the recording? And what's his exposure if this goes badly — is he carrying the number this affects? *(Whose objection, and what is really at stake.)*
>
> **Buyer:** He owns the field team's targets. And no, just the recording.
>
> **You:** Then I'd rather not answer the mobile question at all this week. I'd rather get thirty minutes with him and two of his reps, on their phones, on their data. If it's genuinely not good enough for them, that's something you should know before you sign, and I'd say so. Can you get me that? *(Proof at the highest rung, willingness to lose, deal stays on merit.)*

**A procurement price demand.**

> **Procurement:** We'll need to see a 15% reduction to proceed. That's standard for a new vendor.
>
> **You:** Understood — is that a target you've been given, or a threshold you have to clear to get it approved? *(Split: aspiration or rule. The answers lead very different places.)*
>
> **Procurement:** It's what we've achieved with comparable vendors.
>
> **You:** Then let me be straight about how we work, so we don't waste rounds. Our price moves for scope and commitment, not for asking — so if 15% matters to you, the productive conversation is about what changes alongside it. Before that, is price the only open item on your side, or is there anything in the terms you'd also need moved? *(Refuses the free concession, signals a trade is available, and isolates.)*

Nothing was conceded, nothing was refused, and you now know whether price is the last item or the first of nine. That question alone will change what you do in the next lesson.

## Practice

Work in pairs with an observer if you have three. Around two hours.

1. **Build a diagnosis bank.** Take the five objections you hear most in your own market. For each, write out every distinct thing it could mean — aim for at least four — and next to each meaning, the response that fits it. You will discover that some meanings share a response and some are opposites.

2. **One question only.** Live drill, sixty seconds per round. The buyer delivers an objection with a secret intended meaning written on a card. The seller may ask exactly **one** diagnostic question, then must state which of the four gap types they think they are facing. Reveal the card. Track your hit rate over ten rounds; below 60% means your questions are too open.

3. **Isolate, every time.** Ten more rounds. The seller must use the isolate move — "if we solved that, is there anything else?" — in every round, and the buyer must be instructed in half the rounds to have a second hidden objection. Count how many second objections the seller surfaced.

4. **Drawback honesty.** The buyer asks for a capability the seller's product genuinely lacks. The seller must say plainly that it does not exist, must not mention a roadmap, and must then weigh the gap against a pain established earlier in the scenario. Run it twice and compare how the two versions felt from the buyer's chair.

5. **The late reversal.** The buyer plays an enthusiastic champion who, four days before signature, raises a small technical concern. The seller's only permitted opening is a question about what changed. See how many exchanges it takes to reach the real stakeholder. Debrief: what would have happened if the seller had answered technically?

6. **Hold the line.** Procurement demands a flat 12% "to proceed." The seller must respond without conceding anything, without refusing outright, and without proposing a specific trade — just establishing that price moves only alongside something else, and finding out whether price is the only open item. Write down the exact sentence you used; you will reuse it in the next two lessons.
