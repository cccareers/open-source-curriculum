## Description

Every course you have taken so far in this pathway has looked at security from the inside of the defended perimeter. This one asks you to stand briefly on the other side of it — not to attack anything, but to understand how an authorized, contracted, supervised security test discovers what a defender missed, and how the report that comes out of it should be read and acted on. That change of viewpoint is the point. A support technician who has never seen how a weakness is found and evidenced will accept a bad report and reject a good one.

You will work as an assistant, which is exactly what the job title means at this stage. A supervisor owns the engagement, signs for it, defines its boundaries, and answers for it. Your accountability is narrower and entirely concrete: confirm that written authorization exists before anything happens, stay inside the scope you were given, produce evidence somebody else can reproduce, stop at a boundary instead of stepping over it, and write findings a defender can act on. Every hands-on activity in this course happens inside an instructor-provided, isolated lab range against systems that were built to be broken, with written lab authorization already in place. Nothing here is to be pointed at a system you do not own or have not been authorized in writing to test.

Most of your time will go to the parts of this work that are unglamorous and decisive: the authorization paperwork, the discipline of validating a scanner's claims before repeating them, the evidence standard that makes a finding believable, and the translation of a finding into a failed control and a prioritized fix. Offense is the smallest section of this course by design. The written report is the deliverable, and by the end you will produce one.

## Objectives

- Read and write the authorization and scope documents that make a security test lawful and bounded
- Describe each phase of a supervised penetration test and what the tester is accountable for in it
- Run an authorized vulnerability scan in a lab environment and separate true findings from false positives
- Identify common web application vulnerability classes and describe the evidence that confirms each one
- Translate a test finding into a control weakness and a prioritized remediation recommendation
