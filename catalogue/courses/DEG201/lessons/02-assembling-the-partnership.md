## From referring to building

When your board refers a participant to an existing apprenticeship, someone else has already done the hard part: an employer agreed to hire and train, an education partner agreed to deliver instruction, and a sponsor took responsibility for the program. When your board decides to build a degree-linked apprenticeship, you are the one who has to get those parties into the same room and keep them there.

A degree-linked apprenticeship has three core partners, plus your board as convener. If you cannot name a specific organization and a specific person for each role, you do not have a partnership yet. You have an idea.

## The core roles

| Role | What they bring | What they commit to | Typical California examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Employer | Paid jobs, worksites, journey-level mentors | Hiring apprentices, supervising on-the-job learning, paying wages on a progressive scale | Hospital system, county agency, manufacturer, school district |
| Education partner | Courses, faculty, credit, a degree or certificate | Delivering related instruction, aligning courses to the work, awarding credit through its own approval process | Community college; adult school for earlier or non-credit pieces |
| Sponsor | Legal responsibility for the registered program | Writing and holding the apprenticeship standards, registering apprentices, tracking progress | An employer, a group of employers, a joint labor-management committee, or an intermediary; sometimes the college itself |
| Board (convener) | Labor market data, employer relationships, participant pipeline, some funding | Convening, staff time, recruiting and supporting WIOA-eligible apprentices, connecting funding | Your local workforce development board and its AJCCs |

Two points trip up new builders:

- **The sponsor is a distinct role, even when one organization fills two seats.** An employer can sponsor its own program, and some colleges sponsor programs. What matters is that someone is clearly accountable for the standards and for registration with the Division of Apprenticeship Standards (DAS). Registration mechanics are covered in APP 107; here you only need to know that this role must be filled.
- **The education partner owns the credit.** Your board cannot promise a participant that apprenticeship work will count toward a degree. Only the college, through its own faculty and curriculum process, can decide that.

## Supporting roles worth naming

Not every build needs these, but missing one can stall a project:

- **Labor partner**, where the occupation is represented or a joint apprenticeship committee already exists.
- **Adult school or other K-12 adult education provider**, for pre-apprenticeship, basic skills, or English language preparation that feeds the program.
- **Intermediary**, an organization that handles sponsor paperwork and coordination for several small employers.
- **Industry association**, to recruit additional employers once the first cohort works.
- **Other AJCC partners**, such as vocational rehabilitation or adult education under WIOA Title II, for participant referrals and supportive services.

## Worked example: Valley Regional Workforce Board

Valley Regional Workforce Board (fictional) hears from Mesa Valley Health (fictional), a three-clinic network, that it cannot keep medical assistants. Mesa Valley wants a path that keeps people for five years. The board's business services lead, Dana, sketches a partner map:

| Role | Organization | Contact | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Employer | Mesa Valley Health | HR director | Committed to a first cohort of six |
| Education partner | Granite Peak Community College (fictional) | Dean of health sciences; MA program faculty lead | Interested; needs curriculum committee buy-in |
| Sponsor | Not yet decided | — | Options: Mesa Valley sponsors itself, or a regional health-care intermediary sponsors |
| Board | Valley Regional WDB | Dana (business services); AJCC career specialist | Committed to recruitment and WIOA eligibility screening |
| Supporting | Valley Adult School (fictional) | Program coordinator | Possible pre-apprenticeship feeder |

The open sponsor seat is the real finding. Dana's next step is not to schedule a kickoff; it is to resolve who will hold the standards.

## Running the first convening

A half-day first meeting usually goes better when each partner leaves with one specific task. A simple agenda:

1. The employer describes the job, the hiring need, and what "fully trained" looks like.
2. The college describes which existing courses and degrees are closest, and what its approval process requires.
3. The sponsor candidate (or the group) discusses who will hold the standards.
4. The board describes recruitment, eligibility, and funding it can bring to the table.
5. Agree on owners and dates for the next step: usually the work-process crosswalk, which is the next lesson.

**Discussion prompt:** In your region, which of the four roles would be hardest to fill for a degree-linked program? Why, and who might fill it?

## Red flags in a partner map

- One person is listed for every role.
- The college contact is enthusiastic but has no authority over curriculum.
- The employer wants trained workers but has not agreed to pay apprentice wages.
- No one has agreed to be the sponsor.
- The board is listed as "lead" for tasks it cannot legally or practically perform, such as awarding credit.

## Practice

Build a partner map for one occupation in your region where you believe a degree-linked apprenticeship could work.

1. Copy the five-row table from the worked example.
2. Fill in a real employer type, education partner type, and sponsor option for your region. Use organization types rather than names if you are not ready to name them.
3. For each row, write what that partner must commit to, in one sentence.
4. Mark each row as "Committed," "Interested," or "Not yet identified."
5. Circle the single gap that would stop the project, and write the first action you would take on Monday to close it.
