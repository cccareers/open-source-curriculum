## The thirty days that decide the outcome

The most dangerous stretch of this program is the two weeks after it ends. The structure that got you sending — the lab block, the instructor, the cohort in the room — disappears on Friday, and the pipeline you spent eight weeks building goes quiet inside of ten days if nothing replaces it. Demo Day generated your warmest leads, and warm leads have a short shelf life.

A transition plan is the replacement structure. It is not a list of intentions; it is a dated, sequenced set of actions with milestones you can pass or fail. Thirty days is the right horizon because it is long enough to contain a full application-and-follow-up cycle and short enough that you will still be looking at the plan in week three.

Three tracks run in parallel across those thirty days, and they are not equally weighted.

- **Application follow-up** — moving live apprenticeship applications forward, and the follow-ups that keep them from stalling.
- **Network follow-up** — converting Demo Day and program contacts into scheduled conversations before they cool.
- **Skill maintenance** — enough deliberate practice that you interview well in week four and are ready on day one of an apprenticeship.

If you have to trade, trade against skill maintenance, not against follow-up. A perfectly maintained skill set attached to a dead pipeline gets no offers.

## Sequencing the three tracks

The sequencing rule is **freshness first**: act on the contacts whose memory of you is warmest, soonest. That is why network follow-up leads the plan even though applications feel more urgent.

A workable default shape, which you will adapt to your own live pipeline:

| Days | Application follow-up | Network follow-up | Skill maintenance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1-3 | Submit every application you have evidence for, portfolio URL included | Thank-you and next-step message to every Demo Day contact within 48 hours | Rest, then set the weekly practice block on the calendar |
| 4-10 | First follow-up on anything submitted 5+ days ago | Hold the first 2-3 scheduled conversations; ask each for one introduction | 2 sessions on the capstone stack, small and finishable |
| 11-20 | Second follow-up; open 3-5 new applications from partner and market lists | Re-engage nurture contacts with a specific update, not a check-in | 1 build session per week plus 1 interview-practice session |
| 21-30 | Escalate stalled applications through a network contact rather than the portal | Monthly touch on everyone still warm; add 10 new contacts | Ship one small public artifact; post it |

Two things make this plan real rather than decorative. First, **every row is on a calendar with a specific weekday and time**, not "sometime in week two." Second, **every action lives in your CRM as a dated next action**, exactly as it did during the program. The CRM is the only part of the program's structure that survives Friday, which is why the next lesson section is about making sure it is genuinely yours.

## Milestones that are actually testable

A milestone that cannot fail is not a milestone. "Keep networking" fails this test; "eight scheduled conversations held by day 30" passes it. Write three to five, each with a number, a date, and a source of truth.

Realistic targets for a participant leaving this program with a working pipeline:

- **By day 3:** every Demo Day contact logged in the CRM with a sent follow-up. Source of truth: CRM contact count versus your sign-in sheet.
- **By day 10:** at least 5 live apprenticeship applications submitted with portfolio evidence attached. Source of truth: your application tracker.
- **By day 15:** at least 4 conversations held with network contacts since Demo Day. Source of truth: CRM logged activities.
- **By day 30:** at least 2 applications advanced past first contact — a screen, an interview, or a formal next step. Source of truth: pipeline stage.
- **By day 30:** one new public artifact shipped and posted. Source of truth: your feed.

Calibrate against your own eight weeks rather than copying these numbers. If you averaged three conversations a week under supervision, do not write a plan requiring six a week without it. A plan you abandon in week two is worse than a modest plan you finish, because abandoning it teaches you that plans do not work.

Build in one recovery mechanism: a fixed weekly review, thirty minutes, same time every week. Read your five numbers, mark milestones hit or missed, and reschedule what slipped. Slippage is normal. Silent slippage is what kills the plan.

## Your network is only yours if you can export it

Everything you built over eight weeks — a few hundred contacts, the notes from every conversation, your published posts — lives in systems you do not own. Cohort tooling gets deprovisioned. Accounts expire. Platforms change their terms. The question to answer this week, while you still have access, is: **if every program account were switched off tomorrow, what would I still have?**

Answer it by exporting, verifying, and storing three things.

**1. CRM data.** Export contacts, companies, activity history, and notes to CSV. Most tools put this under a settings or data-export menu; if yours only exports contacts, export the activity log separately, and if it exports neither, copy the working view to a spreadsheet by hand. Confirm the exported row count matches the record count in the tool before you close it. Then open the CSV and check that the columns you actually rely on survived — name, company, role, email, stage, tier, employer-partner flag, last activity date, next action, and your notes. Notes are the field most often dropped by an export and the field you cannot reconstruct.

**2. LinkedIn connections and content.** LinkedIn provides a full data archive under its settings — request it, wait for the email, and download it. The archive contains your connections list with names, companies, and connection dates, your messages, and your posts. Two things to know: the connections export does **not** include the personal email addresses of most contacts, and it is a snapshot, not a live sync. That is precisely why you also keep the CRM copy, where you control the fields. Anyone who matters and exists only on LinkedIn should have a CRM record with a second way to reach them.

**3. Published content and artifacts.** Your posts, your portfolio, your capstone, your playbook. Keep the source, not just the published link. Push code to a personal account you control, save post text into your own document store, and export or re-host anything that lives on a program-provided site or domain. If the portfolio URL you said on stage points at program hosting, get a copy under your own name and know how to redirect.

Storage is part of ownership. One folder, in personal cloud storage on a personal account, with a dated subfolder for this export and a second copy somewhere else. An export sitting only on a lab machine is not a retained asset.

## Verifying, not assuming

Exporting is the easy half. Verification is what turns "I clicked export" into a network you can actually use in six months.

Run this check with the file open in front of you, not from memory:

- Open each export and read ten random rows. Do they contain the fields you need, populated, and readable?
- Pick your five most valuable contacts. For each, confirm you have a name, a company, a way to reach them that does not require the program's tooling, and enough note context to write a credible message a month from now.
- Confirm the personal accounts holding all of this are personal — not a cohort email, not a shared login. Reset any recovery address still pointing at a program account.
- Confirm your portfolio and playbook URLs load in a private browser window with no session.
- Write the date of the export on the folder, and set a calendar reminder to re-export in ninety days.

A contact you cannot reach, cannot place, and cannot remember is not a contact. The point of this work is that on day 45, when a role opens at a company you talked to in week 3, you can find the conversation, recall the detail, and send a message that sounds like it comes from someone who was paying attention.

## Practice

Work on your real accounts and your real pipeline. Both deliverables are due on file this week.

1. **Build the thirty-day plan.** Fill the three-track table with your own dated actions, anchored to the calendar dates after your last program day. Every entry names a day and a time.
2. **Write your milestones.** Three to five, each with a number, a date, and a named source of truth. Beside each, write the number you actually averaged during the program that justifies the target.
3. **Load the plan into the CRM.** Every day-1-through-10 action becomes a dated next action on a real record. Confirm nothing in the plan exists only on paper.
4. **Schedule the weekly review.** Thirty minutes, recurring, same weekday and time, for the next six weeks.
5. **Export the CRM.** Contacts plus activity history plus notes. Verify the row count and check that all ten required columns survived.
6. **Request and download the LinkedIn archive.** Confirm your connections list, messages, and posts are in it. Start the request early in the lab — delivery takes time.
7. **Retain your content.** Collect portfolio, capstone source, playbook, and post text under a personal account, and confirm every published URL loads in a private window.
8. **Run the verification checklist.** Ten random rows per export, five key contacts fully reachable without program tooling, personal accounts confirmed, export folder dated, re-export reminder set.
9. **File it.** Submit the transition plan to your instructor and record the date and location of your exports at the top of the plan.
