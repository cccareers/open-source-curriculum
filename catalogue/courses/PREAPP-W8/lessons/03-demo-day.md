## Goal

Present your capstone at Demo Day, live, in seven to ten minutes, to a room holding CCC staff, regional employer partners, and network contacts you invited yourself — and leave that room with your portfolio and personal playbook in the hands of at least one person who can move you toward an apprenticeship.

This is the program's culminating deliverable. It is graded on whether a stranger in the audience finishes your talk able to state what problem you solved, what you decided, what evidence backs it, and what you want from them.

## Requirements

**The presentation.**

1. Runs between 7:00 and 10:00 minutes, timed from your first word.
2. Follows the problem / approach / evidence / ask spine, in that order, with each section identifiable to a listener who has never met you.
3. Shows at least one live or recorded artifact from the capstone itself — the thing running, the dashboard, the deployed page, the working script.
4. States at least one checkable quantitative result drawn from your own eight weeks of data, with its denominator ("19% of 214 contacts", not "19%").
5. Ends with one specific, low-friction ask, named aloud, aimed at a stated group in the room.
6. Closes on a slide carrying your name, your LinkedIn URL, and the ask, left on screen through questions.

**The portfolio.**

7. A portfolio of your best artifacts is live and reachable at a single URL you can say out loud. This is the Outreach Portfolio you built in Week 7, at the same link your application cites; on stage you *show* only the few pieces your talk relies on. Include only artifacts that evidence a skill you claim on stage or on your resume — three strong pieces beat nine mixed ones.
8. Every artifact carries one line of context: what it is, what problem it solved, and what your specific contribution was.
9. A **personal playbook** is included as one of the artifacts: the repeatable process you actually used, written so someone else could run it. Outreach cadence, pipeline stages, how you research a contact, how you decide the next action, what you do when a reply goes cold. This is the artifact that proves the results were a process and not luck.

**The room.**

10. You invited at least one network contact of your own — someone you met through outreach or an informational interview during the program, not assigned to you.
11. You are present for the full session and you ask at least one substantive question of another presenter.

## Constraints

- **Seven to ten minutes, hard.** Going long is a failure of preparation and the timekeeper will cut you at 10:00.
- **No new content.** Everything you present must come from work already completed in weeks 1 through 7. Demo Day is not a build day.
- **Assume the network fails.** Anything requiring internet access at speaking time must have an offline fallback ready before you walk in.
- **Verifiable claims only.** Every number on a slide must be traceable to your CRM, your repo, your analytics, or your lab notes. If an employer partner asked you to show the source, you could, within a minute.
- **Mixed-audience language.** No program jargon, no unexplained internal vocabulary, no acronyms you have not defined.
- **Your own work, clearly bounded.** Where the capstone was collaborative or built on a template, say what part was yours. Overclaiming in front of employer partners is the one unrecoverable mistake available on this day.
- **Slides are optional; the artifact and the numbers are not.**

## Definition of done

You are done when all of the following are true, verified by your instructor at the close of the session:

- [ ] The presentation was delivered live, in front of the Demo Day audience, within the time window.
- [ ] All four spine sections were present and a staff observer could name each one from your talk alone.
- [ ] The artifact was shown and it worked, from the live version or the fallback.
- [ ] At least one quantified, sourced result was stated with its denominator.
- [ ] An explicit ask was made to a named group in the room.
- [ ] The portfolio URL was shown on screen and resolves for anyone in the audience.
- [ ] The personal playbook is published in the portfolio and describes a process a peer could follow.
- [ ] At least one self-invited network contact attended.
- [ ] You captured names and contact details for everyone who approached you, and every one of them is in your CRM with a dated next action before you leave the building.
- [ ] You asked at least one question of another presenter.

The last two items are not administrative. Demo Day generates the warmest leads you will have all year, and a lead that never gets logged is a lead that dies over the weekend.

## Hints

- **Rehearse the first sixty seconds until it is automatic.** Nerves peak in the opening and drop once you are moving. A memorized opening carries you past the peak.
- **Cut approach before you cut evidence.** When the timer says you are long, the fix is fewer decisions explained, not fewer numbers shown.
- **Say the number twice.** Once when you show it, once in your closing sentence. People remember the repeated figure, and it is what they will quote to a hiring manager later.
- **Make the ask small enough to say yes to standing up.** "A twenty-minute conversation about how your team onboards apprentices" gets accepted; "an opportunity at your company" does not.
- **Bring something physical.** A one-page leave-behind with your name, the headline result, the portfolio URL, and a QR code costs nothing and outlives the room.
- **Curate the portfolio down, not up.** An artifact you would not want an employer partner to open should not be in there at all. The weakest piece sets the perceived level of the whole set.
- **Write the playbook the way you would explain it to next cohort's week-1 participant.** Concrete steps, real cadences, actual message structures. Abstract advice reads as a process you never ran.
- **Have one follow-up sentence ready per audience group** so a hallway conversation after your talk starts somewhere better than "thanks for coming."
- **When a question stumps you, say what you would do to find out.** Employer partners are evaluating how you handle not knowing, and a clean "I have not tested that — here is how I would" scores better than a guess.
