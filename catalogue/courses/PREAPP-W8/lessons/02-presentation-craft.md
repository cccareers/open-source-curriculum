## What a results presentation is

A results presentation is not a demo, a status update, or a thank-you speech. It is a short, evidence-backed argument that you did something real and can do it again. Seven to ten minutes is the whole budget, and the room is mixed: CCC staff who watched you all eight weeks, employer partners who met you once or never, and network contacts you invited yourself. Three audiences, one talk, one shot.

That constraint drives every decision in this lab. At a comfortable speaking pace of about 130 to 150 words a minute, plan on roughly nine hundred to twelve hundred words of actual speech. That fills about six to eight minutes, and the rest of the window goes to transitions, a live artifact, pauses, and nerves. You cannot cover everything you did in eight weeks. You are choosing.

The selection rule is simple: **keep what an employer partner could act on.** Staff already know your story, so telling it again spends time on the audience least able to hire you. The partner in row three is deciding whether to ask for your resume. Everything in the talk should push that decision.

## The spine: problem, approach, evidence, ask

Every strong version of this talk has the same four-part spine, in this order.

**Problem.** One specific problem, named in the first thirty seconds, stated in the audience's language rather than the program's. Not "my capstone was about job search automation" but "I was sending forty outreach messages a week and getting three replies, and I could not tell which forty were worth sending." A problem that a working professional recognizes buys you the rest of the talk.

**Approach.** What you actually did, at the level of decisions and not steps. The room does not need your setup instructions; it needs to hear you choose. "I segmented the list by employer-partner status first, because warm channels reply at a rate that tells you whether the message or the targeting is broken." Approach is where technical judgment becomes visible, and it is the section employer partners listen to hardest.

**Evidence.** The proof. Numbers from your pipeline, before-and-after artifacts, a working piece of the capstone, a screenshot of the thing running. Evidence must be *checkable* — a claim someone could verify if they asked. "Reply rate went from 7% to 19% over three weeks, across 214 contacts" is checkable. "It worked much better" is not.

**Ask.** The single most-skipped section and the one that determines what happens after you sit down. You are not asking for a job on stage. You are asking for one specific, low-friction next step: a conversation, an introduction to a team lead, a look at your portfolio, feedback on a specific decision. State it in a sentence, name who it is aimed at, and stop talking.

A workable time budget for a nine-minute talk:

| Section | Minutes | What the audience should be able to repeat afterward |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Problem | 1.0 | What you were trying to fix and why it mattered |
| Approach | 2.5 | Two or three decisions you made and your reasoning |
| Evidence | 3.5 | One number and one artifact they saw with their own eyes |
| Ask | 0.5 | Exactly what you want them to do next |
| Buffer / questions | 1.5 | — |

Note that evidence gets the largest block. If your rehearsal keeps running long, the cut comes from approach, never from evidence.

## Building for a mixed audience

The three groups in the room need different things, and you serve all three with the same talk by layering rather than switching.

- **Staff** need to see the growth arc — they will hear it inside your approach section without you narrating it.
- **Employer partners** need transferable capability. Every claim should be phrased as something you can do again somewhere else, not something that happened once here.
- **Network contacts** need a way to help. That is what the ask is for.

Two habits keep the layering honest. First, translate program vocabulary. Nobody outside the cohort knows what "week 4 rotation" or "the milestone" means; say what it was. Second, name your tools and your numbers, because those are the shared language between you and the partners. Vague competence sounds like no competence.

## Slides and the live artifact

Slides are a backdrop, not a script. For a nine-minute talk, six to eight slides is the ceiling: a title, one per spine section, one or two evidence slides, and a closing slide that holds your ask, your name, and your LinkedIn URL so it is on screen while people are deciding whether to approach you.

Rules that survive contact with a real room:

1. One idea per slide. If it needs a paragraph, it belongs in your mouth, not on the wall.
2. Numbers get their own slide, large enough to read from the back row.
3. Any live artifact gets a recorded fallback. Live demos fail on unfamiliar networks; a thirty-second screen recording that always plays is not cheating, it is preparation.
4. No slide you have not spoken over at least twice in rehearsal.

## Rehearsal in three passes

Rehearsal is not re-reading. Do three distinct passes, each with a different job.

**Pass one — timed and alone.** Stand up, run it start to finish with a visible timer, no stopping to fix anything. Write your total time and the two places you stalled. Most first passes run twelve to fourteen minutes; that is normal and it means you are still cutting.

**Pass two — recorded.** Record video or audio and watch it back once. You are looking for four specific things: filler words, sentences that trail off without landing, slides you talked past, and the moment your energy drops. Fix the top two only.

**Pass three — in front of peers.** Full delivery, on your feet, with the real slides and the real artifact, followed by structured feedback. This is the pass that counts, and the rest of this lab is about running it well.

## Structured peer feedback

Generic praise is the default failure mode of peer review, and it is worthless. "That was really good, you seemed confident" gives the presenter nothing to change before Demo Day. Structured feedback fixes this by fixing the criteria in advance so reviewers are answering questions rather than reacting.

Use these five criteria. Every reviewer scores every criterion and writes one specific observation for each.

| Criterion | The question the reviewer answers | Score |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Problem clarity | Could you restate the problem in one sentence after the first minute? | 1-4 |
| Decision visibility | Name two decisions the presenter made and why. | 1-4 |
| Evidence strength | Which claim was checkable? Which was not? | 1-4 |
| Ask specificity | What exactly is the presenter asking you to do? | 1-4 |
| Delivery and time | Total time, plus the one moment attention dropped. | 1-4 |

Scores start the conversation; the written observation is the deliverable. A usable observation has three parts — **where, what, and what instead.** For example: "At the evidence slide (where), you said the reply rate improved a lot but never gave the number (what); say '7% to 19% across 214 contacts' and leave the slide up for three seconds (what instead)." That is actionable. "Be more confident" is not, because there is no action in it.

Two protocol rules make the round work. **The presenter does not defend.** Take the note, write it down, ask only clarifying questions. Explaining why the reviewer misunderstood is how you find out on Demo Day that the room misunderstands too. And **reviewers point at the talk, not the person** — every note names a moment in the presentation, not a trait of the presenter.

Receiving is a skill of its own. You will collect ten to fifteen notes and you cannot act on all of them before Demo Day. Sort them: notes that two or more reviewers gave independently are signal and get fixed first; single notes about clarity of the problem or the ask get fixed next; everything about style, pacing, or preference goes in a parking list. Fixing three things well beats fixing twelve things halfway.

## Practice

Work on your real capstone presentation. Everything here feeds Demo Day tomorrow.

1. **Draft the spine.** On one page, write four short paragraphs — problem, approach, evidence, ask — before you open any slide software. Bring the page to lab; your instructor reads the spine, not the deck.
2. **Pick your evidence.** List every number and artifact you could show, then cut to one number and one artifact. Write next to each how someone could verify it. Anything unverifiable comes out.
3. **Build the deck.** Six to eight slides against the time budget above, with the closing slide carrying your ask and your LinkedIn URL. Record the fallback clip for any live artifact.
4. **Run pass one and pass two.** Timed solo run, then a recorded run. Log both times and the two fixes you made after watching yourself.
5. **Run pass three.** Present to a peer panel of at least three. Each reviewer completes the full five-criterion sheet with one where/what/what-instead observation per row.
6. **Review someone else.** Score and write observations for at least two peers. Reread your notes before handing them over and delete any that lack a specific moment or a specific alternative.
7. **Triage and revise.** Sort your received notes into repeated, single-but-structural, and parked. Fix the top three, then re-run the talk once against the timer and confirm you land between seven and ten minutes.
