## Goal

Produce a complete, defensible integrated channel plan for one real or realistic small business, and defend it in a short review.

The plan must show that you can do three things this course assessed separately: write on-brand marketing copy that follows copywriting best practices, choose the right KPIs for a stated goal and say how you will read them, and ship a plan that survives a privacy, ethics, and advertising-policy review. Everything else in the plan exists to hold those three together.

You are not being graded on how many channels you use, how clever the creative is, or how large the imagined budget is. You are being graded on whether a stranger reading the plan can tell **who it is for, what it says, what it costs, how you will know it worked, and why it is compliant** — and whether the answers hold together.

## The business you are planning for

Choose one:

- **Option A — a real small business you know.** Your employer, a family business, a local shop, a nonprofit. Best choice if you have access to real numbers.
- **Option B — one of the supplied briefs.** Use these exactly as written; do not add facts that make the problem easier.

**Brief 1: Northline Bookkeeping.** A three-person bookkeeping practice serving small contractors and trades businesses in one metro area. Average client is worth $3,200 a year in fees and stays 2.5 years. They currently get all clients by referral and have never marketed. Budget: $2,500 a month for six months. Goal: 12 new clients in six months.

**Brief 2: Half Step Coffee.** A single-location coffee roaster that sells bags of coffee in the shop and wants to grow online subscriptions. A subscription is $22 a month, gross margin 45%, and the average subscriber stays 7 months. They have 4,100 social followers and an email list of 900 collected at the counter. Budget: $1,800 a month for four months. Goal: 250 active subscriptions.

**Brief 3: Meridian Community Clinic.** A nonprofit clinic that wants uninsured adults in three ZIP codes to book free preventive screenings. No revenue per booking; the funder requires 900 screenings in the grant year and a cost per booked screening under $30. Budget: $2,200 a month. The audience includes people with limited English and limited home internet. Health information is involved.

If you choose Option A, write a "brief" for it in the same shape as the three above — audience, economics, current assets, budget, goal — before you plan anything. If you cannot fill it in, you do not yet know enough to plan.

## Requirements

Your deliverable is a single document with these seven sections, in this order.

### 1. Situation and audience (about half a page)

State the business, the audience in specific terms, and the single measurable goal with a deadline. Name the realistic alternative the audience has today, including doing nothing.

### 2. Positioning and messaging (about half a page)

A completed positioning statement using the template from the brand lesson, a four-row voice table (we are / we are not / in practice), and a messaging set: one primary message and two or three supporting messages, each with its proof point. Every claim you make anywhere in the plan must trace back to this section.

### 3. Channel plan (one page, table plus commentary)

A table with one row per channel you will use. Columns:

```text
Channel | Journey stage | Owned / earned / paid | What it does |
Monthly cost | What success looks like
```

Then commentary that answers: why this mix and not another, what you deliberately excluded and why, and what changes if the first month underperforms. Include at least one owned, one earned, and one paid element.

### 4. Copy (about one page)

Three finished assets, written to ship — not descriptions of assets:

- **A landing page block:** headline, 40 to 80 words of body, one call to action, plus the reassurance line under it.
- **One email from a lifecycle sequence,** including sender name, subject line, preheader, body under 150 words, and button copy. State which email in the sequence it is and what job it does.
- **One channel-native piece:** a social post, a search ad, or a short-form video script, with its call to action.

Under the three assets, add a short "why this copy" note: name three copywriting practices you applied, and one thing you cut and why.

### 5. Measurement plan (about half a page)

- One primary KPI with a numeric target, derived from the business goal.
- Three to five diagnostics and what each would tell you.
- The unit economics: what a customer or conversion is worth, and therefore the maximum acceptable cost per acquisition. Show the arithmetic.
- Which attribution model you will report on and why, in one sentence.
- One metric a stakeholder will ask for that you will refuse to judge the plan by, and why.
- The link-tagging convention you will use, with one worked example URL.

### 6. Compliance review (about half a page)

Run your own plan against the pre-flight checklist from the privacy lesson. Produce a table: checklist item, current state, action required. Then write, in full, the two or three assets the review forces you to produce — for example the consent line under your signup form, the disclosure sentence on a paid endorsement, or the corrected wording of a claim you cannot substantiate.

Then answer one question directly: **name one thing in your own plan that you would have shipped without thinking about it, that this review caught.** A compliance section that finds nothing wrong is not a compliance section.

### 7. Risks and what you would do next (a short list)

Three risks, each with the signal that would tell you it is happening and the action you would take.

## Constraints

- **Budget is fixed.** Every dollar in the channel table must be accounted for, and the total must not exceed the brief's budget. If you want more, say what you would cut to fund it.
- **Every claim needs a source.** For each factual claim in your copy, add a bracketed note naming the evidence — a real figure, a stated assumption, or "needs substantiation before publishing." Unsupported claims left unmarked are the fastest way to fail this project.
- **No inventing capability.** If the business has one part-time person, a plan requiring daily video production is not a plan. State the labor each channel needs.
- **Vendor-neutral.** Name channel types, not specific products, unless a specific product is genuinely required. You are being assessed on reasoning, not tool familiarity.
- **Stay at survey depth.** Do not produce a keyword research spreadsheet, a full ad-account structure, or an analytics implementation spec. Those belong to later courses. Say what you would do and why; do not do it here.
- **No real personal data.** If you use a real business, do not include any real customer's name, email, address, or other personal information anywhere in your deliverable.
- **Length target: 4 to 6 pages.** Longer is not better. A plan that cannot be read in fifteen minutes will not be read.

## Definition of done

Your plan is finished when all of the following are true. Check them yourself before submitting.

```text
[ ] All seven sections are present, in order
[ ] The goal is a number with a deadline, and the primary KPI measures it
[ ] The positioning statement names a specific audience, a real
    alternative, and a checkable reason to believe
[ ] A competitor could NOT put their name on your headline unchanged
[ ] Every channel row states stage, media type, cost, and success signal
[ ] Channel costs sum to the budget or less, and the sum is shown
[ ] All three copy assets are finished and shippable, each with one
    primary call to action
[ ] Every factual claim carries a bracketed evidence note
[ ] The maximum acceptable cost per acquisition is derived, with the
    arithmetic visible
[ ] The attribution model is named
[ ] The compliance table covers every checklist item and names at least
    one real problem found in your own plan
[ ] The corrected compliance assets are written out in full
[ ] Three risks, each with a signal and an action
[ ] No real personal data appears anywhere
```

## How your work will be read

A reviewer will assess the plan against three things, in this order.

**Does the copy work?** Section 4 is read against the copywriting practices from the brand lesson: benefit before feature, written to one person, specific and numeric where evidence allows, no unfalsifiable adjectives, front-loaded headline, one primary call to action, and a message aimed at the stage the channel serves. A reviewer will try to put a competitor's name on your headline. If it still reads true, section 2 did not do its job.

**Is the measurement sound?** Section 5 is read for whether the primary KPI actually measures the stated goal, whether the target is derived rather than asserted, whether the cost-per-acquisition ceiling follows from real unit economics, and whether the diagnostics would let you locate a failure rather than merely notice one. A plan whose only measure is impressions or followers does not pass this section, regardless of how good the copy is.

**Would this survive review?** Section 6 is read for whether you found real problems in your own plan and wrote real corrections, not whether your plan happened to be clean. The commonest weak submission asserts that everything is compliant and produces no corrected asset.

The other four sections are read for coherence: whether the audience in section 1 is the audience the copy addresses, whether the channels in section 3 are the ones the KPIs measure, and whether the budget adds up.

## Suggested working order

The project is scoped at roughly four hours. A workable split:

```text
~45 min   Pick the business. Write the brief. Do the unit economics
          (section 5's arithmetic) before anything else.
~45 min   Audience notes, positioning statement, voice table,
          messaging set.                                (section 2)
~40 min   Channel table and commentary, costed to budget. (section 3)
~60 min   Write the three copy assets and the "why this copy" note,
          then do one review pass with adjectives deleted. (section 4)
~30 min   Finish the measurement plan and tagging convention.
~30 min   Compliance table, corrected assets, risks, and a final
          pass over the definition-of-done checklist.
```

If you run short, cut length, not sections. A three-page plan with all seven sections is a pass; a six-page plan missing the compliance review is not.

## Hints

**Start with section 5, not section 1.** Working out what a customer is worth, and therefore what you can afford to pay for one, constrains the entire channel plan. Doing it last means rewriting everything.

**Do the arithmetic early and let it disqualify things.** Half Step Coffee: a $22 subscription at 45% margin lasting 7 months is about $69 of gross margin per subscriber, so a $40 cost per subscription is viable and a $90 one is not. Now look at what $1,800 a month can actually buy at plausible acquisition costs, and at the 250-subscription goal. If the numbers do not reach the goal, **say so in the plan.** Identifying that a stated goal is not reachable with the stated budget, and proposing the closest achievable version, is a stronger answer than a plan with optimistic numbers.

**Use the existing assets first.** Every brief contains something free and warm: 900 counter-collected emails, an existing referral flow, a funder's community relationships. Plans that ignore what already exists and start with paid ads are the most common weak submission.

**Pick fewer channels.** Three channels executed well beats seven listed. The commentary about what you excluded is worth more than another row.

**Match the message to the stage.** If a channel is doing awareness work, do not write decision-stage copy for it. This is where most copy sections lose points.

**Meridian is the hard one on purpose.** Health information, a vulnerable audience, limited connectivity, language access, and platform special-category targeting restrictions all collide. If you choose it, the compliance section is the centre of the plan, not an appendix — and note that "run ads targeted at people who seem to have a health condition" is not available to you.

**Write the compliance section honestly.** The instruction to name something your own review caught is not a trick. Reviewers can tell the difference between a plan that was audited and a plan that was decorated with an audit.

**Read your own headline out loud.** If it sounds like something any company in the category could say, it is not positioning — it is filler, and section 2 will not save it.
