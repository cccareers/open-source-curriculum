## What the term "digital marketing" actually covers

Digital marketing is the practice of reaching people, earning their attention, and moving them toward a decision using channels that are measurable and addressable. That definition is doing more work than it looks like. "Measurable" means that, unlike a billboard, you can usually count how many people saw a thing and how many acted on it. "Addressable" means you can often choose who sees it rather than broadcasting to everyone.

Practitioners tend to organize the field in three different ways at once, and confusing the three is the most common source of muddled marketing plans:

- By **channel** — where the message travels: search, social, email, display, video, affiliate, SMS, and so on.
- By **journey stage** — where the person is in their decision: they have never heard of you, they are comparing options, they are ready to buy, they already bought.
- By **media type** — who controls the surface: you own it, someone gave it to you, or you paid for it.

A plan that names only channels ("we will do SEO and Instagram") is not a plan. It has not said who the message is for, what they already know, or why that channel is the right vehicle. This lesson gives you the three vocabularies and the discipline to use them together. Every later course in this pathway goes deep on one column of this map; this lesson is the map itself.

## The customer journey

The customer journey is a model of how a stranger becomes a customer. Different teams use different labels, but a five-stage version covers most cases:

| Stage | What the person is doing | What they need from you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Awareness | Has a problem, does not know you exist | To encounter you at all, in a context that makes sense |
| Consideration | Knows you exist, is comparing options | Evidence: specifics, proof, comparison, price clarity |
| Decision | Ready to act, looking for a reason not to | Low friction, reassurance, a clear next step |
| Retention | Has bought, is deciding whether to stay | Onboarding, usefulness, being remembered without being pestered |
| Advocacy | Is satisfied, may tell others | An easy way to refer, review, or share |

Two cautions about the model. First, it is a **model, not a sequence a real person follows**. People loop, skip, and re-enter. Someone can go from a friend's recommendation (advocacy, someone else's) straight to decision in ninety seconds. Second, the journey has different lengths in different businesses. A $12 phone case has a journey measured in minutes. A $40,000 piece of manufacturing equipment has a journey measured in months and involves five people who each need a different message.

The practical use of the model is **diagnosis**. When a business says "our marketing isn't working," the first question is: at which stage does it stop working? Those failures look identical from the revenue line and require completely different fixes.

Consider a small online retailer with these monthly numbers:

```text
Sessions on site           40,000
Product pages viewed       12,000   (30% of sessions)
Add-to-cart                 1,200   (10% of product page views)
Checkout started              600   (50% of add-to-cart)
Orders completed              150   (25% of checkout starts)
```

Awareness is fine — 40,000 people arrive. The catastrophic step is checkout: three out of four people who start checkout abandon it. Spending more money on awareness ads would pour more people into a leaking bucket. The right move is to investigate checkout: unexpected shipping cost, a forced account creation, a broken payment method on mobile. Journey-stage thinking told you where to look.

## The channel map

Here are the channels a generalist is expected to recognize, what each is genuinely good at, and where it usually fails.

**Organic search (SEO).** Pages you publish that rank in search results without paying for placement. Excellent at capturing people who are already looking for a solution — high intent, no per-click cost. Slow: a new page can take weeks or months to earn its position, and rankings are not owned, only borrowed. Covered in depth in the SEO course later in this pathway.

**Paid search (PPC).** Ads on results pages, usually billed per click. Fast, precisely targeted by the words people type, and turns off the moment you stop paying. Best when you know a term converts and want volume now. Expensive in competitive categories.

**Organic social.** Posts on channels where an audience follows you. Good for personality, community, and staying present in an existing audience's feed. Weak for reach in isolation — most channels show a post to a small fraction of followers unless it earns engagement.

**Paid social.** Ads placed into social feeds, targeted by interest, behavior, or audience list. Good at creating awareness among people who were not looking for you. Weaker intent than search: you interrupted them.

**Email.** Messages to a list of people who gave you permission. The highest-leverage retention channel, because the list is yours and delivery does not depend on an auction or a ranking algorithm. Useless for awareness — you cannot email strangers legally or effectively.

**Content marketing.** Articles, guides, videos, tools, and other useful material. Not a distribution channel so much as the fuel other channels burn: content is what SEO ranks, what social shares, and what email links to.

**Display and video advertising.** Banner and video placements across sites and platforms. Broad reach, cheap impressions, low intent. Mostly an awareness and re-engagement tool.

**Affiliate and partner.** Third parties promote you in exchange for a share of the result. Pay-for-performance, so the risk is low, but you control the message less.

**SMS and push.** Short, immediate messages to people who opted in. Very high open rates, very low tolerance for abuse. Reserve for genuinely time-sensitive or transactional messages.

Now overlay the journey. No channel belongs to exactly one stage, but each has a natural center of gravity:

| Stage | Channels that usually carry it |
| --- | --- |
| Awareness | Paid social, display and video, organic social, top-of-funnel content, broad organic search |
| Consideration | Organic search on comparison and how-to terms, content, retargeted display, email nurture |
| Decision | Paid search on brand and high-intent terms, site content, review and marketplace presence |
| Retention | Email, SMS, organic social, in-product messaging |
| Advocacy | Email, organic social, referral and affiliate programs |

Notice that search appears at three stages. That is because search intent varies enormously by query. Someone typing "why does my laptop overheat" is at awareness. "Best cooling pad for gaming laptop" is consideration. "Cooling pad ABC-500 free shipping" is decision. Same channel, three different jobs, three different pages required.

### Pull channels and push channels

There is one more distinction worth carrying, because it explains most of the difference in how channels perform.

A **pull channel** reaches someone who is already looking. Search is the archetype: the person typed the query. Marketplace listings, review sites, and app-store search behave the same way. Pull traffic converts at high rates because you did not have to create the demand — you only had to be there when it surfaced. The ceiling is the constraint: you cannot get more people searching for something than are searching for it.

A **push channel** puts a message in front of someone who was doing something else. Paid social, display, video, and most email fall here. Intent is lower, so conversion rates are lower — often by an order of magnitude — but the ceiling is enormous, because you are choosing the audience rather than waiting for it.

This is why comparing a search campaign's 6% conversion rate to a social campaign's 0.4% and concluding "social does not work" is a category error. They are doing different jobs. The right comparison is cost per outcome, and even that has to account for the fact that push channels often create demand that later shows up as pull. A brand that runs video ads for three months and then sees more people searching its name is not experiencing a coincidence.

The practical consequence for planning: **harvest existing demand first, then create new demand.** A business that has not yet captured the people already searching for what it sells is not ready to spend money interrupting people who are not.

## Owned, earned, and paid media

The third vocabulary is about **control**, and it is what separates a durable plan from a fragile one.

**Owned media** is any surface you control: your website, your blog, your email list, your app, your customer database. You decide what goes on it, nobody bills you per view, and it does not disappear when a budget ends. It is slow to build and its reach is limited to people who already found you.

**Earned media** is attention other people give you for free: a press mention, an organic search ranking, a share, a review, a mention on someone's podcast. It is the most credible kind, because a third party is vouching for you. You cannot buy it directly and cannot schedule it.

**Paid media** is placement you rent: ads of every kind, sponsorships, paid influencer posts. It is fast, controllable, and dial-adjustable — and it stops the day the invoice stops.

Note that organic search rankings sit in an interesting place: the page is owned, the ranking is earned. That is exactly why SEO is both durable and outside your control.

The three types are strongest together, and the pattern is usually: **paid buys the audience, earned proves the credibility, owned keeps the relationship.** A launch that is 100% paid gets a spike and nothing after it. A launch that is 100% owned reaches only the people you already have. A plan with no earned component has no third-party proof at the exact moment a buyer is looking for it.

### Choosing between them

Four questions decide the mix:

1. **How fast do you need results?** Paid delivers this week. Owned and earned deliver in months.
2. **What happens when the money stops?** Paid goes to zero immediately. Owned persists.
3. **How much control do you need over the message?** Total on owned, high on paid, none on earned.
4. **What does one more unit cost?** Paid scales linearly — twice the reach costs roughly twice as much. Owned and earned have high setup cost and near-zero marginal cost, which is why they compound.

### Worked example: two plans for the same $6,000

A regional HVAC repair company has $6,000 a month and wants more service calls. Two plans:

**Plan A — all paid search.** $6,000 buys roughly 1,000 clicks at a $6 cost per click in this category. At a 6% conversion rate to a booked call, that is 60 calls. Predictable, starts within a day. In month 12, having spent $72,000, they still need $6,000 next month to get 60 calls.

**Plan B — split.** $3,500 to paid search (about 35 calls), $1,500 to a content and local-SEO effort (six service-area pages and a maintenance guide), $1,000 to photography and review generation. Months 1 through 3 deliver fewer calls than Plan A — roughly 35 to 45. By month 9 the service-area pages rank and produce 25 to 30 calls a month at no incremental cost, and the reviews raise the paid conversion rate, so total calls exceed Plan A's 60 while paid spend stays at $3,500.

Plan B is better *for a business that will still exist in nine months*. If the company has three months of cash left, Plan A is correct and Plan B is a fantasy. There is no channel mix that is right in the abstract — the right mix is a function of the goal, the timeline, and the tolerance for risk.

## Building a channel plan in five steps

The map is only useful if it produces a plan. Five steps, in this order, and the order is the point — each step constrains the next.

**Step 1 — State the goal as a number with a deadline.** "More sales" is not a goal. "150 booked service calls a month by the end of Q3" is. Without a number you cannot size a budget, and without a deadline you cannot judge whether a slow channel is acceptable.

**Step 2 — Describe the audience specifically enough to picture one person.** Who has the problem, what triggers them to act, what they would search or scroll past, what they are comparing you to. If the description fits everyone, it will guide nothing.

**Step 3 — Work out the unit economics.** What is one customer worth, and therefore what can you afford to pay for one? A business with $60 of margin per customer and one with $2,000 have almost nothing in common in terms of viable channels, even in the same industry. Do this before you look at any channel, because it disqualifies most of them for you.

**Step 4 — Map the journey and identify the weak stage.** Where do people currently fall out? Fixing the weakest stage usually beats adding a channel — and if you do not know where the weak stage is, that is the first thing to go find out.

**Step 5 — Choose the smallest channel set that can plausibly hit the number**, and assign each channel a stage, a job, a budget, and a success signal. Then write down what you deliberately excluded and why. The exclusions are the part reviewers should read most closely, because a plan that includes everything has decided nothing.

### Sizing a channel from the goal

The arithmetic is worth practising because it turns wishful plans into honest ones. Work backwards from the goal through your known or assumed conversion rates.

```text
Goal:                       150 booked service calls / month
Site-to-booking rate:       6%    (measured, last 3 months)
Sessions required:          150 / 0.06        = 2,500
Current organic sessions:   900
Gap:                        1,600 sessions
Paid cost per click:        $6.00
Paid budget required:       1,600 x $6.00     = $9,600
Cost per booking:           $9,600 / 96       = $100
Margin per service call:    $180
```

The plan is viable but thin — $100 to earn $180 leaves little room for error, and any drop in the booking rate makes it a loss. That single table is more useful than a page of channel descriptions, because it tells you exactly which lever matters most. Raising the site-to-booking rate from 6% to 8% cuts the required sessions to 1,875, which cuts the paid budget by roughly $4,300 a month. Improving the page is worth more than buying more traffic, and you can see it in the numbers before spending anything.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Naming channels before naming the audience and the goal.** "We need TikTok" is a conclusion presented as a strategy.
- **Judging every channel by the same metric.** Holding an awareness channel to a last-click sales number will always make it look like a failure. You will see why in the measurement lesson.
- **Confusing activity with a plan.** Publishing three posts a week is a habit, not a strategy, unless it is attached to a stage and an outcome.
- **Building only rented ground.** A business whose entire audience lives on one platform's algorithm has one bad policy change between it and zero.
