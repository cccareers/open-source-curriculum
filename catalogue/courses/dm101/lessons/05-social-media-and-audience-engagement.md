## What an organic social presence is for

Organic social media is the set of accounts a brand runs and posts to without paying for placement. It is worth being precise about what it does well, because it is the channel most often run on vague hope.

Organic social is good at three things: **staying present** with people who already know you, **showing personality and proof** in a way a product page cannot, and **being the place people come to talk to you** — including when they are unhappy. It is weak at pure reach; most platforms show an unpromoted post to a small fraction of the accounts that follow you, and a brand-new account reaches almost nobody until it earns engagement.

That combination points to a clear job for organic social in a marketing plan: it is a **relationship and reputation channel**, not an acquisition machine. This lesson covers planning that presence and handling the conversation it produces. Paid social campaigns are a separate discipline covered later in this pathway.

## Planning the presence

A presence plan answers five questions before a single post is written.

### 1. Who is the audience, and where are they?

You do not need to be everywhere. You need to be where your audience already spends attention, in a format they already consume. A B2B software audience behaves differently from a local restaurant's neighborhood audience, and a plan that ignores that produces the classic failure: five half-abandoned accounts instead of one good one.

A useful constraint for a small team: **one primary channel, one secondary at most.** A consistently good presence on one platform beats a thin presence on four.

### 2. What is the account actually for?

Give each account one primary job, chosen from a short list:

| Job | What success looks like | Typical content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Awareness | Reach and shares beyond followers | Short, shareable, entertaining or surprising |
| Community | Comments, replies, returning participants | Questions, behind-the-scenes, member spotlights |
| Support | Fast resolution, fewer repeat questions | Answers, status updates, how-tos |
| Proof | Saves, referral clicks | Customer results, before-and-after, demos |

Most accounts try to do all four and end up doing none. Choose one primary and one secondary.

### 3. What do you post?

A **content pillar** is a recurring theme the account returns to. Three to five pillars give an editorial identity without inventing a topic every week. A neighborhood bike shop might use:

```text
Pillar 1  Repair explainers        "Why your brakes squeal in the rain"
Pillar 2  Local rides and routes   Route of the month, trail conditions
Pillar 3  Shop life                Builds in progress, staff, new stock
Pillar 4  Customer bikes           Photos of finished repairs, with permission
```

Note the last two words. Posting a customer's photo, bike, or name requires their permission — a practice point that becomes a legal one when personal data is involved, which the privacy lesson covers.

### 4. How often, and who does it?

Cadence must be honest about capacity. Three posts a week sustained for a year beats fourteen a week for a month followed by silence. Write the cadence into a simple calendar with a named owner per slot:

```text
Mon   Repair explainer          (Dana, drafted Fri)
Wed   Local ride or trail note  (Marcus)
Fri   Shop life photo           (whoever is closing)
Daily Reply pass, 11:00 and 16:00  (Dana)
```

The reply pass is the part people leave out, and it is the part that actually builds the audience.

### 5. How will you know it worked?

Match the metric to the job. An awareness account is judged on reach and shares; a community account on replies, saves, and returning commenters; a support account on response time and resolution. Judging a community account by follower count is how good accounts get shut down.

## Engaging: responding to the conversation

Engagement is not "posting more." It is what happens in the replies, and it is where the competency in this lesson lives. A workable approach has three parts: a triage rule, a response pattern, and an escalation path.

### Triage: sort before you type

Sort every incoming message into one of five buckets:

| Bucket | Example | Response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Question | "Do you service e-bikes?" | Answer plainly, fast |
| Praise | "Best tune-up I've had" | Short, specific thanks |
| Complaint — legitimate | "Third time this cable slipped" | Acknowledge, own it, move to private |
| Complaint — mistaken | "You charged me twice" (they didn't) | Correct gently, with evidence, in private |
| Bait or abuse | Trolling, slurs, spam | Do not engage; hide, block, or report |

Most damage comes from applying the wrong bucket's response — arguing publicly with a legitimate complaint, or apologizing profusely to a troll.

### The response pattern for a complaint

Four moves, in order:

1. **Acknowledge, fast.** Speed matters more than polish; a public complaint sitting unanswered for two days is itself a message.
2. **Own the part that is yours.** "That shouldn't have happened" is worth more than a paragraph of context. Do not blame the customer, the supplier, or the weather in public.
3. **Move the details to a private channel.** Direct message, email, or phone — anywhere the customer's order number and personal details are not on a public timeline.
4. **Close the loop publicly, briefly.** Once resolved, a short public note tells the hundred silent readers that problems get fixed here.

A worked example. A customer posts publicly:

> Picked up my bike from Ridgeline yesterday, paid $180 for a tune-up, and the rear derailleur is skipping worse than when I dropped it off. Not impressed.

A poor response:

> Hi! We're sorry you feel that way. Our mechanics are ASE-certified professionals and we service over 40 bikes a week with a 4.8 star average. Sometimes cables stretch after a service, that's normal. Please call the shop.

It disputes the customer's experience, defends the brand's credentials to an audience that did not ask, blames a normal phenomenon, and puts the work back on the customer.

A good response:

> That's not the result we want you to leave with, and a skipping derailleur after a tune-up is on us. I'm sending you a message now — bring it back any time this week and we'll re-index it and re-check the cable at no charge, or refund the tune-up if you'd rather. — Dana, service manager

It acknowledges within the first clause, owns it, offers two concrete remedies, moves to private, and is signed by a person. Note it makes no claim about *why* it happened, because nobody has looked at the bike yet.

### Escalation

Decide in advance, in writing, what a front-line responder handles alone and what goes up the chain. A minimal policy:

```text
Handle directly:   product questions, hours, pricing, routine complaints
                   under $250, praise
Escalate to owner: refund requests over $250, anything repeated by the
                   same customer three times, safety claims, injury claims
Escalate + do not
respond publicly:  legal threats, regulatory complaints, media inquiries,
                   anything involving a minor's data
Never:             argue with abuse; hide or report and move on
```

Two rules that prevent most self-inflicted crises: **never delete a legitimate complaint** — deletion is what turns one unhappy customer into a screenshot campaign — and **never respond angry**; if a draft reply feels satisfying to write, it is the wrong reply.

## Monitoring brand sentiment

Sentiment is the overall emotional tilt of what people say about you. Monitoring it is not a tooling exercise at this level; it is knowing what signals exist and reading them honestly.

**Signals to watch**

- **Direct mentions**, tagged and untagged. Untagged mentions and common misspellings of your name matter, because people complain *about* you more often than *to* you.
- **Ratio of positive to negative to neutral** over a rolling window. The trend line is more informative than any single number.
- **Reaction mix**, where the platform exposes it. A post with high reach and a spike in angry reactions is a warning even if the comment count looks healthy.
- **Comment themes.** Ten complaints about one thing is a product signal; ten complaints about ten things is noise.
- **Review sites and marketplace ratings**, which often move before social does.
- **Response and resolution time**, your own behavior, which drives sentiment as much as anything you post.
- **Share of voice** — how much of the category conversation mentions you versus competitors.

**Reading them.** Establish a baseline before you interpret anything: if 6% of monthly mentions are negative in a normal month, a week at 9% is noise and a week at 30% is an event. Volume and sentiment must be read together — a small negative share of a huge volume spike can still be a larger absolute problem than a normal week.

**A worked read.** A meal-kit brand's monthly mention log:

```text
Month     Mentions   Positive   Neutral   Negative   Negative %
Jan          420        180        210        30         7.1%
Feb          445        190        220        35         7.9%
Mar          410        170        205        35         8.5%
Apr        1,180        260        640       280        23.7%
```

April's mention volume nearly tripled and negative share tripled with it — that is an event, not drift. The right first move is not a statement; it is reading the April negatives for a theme. If 240 of the 280 say "my box arrived warm," this is a cold-chain failure in a specific region, and the response is operational plus a specific, honest public acknowledgment. If the 280 are scattered across unrelated complaints, the spike is probably attention from an unrelated viral post and calls for a different response entirely.

Note what a sentiment number cannot do: it cannot tell you *why*. Always read a sample of the actual comments before acting on a percentage.

### A monitoring routine you can actually keep

Sentiment monitoring fails when it is a project instead of a habit. A routine a one-person marketing team can sustain:

```text
Twice daily   Check mentions, comments, and direct messages on the
              primary channel. Triage and reply. Target: reply to
              anything actionable within 4 working hours.
Weekly        Skim review sites and the marketplace listing. Log any
              new theme in one shared document.
Monthly       Record mention volume, the positive/neutral/negative
              split, average first-response time, and the top three
              comment themes. Compare to the previous three months.
Quarterly     Read 30 comments end to end, unfiltered. Numbers drift
              away from reality if nobody ever reads the raw text.
```

Two supporting decisions make the routine work. First, **set a response-time target and publish it internally**, because "we reply fast" without a number degrades quietly. Second, **write the escalation policy down before you need it**; a person deciding at 9pm whether a legal threat needs the owner will decide badly.

## Practice

**Part 1 — Presence plan.** For a small business of your choice, write a one-page organic social plan: the primary channel and why, the account's primary job, three to five content pillars with one example post title each, a weekly cadence with a named owner per slot, and the two or three metrics you will judge it by. Justify one platform you deliberately chose *not* to use.

**Part 2 — Reply drafting.** Write a response to each of the following. For each, name the triage bucket first, then the reply, then one sentence on whether it escalates and to whom.

1. "Ordered two weeks ago, no shipping update, no reply to my email. Is this company even real?"
2. "Just got my order — the note from your packing team made my day. Thank you!"
3. "You people charged my card twice." (Your records show one charge and one pending authorization that will drop off.)
4. "This product gave my kid a rash. Taking this to the health department."
5. A stream of profanity with no specific complaint.

**Part 3 — Sentiment read.** Using the four-month table in this lesson, write a short brief for the owner: what changed, what you would look at before saying anything publicly, what would have to be true for you to recommend a public statement, and which two ongoing signals you would add to the monthly report so this is caught in week one next time.
