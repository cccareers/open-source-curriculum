## Three layers of rules

Every decision you make about a customer sits on three layers:

1. **Federal law and regulation.** WIOA itself and the federal regulations, such as 20 CFR 677.150 for participant and exit definitions and 20 CFR 680.730 for OJT reimbursement.
2. **State direction.** California's Employment Development Department issues directives (numbered like WSD24-04) that tell local areas how to carry out the federal rules. This is the "state spine" every California board shares.
3. **Local board policy.** Where federal and state rules leave room, your local workforce board writes its own policies. Two boards in California can lawfully handle the same situation differently.

When a case does not fit the usual pattern, the first question is: **which layer decides this?** If it is a state rule, you follow it and cannot change it locally. If it is delegated, you look up your board's policy, and if the policy is silent, you escalate.

## Typical split

The table below shows common examples. It is a starting point, not a legal list. Confirm each one against the current directive and your board's policies.

| Decision | Usually governed by | Where to look |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who counts as a participant; when exit happens | Federal regulation | 20 CFR 677.150 and federal performance guidance |
| Which documents prove eligibility | State | Acceptable Documentation List in WSD24-04 |
| Priority-of-service order | State, with room for local priority groups | WSD24-06, WSD24-15, and your local plan |
| Security and confidentiality training | State | WSD24-12 |
| Which source documents validation accepts | State | WSD22-15 |
| Which training providers an ITA can pay | State list, local choices | ETPL directive WSD25-02 and local ITA policy |
| ITA dollar limits and duration | Local | Your board's ITA policy |
| Supportive service types and amounts | Local | Your board's supportive services policy |
| OJT reimbursement rate for a given employer | Federal and state limits, local decision | 20 CFR 680.730, state waiver guidance, your board's OJT policy |
| Self-sufficiency definition for employed Adults | Local | Your board's local plan or policy |
| Case note format and timeliness standard | Local | Your board's case management procedures |
| Follow-up contact schedule | Local, within federal requirements | Your board's follow-up policy |

Notice the pattern. The state usually controls **what counts** and **what proves it**. The local board usually controls **how much**, **how often**, and **in what format**.

## Worked example

Priya, a case manager at Valley Regional, has a customer who needs help paying for work boots and a bus pass to start a warehouse job.

- *Can the customer receive supportive services at all?* Federal rules allow supportive services for participants who need them to take part in services. **Federal layer.**
- *What documents show the need?* Check what the state requires and whether the local policy adds anything. **State and local.**
- *How much can she spend on boots?* Valley Regional's supportive services policy sets the limit. **Local layer.** Another board's limit may be different, and both can be correct.

Priya writes in the case note which policy she applied, by name and date. A monitor can follow the decision without asking her.

## When the policy is silent

Sometimes you look and find nothing. Do not invent a rule. Write down the question, the layers you checked, and send it to your supervisor. Silent policy is a finding waiting to happen, and your question may be what gets it written.

## Practice

1. Find your board's local policies. Write down where they live (shared drive, website, binder) and the date of the newest one.
2. Pick five decisions you make in a normal week. For each, label it federal, state, or local, and write the document that governs it.
3. Find one decision where you are not sure which layer controls it. Bring it to your next team meeting as a discussion question: "Who decides this, and where is it written?"
