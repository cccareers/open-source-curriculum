## The deck is not the presentation

You have a brief, a narrative, charts that argue, and a deck that survives being forwarded. None of it has been delivered yet, and delivery is where most of the value is won or thrown away.

Two reps can present the identical deck to the identical room. One gets a pilot commitment. The other gets "this was helpful, let us regroup after the summer." The difference is rarely charisma. It is a set of learnable behaviours: rehearsal that resembles the real thing, an opening that earns attention, the ability to take an interruption and hand it back into the thread, a plan for what to cut when the clock beats you, and an ask delivered as a request rather than a hope.

This lesson is about the version of your presentation that exists only in the room. It ends where a presentation should end: on a specific, agreed next step.

## Rehearsal that actually works

Most rehearsal is clicking through the deck silently on the morning of the meeting, which trains nothing. Rehearsal that works has four passes, and together they cost about ninety minutes for a meeting that matters.

**Pass 1 — the headline pass.** Read the headlines aloud in order, nothing else, and say the transition sentence between each pair. Three minutes. This tests the argument, not the performance, and it is where you discover that slides 7 and 8 are in the wrong order.

**Pass 2 — the no-slide pass.** Deliver the entire presentation with the screen off or the deck closed. This is the highest-value rehearsal in existence and the one reps skip. If you cannot tell the story without the deck, you do not know your story — you know your slides, and the room can tell the difference within two minutes. It also prepares you for the meeting where the projector fails, the screen share breaks, or the CFO says "just talk me through it," all of which happen.

**Pass 3 — full, out loud, timed.** Standing, at the pace you will actually speak, with a clock. Not in your head; in your head you are twice as fast as reality. Write the elapsed time down. Almost everyone runs 30–50% over on the first timed pass, and discovering that at rehearsal is free.

**Pass 4 — the hostile pass.** Get a colleague to interrupt you deliberately. Ask them to challenge a number, ask an off-topic question, raise price on slide four, and mention the competitor. You are not rehearsing answers so much as rehearsing the *return* — the move that brings you back to the thread. That mechanic is the second half of this lesson.

Two things not to do. **Do not memorize sentences.** Memorized prose is audible, it sounds like a script, and one interruption destroys it because you have no idea where to rejoin. Memorize the *sequence of claims*; improvise the words. The exception is the first sixty seconds and the ask, which you should be able to deliver word-perfect under pressure.

**Do not rehearse only the good version.** Rehearse the version where you have twenty minutes instead of forty-five. That is the collapse plan, and it comes later in this lesson.

## Presence

Presence is not personality. It is a small number of physical behaviours, each of which is trainable and each of which reads to a room as confidence.

**Pace.** Nervousness accelerates speech, and fast speech reads as anxious and is harder to follow. The correction is not to speak slowly throughout — it is to **stop**. A full stop after a claim, two seconds, before the next sentence. Silence after an important sentence is the strongest emphasis tool available, and it costs nothing. Reps find two seconds unbearable; the room finds it authoritative.

**Volume and pitch.** Project to the back of the room, not to the nearest person. Pitch rises with tension and drops with authority; the fix is breathing from lower down and pausing to breathe rather than pushing through on a shrinking lungful.

**Filler.** "Um," "so," "basically," "kind of," and the sentence-final upward inflection that turns statements into questions. You cannot eliminate filler by trying not to say it. You eliminate it by replacing it with silence — filler is what fills the gap while you compose the next sentence, and a pause fills the same gap better. Record yourself once and count. The number is always higher than you think, and awareness alone fixes half of it.

**Eyes.** Complete one thought per person. Not scanning, not the trick of looking at foreheads — actually finish a sentence to a single person, then move. In a six-person room that means everybody gets addressed several times, including the silent nineteen-year supervisor whom every rep instinctively avoids because he is not nodding.

**Body.** Feet planted, weight even, hands visible and free. Pacing burns your nerves and the room's attention. If you gesture, gesture from the elbow rather than fidgeting from the wrist. Do not hold a pen — you will click it.

**The screen.** You are the presentation; the slides are evidence. Do not read your slides aloud — the room reads faster than you speak, and reading to them is mildly insulting. Glance at the screen to point at something specific, then turn back. Never present with your back to the room.

**On video, three things change.** Look at the camera, not at the faces, when you are making the point that matters — eye contact on video is a camera behaviour. Be a little more animated than feels natural, because the medium flattens everything. And build in explicit checkpoints, because you have lost your ability to read the room. "Marcus, before I go on — does that match what you are seeing?" every few minutes replaces the feedback the room used to give you for free.

## The first ninety seconds

The room decides how much attention to give you in about a minute and a half, and most reps spend that minute on logistics and thanks.

What the opening has to do: earn permission for the next twenty minutes, set the shape of what is coming, and make the room a participant rather than an audience. What it must not do: introduce your company, apologize for anything, or tell the room how excited you are.

A structure that works in about seventy-five seconds:

**A sentence that raises a question.** *"Before I show you anything, I want to test one thing with you, because if I am wrong about it the rest of this meeting is not worth your forty-five minutes."*

**The frame.** What you will cover, how long, and where questions go. *"I have three things: what we found in your dispatch data, what I think it means, and what I would like to do about it in the next sixty days. Twenty minutes, and please interrupt — I would rather have the conversation than get through the slides."*

**The contract.** Confirm the outcome and the clock out loud. *"Marcus, I know you are with us until about twenty past, so the numbers come first. If by the end this looks worth testing, I will ask for a specific decision. If it does not, I would rather you tell me."*

That last move is worth more than it looks. Saying "if it does not, tell me" lowers the room's defences immediately, because it signals you are not going to spend forty-five minutes closing them. It also earns you the right to ask for a real answer later, which is exactly what the ask depends on.

**Do not open by thanking everyone for their time.** It is reflexive, it wastes your best fifteen seconds, and it starts the meeting from a position of gratitude for an audience rather than of having something worth their attention.

## Interruptions: four kinds and one mechanic

An interrupted presentation is a good presentation. Silence means politeness, distraction, or a room waiting for you to finish so they can leave. Interruptions are the room engaging with you.

The problem is not that they happen; it is that they derail. A rep gets a question on slide four, answers it thoroughly, gets a follow-up, answers that, and eighteen minutes later is somewhere in the appendix with the thread gone and no time for the ask.

The mechanic that prevents this has three moves, and it applies to every kind of interruption:

**Acknowledge. Handle. Return.**

**Acknowledge** — repeat or reframe the question in one sentence. This buys you three seconds to think, confirms you understood, and lets the rest of the room hear a question they may have missed. *"So the question is whether this works when there is no signal in the machine room."*

**Handle** — answer, defer, or convert. Which one depends on the type, below.

**Return** — and this is the move nobody practises — say a sentence that puts you back on the thread explicitly. *"That is the offline model. Coming back to where we were: your callbacks..."* Without a spoken return, both you and the room are left in an undefined place, and the next interruption arrives from that undefined place instead of from your argument.

Rehearse the return until it is automatic. Pass 4 of the rehearsal plan exists for exactly this.

Now the four types.

**1. The clarifying question.** *"Is that 19% of all calls, or 19% of repair calls?"* Straightforward, on-thread, answer it in one sentence and return. These are the good ones; a room asking clarifying questions is a room following the argument.

**2. The objection.** *"We tried a mobile app three years ago and the technicians would not use it."* This is not a derailment, it is the actual meeting. Do not defend and do not rush. Acknowledge it as legitimate, ask one question to understand it, answer briefly, and return. *"That is worth taking seriously — what was it about the app that they rejected? … Right, so if the issue was that it added data entry rather than removing it, that is the specific thing I want to show you on the next slide."* Two rules: never argue with the objection, and never spend more than ninety seconds on it in a presentation. A deep objection is an invitation to a separate conversation, and saying so is a stronger move than trying to close it on the spot.

**3. The tangent.** *"Does this integrate with our payroll system?"* Real question, wrong meeting. This is what the parking lot is for. *"Good question — let me put that on the list and come back to it at the end, because it deserves more than thirty seconds. Where we were..."* Write it visibly. Visible capture is the difference between deferring a question and dismissing one.

**4. The hijack.** Someone takes over — a long anecdote, a hobby-horse, a side argument between two attendees. The move is a polite, structural interruption: use their name, extract the useful part, and reassert the clock. *"Dana — that is the exact case I want to make sure we cover, and I want to keep us on time for Marcus. Can I get through the next two slides and then come straight back to it?"* Using someone's name and citing a third party's constraint lets you take the room back without it being a contest.

## The parking lot

Keep a visible list. A flip chart, a shared doc on the screen, or a page in your notebook that you turn toward the room when you write on it.

It does three things. It lets you defer without dismissing, which preserves the relationship with the person who asked. It creates a written record of what the customer cares about, which is genuinely valuable input to your next steps. And it produces a natural close: the last three minutes become "here are the four things we parked," which is a structure the room recognises as respectful.

The rule that makes it work: **every parked item gets an owner and a date.** A parking lot that empties into nothing teaches the room that "let me park that" means "let me avoid that," and you will only get away with it once.

## Objections that arrive mid-presentation

Four arrive so predictably that you should have a rehearsed opening move for each. All four follow acknowledge-handle-return, and all four end with a return to the thread rather than a resolution.

**Price, early.** *"What does this cost?"* on slide four. Do not deflect and do not answer fully. Give a real range, anchor it to what you are actually asking for, and return. *"For an operation your size, full deployment lands in the low-to-mid six figures annually. But what I am asking for today is a sixty-day pilot in one district, which is a much smaller number, and I would rather earn it before I quote it. Can I show you the finding first?"* Structuring the commercial terms — discount, term length, what you trade for what — is negotiation craft and belongs to se203; here you are only buying back the next ten minutes.

**The incumbent.** *"We already have a dispatch system."* Never attack it — someone in the room chose it. Locate the boundary of what it does. *"You do, and it does the scheduling part well. The gap I am pointing at is not scheduling, it is what the technician knows before the truck is loaded. Does your current system carry unit fault history to the phone?"*

**Timing.** *"This is probably a next-year conversation."* Answer with trajectory, not urgency. *"That is fair. The one thing I would put next to it is that your renewal rate has gone 91, 88, 84. A next-year decision means one more year on that line, and the pilot is designed to be small enough not to need next year's budget."*

**Credibility.** *"Have you done this in elevator specifically?"* Answer honestly and immediately. Any hedge here is fatal. *"Not in elevator. We have done it in three field-service operations of a similar shape, including one regulated one — that is the slope chart at the back. What I would say is that the pilot exists precisely because I do not want you to take my word for it."*

The shared pattern: short answer, honest content, and a return that puts the argument back in motion. None of these is closed in the room, and trying to close them is what turns a presentation into a defence.

## When the clock beats you

Meetings start late. Marcus is on a call. A fire drill takes eight minutes. Plan for it: build a **collapse plan** before every important presentation, and write it on the first page of your notes.

Decide in advance what the presentation is at three lengths.

**Full length (twenty-two minutes of substance).** The whole arc, every proof point, the appendix available.

**Half length (ten to twelve minutes).** Keep beat 1 compressed to a sentence, beat 3's stakes, beat 5's reframe and one proof, and the ask. Cut beat 4 to a single sentence and drop two of three proof points. What survives is the argument's skeleton.

**Five minutes.** The finding, the cost, the ask. Three slides. *"Your callbacks are a parts problem, not a tenure problem; it is costing you a million in visits and $613,000 of renewals; I want sixty days in Queens to prove it."*

Two rules about executing a collapse.

**Cut whole beats, never seconds per slide.** Speeding up is the instinct and it is wrong — a rushed full deck delivers nothing well and signals panic. A calm five-minute version delivers the argument.

**Never say "I will skip ahead."** Announcing a cut tells the room that what you are skipping was optional, and invites them to reassess what came before. Just present the shorter version as though it were the plan.

There is a corollary about Marcus specifically. If the economic buyer leaves at minute twenty, the collapse plan is not a contingency — it is the design. The five-minute version has to have been delivered *before* he leaves, whatever else happens.

## Closing on a next step

A presentation that ends with "so, any questions?" ends in a shrug. Everything you built is now dependent on somebody else in the room volunteering to advance the deal, which they will not do.

The close has four parts and takes about three minutes.

**Summarize in three sentences.** Not a recap of slides — the argument. *"Your callbacks track parts availability, not tenure. It is costing you roughly a million in return visits and it is showing up in renewals. And it is fixable inside this fiscal year without hiring."*

**Clear the parking lot.** Each item, an answer or an owner and a date.

**Make the ask, specifically, on a slide.** Written, precise, with a name, a scope, a date, and a measure. *"A sixty-day pilot in the Queens district: 38 technicians, about 1,900 units, starting the first week of October, measured on first-time fix rate and callback rate against your current baseline of 61% and 19%. Dana, that needs your operational sign-off. Marcus, it needs a line held in the September capital plan so we are not waiting until summer if it works."*

**Then stop talking.** This is the hard part. Ask, and be silent. The silence after an ask feels much longer to the person who asked than to anyone else, and reps break it by softening — "or if that is too much, we could just do another call" — which converts a real request into an optional one. Ask, and wait.

If the answer is not yes, get the next best thing, in this order: a smaller commitment, a date, or a named obstacle. *"What would have to be true for this to be a yes?"* is the most useful sentence in the close, because the answer is either a real obstacle you can work or the discovery that you are talking to the wrong room.

And write down what was agreed, out loud, before you leave. *"So: I send the pilot scope by Friday, Priya's team confirms the machine-room connectivity question by the 20th, and we meet the week of the 30th to sign off. Have I got that right?"* Then send exactly that in an email within two hours, because the version in writing is the version that exists.

## Practice

These exercises build toward lesson 8's recording, so do them with a camera or phone running wherever they call for delivery.

**Exercise 1 — the no-slide pass.** Deliver your Fairmount narrative from lesson 3 with no deck, out loud, recorded, from beat 1 to the ask. Watch it back and write down: total time, the number of fillers you counted, the longest single pause you took, and the point at which you first lost the thread. If you could not complete it without the deck, do it again before moving on.

**Exercise 2 — the opening, three ways.** Write and record your first ninety seconds three times: once as a question-raising opening, once as a direct-finding opening ("I want to start with something in your data that surprised me"), and once as a discovery playback opening. Deliver each to camera. Then argue in four sentences which one fits this specific room, given that one DC manager in it believes the problem is discipline.

**Exercise 3 — timed full pass.** Deliver the full presentation to camera at real pace, with a timer, no interruptions. Report the elapsed time against your fifty-minute budget from lesson 2. If you are over, list the specific beats or slides you cut, and re-run it.

**Exercise 4 — the hostile pass.** Have a colleague deliver these four interruptions at the points indicated, or record yourself handling each cold:

- On your second slide: *"Sorry — what does this actually cost?"*
- During your stakes beat: *"I think your accuracy number is wrong. We measure it at the order level, not the line level."*
- During your reframe: *"Does this talk to our transportation management system?"*
- During your proposal: *"We tried handhelds in 2019 and the associates hated them."*

For each, write which of the four types it is, then deliver an acknowledge-handle-return out loud and transcribe your return sentence. Grade yourself on one thing only: did you get back onto the thread in a single sentence?

**Exercise 5 — build the collapse plan.** Write the full, half-length, and five-minute versions of the Fairmount presentation as slide lists. Then deliver the five-minute version to camera. State which beats survive at each length and defend the one you were most reluctant to cut.

**Exercise 6 — the four objections.** Write your rehearsed opening move for each of price-early, incumbent, timing, and credibility, adapted to Fairmount. Each must be no more than four sentences and must end with a return to the thread. Deliver all four out loud, back to back, and note which one you are least convincing on.

**Exercise 7 — the close, and the silence.** Record your three-minute close: three-sentence summary, parking-lot clearance, the written ask, and then five full seconds of silence. Watch it back and check whether you actually stayed silent, or whether you softened the ask. Then write the confirmation email you would send within two hours, in under 120 words.

**Exercise 8 — self-review.** Watch your full timed pass with the sound off, then again with the picture off. From the silent viewing, note three things about your body and eyes. From the audio-only viewing, note your fillers, your pace, and whether the pauses fell after your important claims or in the middle of them. Write six specific, behavioural changes for the next attempt — not "be more confident," but "stop after the callback number and count two."
