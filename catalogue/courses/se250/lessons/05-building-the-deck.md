## The deck outlives the meeting

Everything so far has been preparation: a brief that says what the meeting must accomplish, a narrative that carries it, and visuals that prove it. This lesson is where you finally open the presentation software — and the reason it comes fifth is that learners who open the tool first reliably produce decoration instead of argument.

There is a second reason this lesson exists separately from delivery. **The deck has a life after you leave the room.** Dana forwards it to a board member. Priya sends it to her security lead. Marcus opens it three weeks later, on a phone, in an airport, ten minutes before the capital planning meeting where he will decide whether to defend your line item. In every one of those readings, you are absent. Your voice, your emphasis, your ability to answer a raised eyebrow — gone. What is left is a file.

Most decks fail that test badly, and they fail it in a specific way: they were built as a **prompt for the presenter** rather than a document for a reader. A slide reading "Dispatch · Mobile · Truck Stock · Contracts" is a fine set of cue cards for a rep who knows what to say. To Marcus in the airport it is four nouns.

The craft in this lesson is building one artifact that works in both situations. It is possible, it is not difficult, and it comes down to a small number of decisions applied consistently.

## Two documents, or one that does both?

The traditional answer to this problem is to build two artifacts: a sparse presented deck of images and a few words, and a dense written leave-behind. That is a genuinely good answer, and if your time allows it, take it.

Your time will not usually allow it. So the practical answer for most sales situations is **one deck built to the reader's standard, presented with discipline** — plus a short executive summary at the front for the person who will read three slides and stop.

"Built to the reader's standard" means every slide states its point in words on the slide. "Presented with discipline" means you do not read those words aloud; you say something adjacent while the room takes the point in visually. This is the crucial habit and it is the one reps get wrong in the opposite direction — they either strip the deck to imagery and leave the absent reader nothing, or they write full paragraphs and then recite them.

There is one situation where the two-artifact answer is worth the effort: a high-stakes executive readout where you are certain the deck will circulate. Then build the presented version to be sparse and beautiful, and send a written narrative — prose, not slides — afterward. Executives read prose faster than they read slides, and a two-page memo has stopped more competitive deals than any deck.

## Slide anatomy

Every content slide in a sales deck has the same four parts.

![The four parts of a content slide: assertion headline, single evidence body, source line, and quiet furniture](./img/slide-anatomy.png)

**The assertion headline.** A full sentence stating this slide's single point. Top of the slide, largest type on it, sentence case, one or two lines. Not a topic label. This is the same rule you applied to charts in lesson 4, generalized to every slide in the deck.

**The evidence body.** One thing: a chart, a diagram, a short table, a photograph, a quotation. Its only job is to make the headline visibly true. If the headline needs two kinds of evidence, that is two slides.

**The source line.** Small, bottom, for any slide carrying customer data or an external claim. Costs nothing, buys credibility, and pre-empts the question.

**The furniture.** Section marker, slide number, and — for anything that might circulate — a confidentiality mark. Small, consistent, at the edge. Logos on every slide are wasted space; nobody forgets whose deck they are reading.

What is deliberately absent from that list: bullet lists as the primary body. A bullet list is a claim that has been chopped into fragments and stripped of the connective words that made it an argument. Occasionally you genuinely have a set of parallel items — three markets, four integration points — and a short list is the honest form. That is rare, and it should look rare in your deck.

**The headline test.** Print or export the deck's headlines alone, in order, on one page. Read them consecutively. You should get a coherent, complete argument that a stranger could follow. This test is the single best diagnostic in this lesson because it fails loudly: a deck of topic labels produces "Introduction / Our Company / The Challenge / Our Solution / Architecture / Case Study / Next Steps," which is a table of contents, not an argument. A deck of assertions produces the spine you wrote in lesson 3.

## The build order

Six steps. The tool does not appear until step four.

**1. The brief.** Lesson 2. Already written.

**2. The spine.** Lesson 3. Six sentences.

**3. The storyboard, on paper.** One index card or one box on a sheet per slide. On each: the headline as a full sentence, and a rough sketch of the evidence. Nothing else — no fonts, no colours, no alignment. Ten to fifteen cards for a forty-five-minute meeting. Lay them on a table and read the headlines in order. Move them. Throw two away. This step takes twenty minutes and prevents the most expensive kind of rework, which is rearranging a deck you have already spent three hours styling.

**4. Grey boxes in the tool.** Build every slide with real headlines and placeholder rectangles where the evidence goes. No formatting. Now you can see pacing, count slides against your time budget, and discover that beat 3 is somehow six slides long.

**5. Evidence.** Build the charts from lesson 4, one per slide, each proving its headline.

**6. Craft pass.** Type, alignment, spacing, colour, contrast. Last. It takes an hour and it is enjoyable, which is exactly why it must not come first.

Reps resist step 3 hardest, because moving index cards does not feel like building a deck. But the cost of discovering a structural problem at step 3 is thirty seconds and the cost of discovering it at step 6 is your evening.

## Consistency without a design degree

You are not a designer and do not need to be. Almost everything that makes a deck look competent comes from **repetition**, which is a mechanical property, not an aesthetic one. A deck where every headline sits in the same place, in the same size, in the same colour reads as considered. A deck where headlines wander by fifteen pixels reads as rushed, and readers transfer that impression to the argument.

Every serious presentation tool provides the same three mechanisms under different names. Learn yours; the concepts transfer.

**Themes.** One place where fonts, the colour set, and default sizes are defined for the whole file. Set them once. Changing the accent colour should be one edit, not eighty.

**Layouts and masters.** Reusable slide skeletons — headline-and-chart, headline-and-two-panels, section divider, big-number. Build four or five and use them for everything. This is what puts the headline in the same place on every slide.

**Guides and alignment tools.** Rulers, snapping, and distribute commands. Nudging by eye is how misalignment enters a deck.

A few defaults that survive any tool:

- **Two type sizes and one weight change** carry almost all hierarchy. Headline large, body smaller, one bold for emphasis. A deck with six type sizes looks unplanned because it is.
- **One type family**, or two with an obvious job each. Use whatever your company's template specifies; consistency beats taste.
- **A generous outer margin** on every slide, never violated. Content that runs to the edge reads as crowded and gets cropped by some projectors.
- **Left-align body text.** Centred paragraphs are hard to read because the eye loses the line start.
- **Whitespace is not waste.** The instinct to fill the slide is the instinct that produces the seven-bullet slide.
- **One idea per slide, always.** More slides with less on each is better than fewer, denser slides — the slide count is not the cost, the reader's effort is.

Two traps worth naming. **Animation** should be used only to control the sequence of a genuinely complex build — revealing a chart in two stages so the room does not read ahead. Anything that flies, spins, or bounces converts attention from your argument to your transitions. And **embedded video or live product demos inside the deck** fail often enough, in enough rooms, that you should have a still-image fallback for every one of them.

## A slide rewrite, worked

Here is a real-shaped slide from the Bay Ridge deck, before and after.

**Before.** Headline: **"Platform Capabilities."** Body: seven bullets in twenty-point type, filling the slide edge to edge.

> - Intelligent dispatch with route optimization and skills-based assignment
> - Mobile technician application with offline capability
> - Comprehensive asset and unit history at your fingertips
> - Predictive parts recommendation engine
> - Truck stock and van inventory management
> - Contract lifecycle management with renewal alerting
> - Real-time dashboards and configurable reporting

Everything on it is true. As a presented slide it is a trap — the room reads seven bullets in eight seconds and stops listening while you narrate bullet one. As a document it is worse, because it makes seven equally weighted claims and Marcus in the airport has no idea which one addresses the problem he was told about. And it has no protagonist, no assertion, and no connection to the narrative it interrupts.

**After — three slides.** The seven capabilities become three slides, each with an assertion headline about Bay Ridge, and the full capability list moves to the appendix where Priya's team can have all of it.

*Slide A.* Headline: **"The technician sees the unit's full fault history before they leave the yard — with no signal in the machine room."** Body: a single screen image of the mobile job view, one callout arrow pointing at the last-repair panel, one small badge reading "works offline." Source line: none needed. Spoken over it, roughly fifteen seconds: what a technician at 14 Court Street actually sees at 7:40am.

*Slide B.* Headline: **"The system predicts the likely part from that history and loads it to the truck before the route starts."** Body: a four-step diagram — fault history → predicted part → truck stock check → route load — with the third step in accent because that is the step Bay Ridge does not have today.

*Slide C.* Headline: **"Comparable operators reach the mid-70s on first-time fix in year one."** Body: a slope chart, three named-by-industry operators, before and after, with the range annotated. Source line: *Aggregate from 3 field-service operators, 2,000–4,000 units, first 12 months post-deployment.*

Look at what changed. Three assertions instead of seven fragments. Each is a sentence about Bay Ridge's operation, not about your feature set. Each has exactly one piece of evidence. The headline test now yields a small argument by itself: *the technician sees the history → the system loads the part → operators like you get to the mid-70s.* And the slide that was doing the least work — the capability inventory — is exactly where it belongs, in the back, available to the one person who wants it.

The cost is two extra slides, and it is worth noting that this is the correct direction to spend slides. Three slides that each land beat one claim are faster in a room than one slide that lands none.

## The appendix, used properly

An appendix is not where content goes to be forgotten. It is a deliberate structure that lets the presented narrative stay short while the document stays complete.

Behind a clearly marked divider, put: the full capability matrix, the architecture and integration detail, the security posture summary, the implementation plan and staffing, the detailed pricing mechanics, additional customer references, and the source data behind every chart in the main body.

Three habits that make an appendix actually useful.

**Index it.** A contents slide immediately after the divider, listing what is back there. Otherwise it is twenty-two slides of unlabelled material and nobody scrolls.

**Build it for the question you expect.** When Priya asks about the offline sync model in minute nineteen, you want to reach one slide, not seventeen. Order the appendix by likely question, not by internal team.

**Never apologize for it.** "I have a lot more detail at the back" is fine. Flipping through the appendix while saying "I will not bore you with this" tells the room you included material you did not think was worth their time.

## Making it survive the forward

A concrete checklist for the absent reader, because this is the objective the lesson is named for.

**A summary at the front.** After the title slide, one slide that gives the whole argument: the situation, the finding, the cost, the proposal, the ask. Marcus in the airport reads that slide and stops, and he now has what he needs. Everyone else reads on. This single slide is the highest-leverage thing in the deck.

**Headlines that stand alone.** The headline test again. If the headlines alone tell the story, the forwarded deck works.

**Sources on every data slide.** A forwarded deck gets read by people who will ask "says who," and you will not be there.

**Export to a fixed format.** Send a PDF, not the editable file. It removes font substitution, layout drift, and the small risk of someone opening your speaker notes — which they will, and which are usually written in a register you did not intend a customer to read.

**Check the speaker notes anyway.** Reps have shipped notes reading "push hard here, Marcus is the blocker." Read them before you export, every time.

**Name the file like a document, not a draft.** `Bay-Ridge-Service-Operations-Readout-2024-06-12.pdf`, not `BayRidge_deck_v7_FINAL_new.pptx`. The filename is the first thing the board member sees.

**Check the accessibility floor.** Sufficient text contrast, no meaning carried by colour alone, alt text on images if your tool supports it, and a version that is legible on a phone screen. Some of your most important readers will read it on a phone.

## The read-without-you test

Before any deck goes to a customer, run this. It takes fifteen minutes and it is the closest thing to a guarantee available.

Hand the deck — no narration, no context — to a colleague who does not know the account. Give them four minutes. Then take it back and ask four questions:

1. **What is this company's problem?**
2. **What is it costing them?**
3. **What are we proposing?**
4. **What are we asking them to do next?**

If they cannot answer all four, the deck does not survive the forward, and the fix is almost always in the headlines rather than in the visuals. Ask a fifth question if the first four pass: **which slide would you delete?** The honest answer to that is worth more than any praise, and it is usually right.

Run the same test on yourself for pacing: click through at the speed you will actually present, with a timer, saying nothing. If the silent pass takes longer than your substance budget from lesson 2, the deck is too long — cut slides, not seconds per slide.

## Practice

You are building the deck for the **Fairmount Distribution** readout — fifty minutes, video call, Ted Alvarez leaves at minute fifteen, deck will be forwarded to a COO who will not attend. You have your brief from lesson 2, your six-beat spine from lesson 3, and your three specified charts from lesson 4.

**Exercise 1 — storyboard on paper.** Produce a storyboard of ten to fifteen slides. For each: the slide number, the assertion headline as a full sentence, a one-line description of the evidence, and the beat it serves. Do this on paper or in a plain text file. Do not open a presentation tool for this exercise.

**Exercise 2 — run the headline test.** Write your headlines alone, in order, as a numbered list with nothing else. Read them consecutively and answer in writing: does a stranger get a complete argument? Name every headline that is a topic label rather than an assertion, and rewrite each one.

**Exercise 3 — the summary slide.** Write the front-of-deck summary slide for the absent COO: the situation, the finding, the cost, the proposal, and the ask, in no more than six lines total. Every number traceable to the Fairmount data.

**Exercise 4 — rewrite a bullet slide.** Here is a slide from your company's standard deck:

> **Headline:** Warehouse Execution Platform Overview
>
> - Real-time inventory visibility down to the bin location
> - Dynamic slotting with location-change impact modeling
> - Handheld application with offline queueing and auto-sync
> - Labor standards and performance tracking by associate
> - Guided pick paths with barcode verification at each touch
> - Configurable dashboards and exception alerting

Diagnose it in three sentences: what it does to a live room, what it does to a forwarded reader, and what it does to your narrative. Then rewrite it as two or three slides, each with an assertion headline about Fairmount's operation and exactly one piece of evidence, and state what goes to the appendix instead.

**Exercise 5 — build the appendix.** List the appendix contents for this deck, ordered by the question you expect it to answer, and name the person in the room who would ask for each. Then write the appendix index slide.

**Exercise 6 — the two-artifact decision.** Argue in five sentences whether this specific deck should be one artifact or two, given the room, the video-call format, and the absent COO. State what you would do differently under each answer.

**Exercise 7 — run the read-without-you test.** Give your storyboard to someone who does not know the scenario, allow four minutes, and ask the four questions. Record their exact answers, then list every change you made as a result. If nobody is available, wait twenty-four hours and grade your own storyboard against the four questions in writing — and answer the fifth question honestly.

**Exercise 8 — the pacing pass.** Click or read through your storyboard silently at presentation speed with a timer. Report the elapsed time against the substance budget you computed in lesson 2, and name the specific slides you cut if it ran long.
