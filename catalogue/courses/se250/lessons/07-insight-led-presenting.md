## Two ways to open, and what each one costs

Every sales presentation opens in one of two postures.

**The playback.** *"Here is what we heard from you, here is what matters most, and here is how we address it."* Safe, respectful, and it works. It confirms you listened. It also, quietly, accepts the buyer's own diagnosis of their situation as the frame for the entire conversation.

**The teach.** *"Here is something about your situation that you have not seen, and it changes what the right response is."* Riskier. Requires actual work. And when it lands it does something a playback cannot: it makes you the person who improved their understanding of their own business, rather than the vendor who accurately transcribed it.

The difference matters commercially, not just rhetorically. If you accept the buyer's diagnosis, you are competing inside their frame — and inside their frame the requirements are already written, the criteria are already set, and the competitor with the longer feature list or the lower price wins. If you change the diagnosis, the criteria change with it. That is why this is worth learning even though it is the hardest thing in this course.

There is a specific catch that makes the teach genuinely risky, and it should be said before anything else: **an insight that is wrong is much worse than a playback.** You are telling a room of experienced people that they have misread their own operation. If your evidence does not hold, you have spent your credibility and made an enemy of whoever owned the original diagnosis. Everything in this lesson is about earning the right to make that move.

## What actually counts as an insight

Reps use "insight" to mean any interesting-sounding sentence. Three tests separate a commercial insight from a trend slide.

**Test 1 — Is it new to them?** Not new to the market, not new to you. New to the people in this room. "Companies are moving to the cloud" is new to nobody. "Your callbacks separate by parts availability, not tenure" is new to Dana, who has been running the opposite theory for eighteen months.

**Test 2 — Can you prove it, preferably with their data?** An insight is a claim about their business, and claims about someone's business get tested by people who know it better than you. The strongest form is their own data segmented in a way they have not segmented it. The next strongest is a benchmark from comparable operations. The weakest is an assertion about the industry, which is where most "insight" slides live.

**Test 3 — Does it lead to you?** This is the commercial half, and it is what separates an insight from an interesting fact. If the correct response to your insight is "hire two more supervisors," you have done a favour for a prospect who will now not buy anything. The insight must be true *and* the capability that addresses it must be one you actually have. If those two things are not both true, you have a consulting observation, not a commercial teaching point.

An insight that passes all three is rare and reusable. Most companies have two or three that work across an entire segment, and reps who find one use it for a year.

A fourth informal test, worth applying: **would they be annoyed if a competitor told them first?** If the answer is no, it is not an insight.

## Where insights come from

They are found, not invented, and there are five reliable places to look.

**Their own data, segmented differently.** The richest source by a distance. Every operation reports its numbers the way its systems happen to slice them, which is usually by org chart, by period, or by category. Slicing by a *causal* dimension instead — proximity, sequence, availability, timing, exposure — routinely reveals something the standard report structurally cannot show. Bay Ridge reports callbacks by technician and by month. Nobody reports them by whether the part was on the truck, because no system produces that view.

**Cross-account pattern.** You see forty operations a year; your buyer sees one. What is normal to you is invisible to them. "Every operator we work with who crossed 10,000 units hit the same wall at the same place" is a genuine insight, and it is available to you and to nobody inside their building.

**Benchmark against comparable operations.** Numbers are meaningless without comparison, and buyers usually lack the comparison set. A first-time fix rate of 61% sounds unremarkable until it sits next to a distribution of comparable operators. Two cautions: the comparison set must be genuinely comparable, and you must say where it came from.

**A cost they do not count.** Every operation has costs that no system owns and therefore no report shows: the time between jobs, the second visit, the rework nobody codes as rework, the customer who does not complain and does not renew. Surfacing an uncounted cost is powerful because it cannot be disputed by pointing at a report — there is no report.

**A change in their world they have not fully priced.** A regulation, a customer expectation, a labour-market shift, a demand pattern. The insight is not that the change happened; they know. It is that the change has invalidated an assumption their operating model still runs on.

Bay Ridge's insight comes from the first and fifth of these together: the route model assumed technician familiarity (an assumption invalidated by a change), and their own data segmented by parts availability proves the consequence.

## The structure of a reframe

An insight delivered badly is just an argument. The sequence below is a well-established commercial-teaching pattern, and its ordering is doing specific work at every step.

**1. The warmer.** Show you understand their world before you say anything challenging. Two or three sentences of their operation described accurately, ideally including a problem they did not tell you about but which you know operations like theirs have. This buys the right to the rest. *"Most operators your size tell us the same three things: the newer buildings are harder, dispatch is doing triage by 9am, and nobody trusts the parts data."*

**2. The reframe.** State the different way of seeing it. Short, clear, and slightly uncomfortable. *"We think the callback problem in operations like yours is almost never a skill problem."*

**3. The rational drowning.** The evidence, in their numbers, building until the old explanation is untenable. This is where lesson 4's charts live. The word "drowning" is deliberate: one data point is arguable, a sequence of them that all point the same direction is not.

**4. The emotional impact.** Make it their problem, not an abstraction. This is not manipulation; it is the step where the room recognises themselves. *"Which means every one of those 6,270 return visits was a technician who knew exactly what was wrong and could not fix it. And the building manager watched a second van arrive."*

**5. The new way.** What the right response looks like — described as a capability requirement, not as your product. *"What has to change is that the truck gets loaded from the unit's history rather than from the technician's guess."* Stating it as a requirement rather than a product is the pivotal move; it lets the buyer arrive at your category on their own.

**6. Your solution.** Only now. And briefly — you have done the work; the product is the last two minutes of a fifteen-minute sequence, not the first.

The failure mode is compression. Reps get to step 2, feel the discomfort in the room, and jump to step 6 to relieve it. That converts a reframe into an unsupported accusation followed by a pitch, which is the worst possible version of this.

## Worked example: Bay Ridge reframed

Here is the sequence in full, as spoken. It runs about four minutes.

**Warmer.**

> *"Before I get to your numbers, let me describe what we see in operations shaped like yours — three markets, eleven thousand units, a route model that has worked for decades. Three things are usually true. The buildings that came on in the last five years are harder than the older stock, because the newer equipment has more electronics and fewer people who have worked on it. Dispatch is doing triage by nine in the morning rather than executing a plan. And nobody entirely trusts the parts data, so the truck gets loaded on the technician's judgement. Does that sound like Bay Ridge?"*

That question is not rhetorical. You want a yes, and you want it out loud, because everything after this depends on the room agreeing that you understand their world.

**Reframe.**

> *"Here is the part that surprises people. In operations like this one, the callback problem is almost never a skill problem. And I know that is the opposite of what you have been working on."*

Short. Named as a contradiction. Then stop — a two-second pause here is worth more than another sentence.

**Rational drowning.**

> *"Three things from your own dispatch export."*
>
> *"First: your callback rate by technician tenure. Under two years, 21%. Two years and over, 18%. Three points. If tenure were the driver, that gap would be enormous, and it is not."*
>
> *"Second: the same callbacks, sliced by whether the technician had the right part on the truck. With the part: 4%. Without it: 71%. That is not a gap, that is a different phenomenon."*
>
> *"Third: your renewal losses. Forty-two contracts last year. Thirty-one of them were buildings with three or more callbacks in the twelve months before they left. Buildings with zero or one callback renewed at 94%. Your renewal problem and your callback problem are the same problem, and it has a specific cause."*

Three moves, in order: eliminate the current explanation, establish the actual one, connect it to the number the CFO cares about. The third is what turns an operational finding into a commercial one, and it is what makes Marcus stay past minute twenty.

**Emotional impact.**

> *"What that means in practice is that most of those 6,270 return visits were not technicians who could not fix the problem. They were technicians who knew exactly what was wrong, wrote a part number on a form, and drove away — and then a building manager watched a second van pull up two days later. Dana, that is the thing your renewal number is actually measuring."*

**New way.**

> *"So the requirement is not more training and it is not more supervision. The requirement is that the truck gets loaded from what the unit has already told you, instead of from what the technician can guess before they have seen it. That means the unit's fault and repair history has to be in the technician's hand — in a machine room with no signal — and it means the parts decision has to happen before the route starts, not after the first visit."*

**Your solution.**

> *"That is what we do. Three slides, and then I want to talk about proving it in sixty days."*

Read the whole sequence back and notice how little of it is about your product. Four minutes of a room's attention, of which about fifteen seconds mention what you sell — and by the time you get there, the room has already agreed to the requirement your product satisfies.

## Tension: how much, and where

A reframe requires tension. Without it there is no reason to change anything. But tension has a shape, and getting the shape wrong is what makes an insight land as an insult.

**Put the tension on the situation, not on the people.** "The route model made an assumption that stopped being true" is a statement about a system. "You misdiagnosed this" is a statement about Dana, who is in the room and who has staked eighteen months on the tenure theory.

**Give the old explanation its due.** It was a reasonable read of the available evidence. Say so, and mean it. *"Tenure is the obvious place to look, and the fixes you put in were the right response to that diagnosis"* costs you nothing and buys you the ability to say the next sentence.

**Attribute the finding to their data, not to your cleverness.** "Your export says this" is unarguable and blame-free. "We figured out what you missed" is a fight.

**Give the room a way to be right.** The strongest framing makes the old diagnosis part of the new one rather than an error. Bay Ridge's tenure concern is real — newer technicians *are* worse at guessing the part without history, which is exactly why the information gap hits them hardest. Dana was seeing a real signal and attributing it to the wrong cause. Saying that lets her keep her credibility and adopt your frame in the same sentence.

**Do not over-hold the punchline.** Some tension is productive; a rep who spends eight minutes building suspense reads as manipulative. Reframe by minute four.

And release the tension when it has done its work. The reframe should end in a requirement the room can act on, not in an unresolved sense that everything is broken. People who feel exposed defend; people who feel newly equipped decide.

## Testing an insight before you use it

Never take an untested reframe into a room that matters. Four checks.

**Check the data twice, and check it their way.** If your slice of their export disagrees with their internal report, find out why before the meeting, not during it. Fairmount measures accuracy at the order level; you measured at the line level. Whoever raises that first controls the next five minutes.

**Test it on the champion first.** Walk Dana through the reframe in a private call a week before. Three outcomes, all useful: she agrees and becomes an advocate who can carry it when you are not there; she disagrees and tells you why, which saves you from being wrong in front of a CFO; or she agrees but tells you the room will not hear it that way, which is information about framing rather than about truth. There is a real cost to this — you lose the surprise — and it is almost always worth paying.

**Say it out loud to a colleague who will push.** An insight that survives a hostile listener survives a room.

**Have the fallback ready.** If someone in the room dismantles a data point, you need a next sentence that is not defensive. Usually: *"Then I have that wrong, and I would like to understand it — what am I missing?"* Genuine curiosity at the moment of being contradicted is one of the highest-credibility moves available, and it is only possible if you have decided in advance that you will make it.

## When not to lead with an insight

Insight-led is a technique, not a religion. Four situations where the playback is the better opening.

**When you do not have one.** A manufactured insight is transparent and expensive. If the honest answer is that you have nothing they do not know, run an excellent playback.

**When the buyer's diagnosis is already correct and already yours.** Some buyers have done the analysis. Telling them they are wrong when they are right is a strange way to open. Confirm, add precision, and move to how.

**When trust has not been established.** A reframe from a stranger is a lecture. You need enough relationship for the warmer to be credible; a first call with someone you have never spoken to is usually too early.

**In a procurement-controlled process.** When the requirements are locked and the evaluation is scored against a matrix, the room may have no authority to change the frame. Reframing there is worth attempting only with the person who *can* reopen the requirements, and usually not in the scored meeting.

The general rule: **reframe when the buyer's current diagnosis leads them somewhere that is not you, and you can prove the diagnosis is wrong.** That is the exact condition. In every other case the playback is not a lesser option; it is the right one.

## Practice

Return to **Fairmount Distribution**. Rina Okafor's stated position is that two of her four DC managers are right — this is a discipline problem, and if associates followed the process the numbers would fix themselves. That belief is the frame you may need to change.

The data you have:

- Mispick rate on items whose pick location changed within 30 days: **2.8%** (1.4M lines). Items with stable locations: **0.9%** (8.9M lines).
- Mispick rate by tenure: under 5 weeks **1.4%** (3.2M lines); 5 weeks and over **1.2%** (7.1M lines).
- Location changes per month by DC: Ashland 310, Brant 2,940, Cordell 505, Dunmore 690.
- Order accuracy by DC, December: Ashland 97.9%, Brant 95.8%, Cordell 97.7%, Dunmore 97.2%. Brant is the only DC declining.
- Credits: 2,880 → 3,150 → 3,410 → 3,690 by quarter, at $74 each.
- Two major customers require 99.5% accuracy; Ted Alvarez lost a $3.1M customer last year to "service reliability."
- Associate turnover 72%; new hires take five weeks to reach standard rate.
- Brant is the DC that ran the discipline initiative, and its manager is in your meeting.

**Exercise 1 — state the insight.** Write the reframe as a single sentence, in Fairmount's language. Then answer each of the three tests in one sentence: is it new to them, can you prove it with their data, and does it lead to your capability? If it fails any test, revise it until it passes all three.

**Exercise 2 — trace the source.** Name which of the five sources this insight comes from, and identify one *additional* insight available from a different source in the same data set. State in two sentences why you would or would not use the second one.

**Exercise 3 — build the rational drowning.** Write the evidence sequence as three moves: eliminate the current explanation, establish the actual one, and connect it to the number Ted cares about. Show the arithmetic that turns the location-stability finding into an annual dollar figure, and state every assumption you added.

**Exercise 4 — write the full sequence.** Produce all six steps as spoken text — warmer, reframe, rational drowning, emotional impact, new way, your solution — running about four minutes. Step 5 must describe a capability requirement without naming your product. Step 6 must be no more than three sentences.

**Exercise 5 — defuse the tension.** The Brant DC manager championed the discipline initiative and is in the room, and Brant is both the worst-performing DC and the one with nine times the location churn of Ashland. Rewrite your reframe paragraph so that this person can adopt it without being humiliated. Then write the one sentence that makes their original diagnosis part of the new one rather than an error.

**Exercise 6 — pre-test it.** Write the three-sentence version you would walk Rina through privately a week ahead. Then write the two questions you would ask her afterward, one about whether the finding is right and one about whether the room can hear it.

**Exercise 7 — the fallback.** Fairmount's analyst says in the meeting: *"Your location-change number includes seasonal resets. We move 1,800 items every October by design and they are not errors."* Write your response in no more than four sentences. It must not be defensive, it must establish whether the finding survives, and it must return to the thread. Then state what you would have done before the meeting to avoid needing it.

**Exercise 8 — argue the other side.** Make the three-sentence case that this presentation should open with a playback rather than an insight, given this specific room. Then say which you would actually choose and why. A defensible answer either way; an undefended one is the failure.
