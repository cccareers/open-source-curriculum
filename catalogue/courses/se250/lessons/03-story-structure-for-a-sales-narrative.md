## Why a list loses to a story

Take two versions of the same twenty minutes.

**Version A.** "Today I want to walk you through our platform. We have four modules. Dispatch handles scheduling and routing. The mobile app gives technicians job history and parts lookup offline. Truck stock tracks inventory at the van level. And the contract module ties asset history to the maintenance agreement. Let me show you each one."

**Version B.** "Three years ago Bay Ridge renewed 91% of its maintenance contracts. Last year you renewed 84%. That is 42 buildings, about $613,000 of recurring revenue, and nobody in this room can tell me with confidence why it happened. I think I can, and I think it is the same reason your callback rate is 19%. Let me show you what I found in your own data."

Version A is complete, accurate, and forgettable. Version B is incomplete, and the room is now leaning forward.

The difference is not enthusiasm or polish. It is structure. Version A is a **list**: items with no necessary order, no tension, and no reason for the listener to care about item two more than item one. Version B is the opening of a **story**: there is a character in trouble, something is at stake, a question has been raised, and an answer has been promised but withheld.

People remember stories and forget lists, and that is not a soft claim about human warmth. It is mechanical. A list has to be memorized item by item. A story has causal links between its parts, so recalling one beat pulls the next one with it. Your champion is going to have to retell your presentation to three people who were not there. If you gave them a list, they will retell two items badly. If you gave them a story, they will retell the shape of it, and the shape is the argument.

There is a second reason, more commercial. A list invites feature-by-feature comparison with your competitor, which is a game decided by whoever has the longer list. A story that frames the problem a particular way makes your competitor's list answer the wrong question.

## The protagonist is not your product

Here is the single most common structural failure in sales presentations: the product is the hero. The narrative goes *our company was founded, we saw a gap, we built a platform, it has these powers, and now it has come to save you.* Every slide is a demonstration of the hero's abilities.

In that story the buyer is a bystander. They are watching someone else be impressive. Nothing in it is about them, and there is no role for them except audience.

Invert it. **The customer is the protagonist. Your product is what they get partway through that lets them win.** In story terms your product is the mentor, the map, the tool — never the one whose journey this is. Dana Whitfield is the one with a problem, a deadline, a boss, and a decision. The platform is what she picks up in the middle of the story.

This inversion has an immediate practical consequence you can check in any deck: **count the slides that would still exist if your company did not.** In a customer-as-protagonist narrative, the first third of the deck is entirely about the buyer's world and would survive your company's disappearance. In a product-as-hero deck, slide two is already an architecture diagram.

Make the protagonist a person, not an organization. "Bay Ridge is losing contracts" is an abstraction and abstractions have no stakes. "Dana has 240 technicians, 11,400 units, and a renewal number that has moved the wrong way three years running — and she does not know why" has a person in it. A named person with an unsolved problem is the smallest unit of story that works.

A caution: this is not license to invent. Everything you attribute to the protagonist comes from your discovery notes, in their words where possible. A story built on things they told you is unarguable. A story built on things you assumed collapses the first time somebody in the room says "that is not actually how it works here."

## The six-beat arc

Sales narratives that work almost always have the same skeleton. Different traditions name the beats differently; the version below is chosen because each beat maps cleanly onto slides.

![The six beats of a sales narrative, from stable world through the turn to the new normal](./img/narrative-arc.png)

**Beat 1 — The world as it is.** The buyer's situation, described accurately and without judgment. This beat's whole job is recognition: they should be thinking *yes, that is us,* not *how did you know that.* No product, no problem framing yet, just their operation as they would describe it.

**Beat 2 — The change.** Something moved. The market shifted, volume grew, a customer's expectations rose, a regulation tightened, tenure fell. This beat is why the status quo stopped being viable, and it is what makes the story *now* instead of *someday*. A narrative missing this beat has no answer to "why not next year."

**Beat 3 — The stakes.** What it costs to stay where they are, in numbers and in consequences. This is the beat reps soften and should not. If the cost of doing nothing is small, the honest conclusion is that they should do nothing.

**Beat 4 — The struggle.** What they have already tried, and why it did not fully work. This beat is the one most often skipped, and skipping it is expensive: it is the beat that proves you understand them, and it is where you neutralize "we are already handling this" before anyone has to say it out loud. Handle their previous attempts with respect. They were not stupid; the attempts were reasonable and insufficient.

**Beat 5 — The turn.** The new way of seeing it, and then — and only then — the capability that makes the new way possible. This is the first appearance of your product, and it should feel like a consequence of everything before it rather than an arrival.

**Beat 6 — The new normal.** What their operation looks like afterward, concretely. Not "transformation" — Tuesday. A named person's day, a number that moved, and then the ask that gets them there.

Two structural rules about this arc.

**Beats 1 through 4 belong to the customer; beats 5 and 6 belong to the change.** In a twenty-minute presentation that is roughly twelve minutes before your product appears. Reps find that unbearable and cheat, and the cheating is why the product lands as an interruption rather than a resolution.

**The resolution must be enabled by the product, not equal to it.** "Our platform gives you truck stock management" is a feature. "Technicians arrive with the part because the system knows what the unit failed with last time" is a resolution. The difference is that the second one is a sentence about Bay Ridge and the first one is a sentence about you.

## Stakes are what make a conflict a conflict

A "problem" without stakes is a preference. Most weak sales narratives have a problem and no stakes, and they feel low-energy for exactly that reason.

Stakes come in three layers, and a strong narrative uses all three.

**Organizational stakes** are the numbers: the recurring revenue at risk, the cost of the callbacks, the credits issued. This is the layer a CFO acts on and it is the layer your business-case work in se201 produces.

**Personal stakes** are what the problem costs the people in the room. Dana defends the renewal number in a quarterly review. The dispatch supervisor takes the call when a building manager is angry. Nobody says this part out loud, and you should not say it for them — but a narrative that ignores it entirely feels bloodless.

**Trajectory stakes** are what happens if nothing changes. This is the most under-used and the most persuasive, because it converts a static problem into a moving one. Bay Ridge's renewal rate did not just fall; it fell 91 to 88 to 84. The next point on that line is the argument for acting this year, and it is more compelling than any single-year figure.

State stakes precisely and conservatively. Precision reads as confidence; exaggeration reads as sales. "Roughly $613,000 of recurring revenue left last year, and the trend line says next year is worse" beats "you are hemorrhaging millions" every time, and it survives being checked.

## Worked example: the Bay Ridge arc

Here is the full arc for the readout you briefed in lesson 2, written as the sentences you would actually say. The single message from that brief was: *"Your callbacks are not a tenure problem. They are a parts-and-sequencing problem, they are costing you contract renewals, and they are fixable this year without hiring anyone."*

**Beat 1 — The world as it is.** *"Bay Ridge maintains 11,400 units across three markets with 240 technicians. About 33,000 service calls a year. The model is the one the industry has run for forty years: a route, a truck, a technician who knows their buildings, and a maintenance agreement that renews quietly as long as nobody notices anything."*

No product, no problem, and everyone in the room agrees with every word. That agreement is the foundation everything else rests on.

**Beat 2 — The change.** *"Two things moved. Your unit count grew 14% over three years, mostly in newer buildings with newer equipment your longest-tenured technicians have never worked on. And a third of your technicians now have under two years in the trade, because the retirements arrived faster than the apprenticeships. The route model assumed a technician who knew every unit on their route. That assumption is no longer true, and it did not stop being true on a particular day, which is why nobody flagged it."*

This is the beat that makes the presentation urgent without making anyone defensive. Nothing here is a failure; something changed underneath a working system.

**Beat 3 — The stakes.** *"Here is what it costs. 19% of your service calls are callbacks — 6,270 return visits a year at $180 loaded, about $1.13 million of technician time spent on units you already visited. Overtime is 9% of a $19.8 million field payroll. And the number that actually matters: your renewal rate went 91, 88, 84. Last year that was 42 contracts and $613,000 of recurring revenue. At that slope, next year is not 84."*

Three layers in one beat: the operational cost, the trajectory, and the recurring revenue a CFO cares about most.

**Beat 4 — The struggle.** *"You have not ignored this. You extended the apprenticeship program and added a mentor pairing for first-year technicians. You added a second dispatch supervisor. You put the callback report on the weekly ops review. Every one of those was the right response to the diagnosis you had — that this is a tenure problem. And the callback rate has moved from 20% to 19% in eighteen months."*

Notice what this beat does. It removes the option of dismissing you as someone who does not know the account, it pre-empts "we have this in hand," and it sets up the turn — because if all the correct responses to a tenure diagnosis produced one point of improvement, the diagnosis is worth re-examining. That is the door the next beat walks through.

**Beat 5 — The turn.** *"So we pulled your callback data and segmented it a different way. Not by technician tenure — by whether the technician had the right part on the truck. Callbacks where the technician had the part: 4%. Callbacks where they did not: 71%. Tenure barely separates the two groups; parts availability separates them almost completely. Your first visits are diagnostic visits, and about a fifth of them end with a technician writing down a part number and driving away. That is not a skill problem. It is an information problem — the truck was loaded before anyone knew what the unit needed, because the unit's own history lives in a system the technician cannot see from a machine room."*

*"That is what our platform changes. Every unit's fault and repair history is on the technician's phone, offline. The system predicts the likely part from that history and the fault code, and the truck-stock module loads it before the route starts."*

The reframe lands first; the capability lands second, as the answer to a question the room is now actively asking. Reverse those two paragraphs and the whole thing becomes a feature pitch. Note also that the reframe here is a *commercial insight* — the technique for finding and building one is lesson 7's whole subject.

**Beat 6 — The new normal.** *"Six months in, this is what changes. Your technician arrives at 14 Court Street with the contactor already on the truck because the unit failed the same way in March. The call closes in one visit. First-time fix goes from 61% toward the mid-70s, which is where comparable operators land in the first year. Callbacks fall, so 4,000 or so visits a year come back as capacity — about $720,000 of technician time at $180 a visit, or roughly 40% of the $1.78 million overtime line. And the building managers who were quietly deciding whether to renew stop seeing a second van in their lobby."*

*"What I want to leave with is a sixty-day pilot in Queens: 38 technicians, 1,900 units, starting the first week of October, measured on first-time fix and callback rate. And a line in the September capital plan so that if the pilot works we are not waiting until next summer."*

Read those six beats back to back. There is no capabilities section. There is no company overview. Your product appears once, in the middle, as the answer to a question the audience is now asking. And the whole thing runs about eleven minutes spoken, which leaves room for the interruptions lesson 6 will teach you to absorb.

## The spine test

Before you build a single slide, write the arc as **six sentences on one line each**, then read them consecutively. This is the spine test, and it catches most structural problems in ninety seconds.

The Bay Ridge spine:

```text
1. Bay Ridge runs 11,400 units on a route model that assumes technicians know their buildings.
2. Unit count grew and tenure fell, so that assumption quietly stopped being true.
3. It costs $1.1M in callback visits and the renewal rate has gone 91 to 88 to 84.
4. The fixes tried so far all treated it as a tenure problem and moved the number one point.
5. Segmented by parts availability instead of tenure, the callback data separates almost completely.
6. Technicians arrive with the part, first-time fix rises, and the pilot proves it in sixty days.
```

Three things to check.

**Does each sentence require the one before it?** If you can shuffle two beats without noticing, you have a list wearing a story's clothes.

**Can a stranger predict the product from beats 1 through 4?** If they can, the narrative is doing its job. If beat 5 arrives out of nowhere, the earlier beats are not aimed properly.

**Is there a sentence with no customer noun in it?** Beat 5 is allowed to mention you. If beats 1 through 4 mention your company at all, cut it.

## Mapping beats to slides

The arc is not one slide per beat. Beats get more or fewer slides according to how much proof they need. A workable allocation for a twenty-two minute substance budget:

| Beat | Slides | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. The world as it is | 1 | Recognition is fast. One slide, their operation in their units. |
| 2. The change | 1–2 | One chart of the thing that moved. |
| 3. The stakes | 2–3 | The costliest beat to prove; carries the numbers a CFO acts on. |
| 4. The struggle | 1 | Their own attempts, respectfully listed, with the outcome. |
| 5. The turn | 3–4 | The reframe gets its own slide. Then the capability, then a proof point. |
| 6. The new normal | 2 | One before-and-after, one ask. |

That is ten to thirteen slides, which is what lesson 2's time budget said would fit. This is not a coincidence — the brief and the arc are the same decision seen twice, and if the arc does not fit the budget, one of them is wrong.

One rule worth internalizing: **the ask is a slide.** Reps deliver a careful narrative and then finish with "so, thoughts?" — dissolving twenty minutes of structure into an unstructured pause. Beat 6 ends with a written, specific request on screen.

## A rewrite, worked

Real openings usually need surgery rather than invention. Here is a genuine-shaped opening for the Bay Ridge meeting, of the type produced by a rep who skipped the brief.

**Before:**

> "Thanks everyone for the time. Quick bit about us first — we were founded in 2011, we now serve over 400 field service organizations across North America, and we were named a leader in the field service category last year. Our platform has four core modules. Today I want to focus on dispatch and mobile, since Dana mentioned those are the priorities, and then I will show you a bit of the truck stock capability, which I think is really relevant to what you described. Priya, I have some architecture material toward the back for you as well."

Diagnose it against the arc. Beat 1: absent — nothing about Bay Ridge's world. Beat 2: absent — no change, so no urgency. Beat 3: absent — no stakes, so no reason to act. Beat 4: absent. Beat 5: arrives at the second sentence, before anyone has a question it answers. Beat 6: absent. The protagonist is the vendor. And the closing sentence tells Priya explicitly that the next fifteen minutes are not for her, which is how you lose the person with a veto.

**After:**

> "Before I show you anything, I want to test something with you, because if I am wrong about it the rest of this meeting is not worth your forty-five minutes."
>
> "Bay Ridge maintains 11,400 units with 240 technicians on a route model that has worked for four decades — a technician, a truck, and a set of buildings they know cold. Two things have moved underneath that model. Your unit count is up 14%, mostly in newer buildings, and a third of your technicians now have under two years in the trade."
>
> "The visible symptom is a 19% callback rate. The expensive symptom is that your renewal rate has gone 91, 88, 84 — 42 contracts and about $613,000 of recurring revenue last year. Dana, you have told me you read that as a tenure problem, and everything you have done about it — the mentor pairing, the second dispatch supervisor, the weekly callback review — is the right response to that diagnosis. In eighteen months it has moved the callback rate one point."
>
> "I think the diagnosis is wrong, and I think your own data says so. That is what I want to show you first."

Same rep, same product, same forty-five minutes. The rewrite contains no company history, no module list, and no product. It contains a person, a change, a cost, a respectful account of what they have already tried, and a promise withheld. Priya, incidentally, is more likely to stay engaged through this version than through the one that offered her an architecture appendix, because she now has a reason to care what the answer is.

## Three failure modes to watch for

**Product as protagonist.** Diagnosed by counting the beat-1-to-4 slides that mention your company. The fix is not to delete those mentions; it is to rebuild the front of the narrative out of discovery material you already have.

**Conflict without stakes.** The narrative names a problem but never says what it costs. Symptom: the room agrees with you pleasantly and does nothing. The fix is beat 3, with numbers and a trajectory.

**Resolution before conflict.** The most common of the three, because the product is the part reps are confident about. Symptom: you get feature questions early and never regain control of the thread. The fix is discipline — beat 5 does not move earlier, no matter how much you want to get to it.

There is a fourth, subtler failure: **a story the buyer cannot verify.** If beats 1 through 4 contain any claim about their operation that came from your assumption rather than their mouth, one person in the room will notice, and every number after it becomes suspect. Sourcing is not bureaucracy; it is what makes the narrative unarguable.

## Practice

Return to **Fairmount Distribution** from lesson 2 — the four-DC grocery wholesaler with 97.2% order accuracy, 88% inventory accuracy by location, 1,100 monthly credits at $74 each, 72% associate turnover, and $27.4M in annual warehouse labor. Rina Okafor believes two of her four DC managers are right that this is a discipline problem; Ted Alvarez lost a $3.1M customer to "service reliability" last year.

Two additional facts from a later call, which you have not yet used:

- Mispicks are **3.1 times more likely** on items whose pick location changed in the previous 30 days than on items whose location has been stable, regardless of who picked them
- Associates in their first five weeks account for **31%** of picks but **34%** of mispicks — a gap far smaller than anyone at Fairmount expects

**Exercise 1 — write the spine.** Produce the six-beat spine for the Fairmount readout: six sentences, one per beat, one line each. Beats 1 through 4 must contain no mention of your company.

**Exercise 2 — run the spine test.** Check your spine against all three tests: necessary ordering, product predictable from beats 1–4, and no vendor nouns in the first four beats. Write one sentence per test saying whether it passes, and revise anything that fails.

**Exercise 3 — build beat 3 properly.** Write the stakes beat in full, using all three layers: the organizational number, the personal cost to a named person in the room, and the trajectory. Every figure must come from the discovery notes or be derived from them with the arithmetic shown.

**Exercise 4 — write beat 4 without condescension.** Fairmount has run cycle counts, retrained on process, and put two DC managers on a discipline initiative. Write the struggle beat in a way that a DC manager who championed the discipline initiative could sit through without becoming your enemy. Then write, in one sentence, what that beat sets up for beat 5.

**Exercise 5 — the turn, in the right order.** Write beat 5 as two paragraphs: the reframe first, using the two additional facts above, and the capability second. Then write the same beat with the paragraphs reversed, and in three sentences describe what changes about how the room receives it.

**Exercise 6 — rewrite a bad opening.** Here is the opening a rep actually drafted:

> "Thanks for the time. I will keep the intro short — we work with 200-plus distribution operations, including three of the top ten grocery wholesalers. Our platform covers inventory, labor, and pick optimization. Rina, you mentioned accuracy is the priority, so I will spend most of the time there, and I have some integration detail at the back for Sam."

Diagnose it beat by beat, saying which beats are present and which are missing. Then rewrite it as a spoken opening of four short paragraphs that reaches the end of beat 3 without naming your product once.

**Exercise 7 — allocate the slides.** Using the beats-to-slides table and the fifty-minute meeting from lesson 2 (Ted leaves at fifteen minutes), assign a slide count to each beat and state which beat has to be complete before minute fifteen. Justify any place where you deviate from the table.
