## The deck is the last thing you should build

Watch a rep prepare for a customer meeting and you will usually see the same sequence. They open the presentation software. They find the most recent version of the standard deck. They delete the slides that obviously do not apply, add two about the integration the prospect asked about, change the logo on slide one, and rehearse by clicking through it once on the morning of the meeting.

That sequence produces a deck. It does not produce a presentation, because at no point in it did anyone decide what the meeting was for.

The habit is understandable. The tool is right there, the standard deck exists, and moving slides around feels like progress in a way that staring at a blank page does not. But every design decision you make in the tool — which chart, which order, how many slides, what the first slide says — depends on an answer you have not written down yet. So you make those decisions by default, which means you make them the same way you made them for the last customer, which is why the deck ends up being about your product instead of about this buyer's decision.

The discipline this lesson teaches is small and unglamorous: **before you design anything, write a one-page presentation brief.** Ten to fifteen minutes of work. It is the difference between a presentation that has a job and one that merely has slides.

## A presentation is an instrument for moving one decision

Start from what a sales presentation actually is. It is not a document. It is not a training session. It is not a product tour. It is an attempt to move a specific group of people, in a specific amount of time, from the belief they hold now to a belief that makes the next commercial step obvious.

That framing has three consequences you can act on.

**It has a target state, so it can succeed or fail measurably.** "The meeting went well" is not a result. "Marcus agreed to put the pilot on the September capital agenda" is a result. If you cannot name the observable thing that will have happened by the end, you are not going to get it.

**It is bounded by a room and a clock.** Six people, forty-five minutes, two of whom will leave early. That is a hard constraint on how many ideas can survive, and the number is smaller than you think.

**It competes with the buyer's other problems.** Nobody in the room woke up thinking about your category. They are thinking about the thing that went wrong last week. The presentation has to earn the shift.

Which means the first question is never "what should I show them?" It is "what has to change in their heads, and what is the smallest amount of material that changes it?"

## The presentation brief

Here is the artifact. Eight fields, one page, written in full sentences before you open any design tool.

**1. The decision.** What decision does this meeting exist to move? Not "build interest" — a decision, with a decider, a verb, and a date. If no decision is available at this stage, the meeting's job is to *qualify a decision into existence*, and you should say so plainly.

**2. The room.** Every attendee by name, role, what they care about, and what they can say yes or no to. Include who is *not* coming but will see the deck afterward. That absent reader is why lesson 5 exists.

**3. Where the deal actually is.** What has already happened — discovery calls, a technical walkthrough, a security questionnaire — and what has not. A presentation that re-runs discovery in front of an executive wastes the most expensive twenty minutes you will get.

**4. What they believe now.** One or two sentences in the buyer's own words, from your notes. This is the starting position. If you cannot write it, your discovery was thin and you should fix that before you build slides.

**5. The single message.** One sentence, in the buyer's language, that you want repeated in the hallway afterward by someone who was in the room. Not a tagline. A claim about *their* situation. Everything in the deck either supports this sentence or gets cut.

**6. The proof required.** What specific evidence would make that sentence credible *to the skeptic in the room*, not to a friendly champion. Usually two or three things: their own numbers, a comparable customer, and a demonstration that the hard part is actually solved.

**7. The ask.** The exact next step you will request, with a name attached and a date. "A sixty-day pilot in the Queens district, starting the first week of October, measured on first-time fix rate."

**8. The constraint.** Time, room, format, and anything else the physical situation imposes. Forty-five minutes on a video call with a hard stop is a different artifact from ninety minutes in a conference room with a whiteboard.

Two of these fields do most of the work. Field 5 is what stops the deck from sprawling. Field 7 is what stops the meeting from ending in "this was great, let us circle back."

## Meeting types have different jobs

The same customer, the same product, four different meetings, four different presentations. Reps who use one deck for all of them are answering the wrong question three times out of four.

| Meeting | The decision it moves | What it must contain | What kills it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First substantive meeting | Whether this is worth their team's time at all | A reframe of their situation, one credible proof point, a clear next step | A capabilities tour before you have earned the right to give one |
| Discovery playback | Whether you understood them correctly | Their words, their numbers, their priorities ranked, an explicit "did we get this wrong?" | Slipping in solution slides and turning it into a pitch |
| Technical validation | Whether the hard parts are actually solved | Architecture, integration surface, security posture, honest limits | Hand-waving; the evaluator's veto is real and rarely about value |
| Executive readout | Whether to fund it and when | The business case in their numbers, the risk, the ask, the timeline | Depth. An executive who wanted forty slides would have asked for the deck |

The most common error is running an executive readout with a technical validation deck, because that is the deck that already exists. The CFO sits through eighteen minutes on the integration layer, and then there are four minutes left for the only part of the deck he was there for.

## Reading the room

Attendees do not experience your presentation the same way, and the brief should say so per person. Four rough postures cover most rooms.

**The economic buyer** is deciding whether this is worth money that has other claims on it. They want the size of the problem, the confidence level, the risk of the change, and the ask. They will typically speak twice and leave with two numbers in their head. Make sure they are the right two.

**The operational owner** lives with the problem daily and will live with the change. They want to know what their team's Tuesday looks like afterward, and whether you understand the mess well enough to be trusted with it. They are the most likely person to catch you being wrong about their operation, and the most valuable ally if you are right.

**The technical evaluator** is protecting the estate. They want fit, effort, data handling, and honest limits. They very rarely say yes; they say "no objection," and that is the win.

**The skeptic** exists in most rooms and is often not on the agenda — the long-tenured supervisor who has watched three systems get rolled out and rolled back. Your evidence needs to be aimed at this person. Convincing the champion is not an achievement; they were already convinced.

A useful test on the brief: for each named attendee, write the one sentence they would say to a colleague afterward if the meeting worked. If two of them would say the same generic thing, your message is too vague; if you cannot write one at all for somebody in the room, either they should not be there or you have not built anything for them.

## Worked example: Bay Ridge Elevator Services

We will use this scenario for the rest of the course, so it is worth setting up properly. Read it once carefully; later lessons will assume you know it.

**The account.** Bay Ridge Elevator Services installs, maintains, and repairs elevators across three metropolitan markets. 240 field technicians. 11,400 units under maintenance contract. Roughly 70% of revenue is recurring maintenance, 30% is repair and modernization work.

**What you sell.** A field service management platform: dispatch and scheduling, technician mobile app, parts and truck-stock management, contract and asset history. Their incumbent is a fifteen-year-old dispatch system plus a large amount of spreadsheet. A competitor is also bidding.

**The people.**

- **Dana Whitfield**, VP of Service Operations — your champion, owns the problem, cannot sign
- **Marcus Iyer**, CFO — economic buyer, will attend for the first twenty minutes only
- **Priya Raman**, Director of IT — technical veto, has already run a security review
- **Two dispatch supervisors**, one of whom has been at Bay Ridge for nineteen years and has seen two systems fail

**What discovery produced.**

- First-time fix rate: **61%**
- Callback rate (a return visit to the same unit within 30 days): **19%**
- Roughly **33,000** service calls a year; loaded cost of a technician visit **$180**
- Contract renewal rate has fallen from **91% to 84%** over three years; 42 contracts lost last year at an average **$14,600** annual value
- Overtime runs **9%** of a **$19.8M** annual field payroll
- Mean response time to an entrapment call: **41 minutes**, against a 30-minute internal target
- Dana, on the second call: *"Our problem is tenure. A third of my techs have under two years in the trade, and it shows in the callback numbers."*

**The meeting.** Week six of the cycle. Forty-five minutes, in person, all six attendees, hard stop. You have completed two discovery calls and one technical walkthrough with Priya.

Now the brief.

**1. The decision.** Whether Bay Ridge commits to a paid sixty-day pilot in one market, and whether Marcus reserves a line for the full rollout in the September capital planning cycle. Both are needed; the pilot without the capital line is a project that dies in November.

**2. The room.** As above. Dana can authorize the pilot's operational cost. Marcus controls capital. Priya can stop it. The nineteen-year supervisor can quietly kill it afterward by telling the other supervisors it will not work.

**3. Where the deal is.** Discovery is done and the numbers above are confirmed. Security review is complete with no findings. Nothing has been presented back to Bay Ridge as a whole; Marcus has heard about this secondhand and has not seen a number.

**4. What they believe now.** That callbacks are a technician-experience problem, that the fix is more training and slower promotion, and that a software purchase is a way of avoiding the real issue. That belief is Dana's, it is sincere, and it is expensive.

**5. The single message.** *"Your callbacks are not a tenure problem. They are a parts-and-sequencing problem, they are costing you contract renewals, and they are fixable this year without hiring anyone."*

**6. The proof required.** Their own callback data segmented in a way they have not looked at it. The renewal loss traced to callback frequency at the building level. One comparable operator with a before-and-after. And, for Priya, evidence that the mobile app works where a technician has no signal in a machine room.

**7. The ask.** A sixty-day pilot in the Queens district — 38 technicians, roughly 1,900 units — starting the first week of October, measured on first-time fix rate and callback rate, with a September capital line reserved for the full rollout pending pilot results.

**8. The constraint.** Forty-five minutes with a hard stop. Marcus leaves at minute twenty. In-room, projector, no whiteboard. Assume the deck gets forwarded to a board member who was never in the room.

Look at what that brief has already decided. Marcus leaves at minute twenty, so the business case cannot be at the back. The message is a reframe, so the presentation cannot open with a capabilities overview. The proof list has four items, so at forty-five minutes the deck has room for roughly four proof moments and no more. The absent board member means the deck has to read without you. None of those decisions required opening a design tool, and every one of them would have been made badly by default.

## Sizing: what actually fits

Time is the constraint reps most consistently misjudge. Some rough arithmetic that survives contact with real meetings:

- A slide you actually *talk to* takes **90 seconds to 2 minutes**. Not 30 seconds. Reps rehearse at a pace they never hit live.
- **Interruptions consume 25–40%** of a good meeting. A good meeting. A silent room is a bad sign, not an efficient one.
- Your **first and last three minutes** are worth more than the middle twenty combined, because that is what gets remembered and repeated.

So a forty-five-minute meeting with a hard stop budgets roughly like this: five minutes of arrival, greetings, and settling; three minutes of opening; twenty-two minutes of substance; five minutes on the ask and next steps; and ten minutes absorbed by questions you did not schedule. Twenty-two minutes of substance at a realistic pace is **twelve to fifteen slides**, and that is if none of them is complicated.

Against that, the standard corporate deck is forty slides. The gap is not a rounding error. It is the reason presentations end with "we are almost out of time, so I will skip ahead" — which is the sentence that tells the room the last third was optional, and invites them to wonder whether the first two-thirds were too.

The corollary: **an appendix is not a compromise, it is a tool.** Everything the brief says is not required to move the decision, but that somebody might ask about, goes behind a divider slide. You will build that properly in lesson 5.

## The kill list

The most useful column in the brief is the one you write last: what this presentation will *not* contain. Writing it down converts a vague instinct into a decision you can defend when your manager asks why the standard company-overview slides are gone.

For the Bay Ridge readout, the kill list is:

- **The eight-slide company overview.** Bay Ridge has met you four times. Founding date and office map earn nothing.
- **The full capability matrix.** Priya's team has it already, and Marcus will not read it. It goes to the appendix.
- **The competitive comparison grid.** Nobody in the room asked. Raising it unprompted introduces a competitor you were not being compared against in that moment.
- **Pricing mechanics beyond a single total-cost line.** The ask is a pilot. Structuring the commercial terms is a separate conversation with a different agenda.
- **The product roadmap.** Roadmap slides in an executive readout convert a decision about today into a wait for next year.

Each of those is a slide somebody in your company will ask you to include. The brief is what lets you say "not in this meeting, and here is what it displaces."

## What good looks like, restated

If the brief is done properly, someone else in your company could pick it up, and — without any knowledge of the account — say what the deck must contain, in what order, and how long it runs. That is the test. The brief is not a warm-up exercise; it is the specification. Everything in lessons 3 through 5 is an implementation of it: the narrative structure carries field 5, the data visuals carry field 6, and the deck itself carries field 8's absent reader.

And when the meeting is over, the brief is what you review against. Not "how did it go" — did the belief in field 4 change, and did you get the ask in field 7? Those are answerable, and answering them honestly for ten meetings will teach you more than any course will.

## Practice

You sell a **warehouse execution platform**: inventory accuracy, pick-path optimization, labor tracking, and a handheld app for pickers and putaway staff.

**The account.** Fairmount Distribution runs four regional distribution centers for a grocery wholesaler. 620 warehouse associates. Roughly 41,000 order lines picked per day across the network.

**Discovery notes — Rina Okafor, VP of Supply Chain, 8 April:**

- "Our order accuracy sits around 97.2%. Customers expect 99.5% and two of the big ones have said so in writing."
- Mispicks generate roughly **1,100 credits per month** at an average **$74** each
- Inventory accuracy by location: **88%**
- "We cycle count constantly and we are still surprised at least once a week."
- Associate turnover: **72% annually**. "New hires take five weeks to reach standard rate."
- "Two of my four DC managers think this is a discipline problem. Get people to follow the process and the numbers fix themselves."

**Discovery notes — Ted Alvarez, CFO, 15 April:**

- Attends for the first fifteen minutes of any vendor meeting, "and I have never regretted leaving"
- Fully loaded warehouse labor: **$27.4M** a year across the network
- "I lost a customer worth $3.1M last year and the exit interview said 'service reliability.'"
- Capital requests are decided in **October**

**Discovery notes — Sam Beaudry, Director of Applications, 22 April:**

- Warehouse management system is a decade-old on-premise install with a supported but slow integration path
- Security review is done; no findings
- "Whatever we buy has to work when the wireless drops in the freezer aisle. It drops in the freezer aisle."

**The meeting.** Fifty minutes, video call, hard stop. Attending: Rina, Ted (first fifteen minutes only), Sam, and two DC managers — including one of the two who believes this is a discipline problem. You have completed three discovery calls and a technical session with Sam. A competitor is also bidding. Assume Rina forwards the deck to the COO, who will not attend.

**Exercise 1 — write the brief.** Produce all eight fields for this meeting, in full sentences. Field 5 must be a single sentence in Fairmount's language that makes a claim about their situation, not about your product. Field 7 must name a person, a scope, a start date, and the measure that will decide it.

**Exercise 2 — the room, person by person.** For each of the five attendees plus the absent COO, write the one sentence you want them saying to a colleague the next day. Then mark which of those six sentences your current field 5 actually delivers. If it delivers fewer than four, revise field 5 rather than adding slides.

**Exercise 3 — size it.** Ted leaves at minute fifteen. Build a minute-by-minute time budget for the fifty minutes that accounts for arrival, opening, substance, the ask, and interruption load. State the resulting maximum slide count, and state what has to happen before minute fifteen as a consequence.

**Exercise 4 — the kill list.** Name at least five things that a standard version of this deck would contain and that yours will not, with a one-sentence reason for each. At least one must be something you expect your own company to push back on.

**Exercise 5 — the meeting type.** The internal calendar invite calls this a "solution presentation." Using the meeting-type table, argue in three sentences which type it actually is, and name the one thing your brief would have to change if it were a different type instead.

**Exercise 6 — the failure test.** Write the two sentences describing the most likely way this meeting fails, given the room. Then name the single change to the brief — not the deck, the brief — that most reduces that risk.

## Check your understanding

2. In a forty-five-minute meeting with a hard stop, roughly how many slides you actually talk to will fit, and why? *(Answer: twelve to fifteen — about twenty-two minutes of substance at 90 seconds to 2 minutes a slide, after arrival, opening, the ask, and interruption load.)*
3. Why does the kill list matter? *(Answer: it turns a vague instinct into a defensible decision about what each cut slide would have displaced.)*
