## What this lesson is, and what it is not

A digital marketer is regularly asked to produce something that is not words: a short video, a voice-over walkthrough, a graphic that carries a number. You are not expected to be a filmmaker or a designer. You are expected to **plan a piece to a brief, produce it to a usable standard on the equipment you already have, and ship it accessible**.

That is the level here. Professional video and audio production, motion graphics, and graphic design are separate trades with their own tools and their own years of practice, and this course does not pretend otherwise. What it does insist on is that the planning discipline — brief, script, shot list, definition of done — is identical at every level, and it is the part beginners skip.

The running example is Harvest Lane's week 9 piece from the editorial calendar: **"Why Seedlings Get Leggy — and the Fix."** The plan calls for a guide plus a 45-second vertical video. The video is what we build here, end to end.

## Choosing the format before you choose the camera

The first question is not "video or graphic." It is "what shape is the answer?"

| If the answer is… | The format is | Because |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A physical motion or sequence | Video | Text describing a hand movement is always worse than showing it |
| A visible difference between two states | Photo pair or short video | "Leggy" is instantly obvious side by side and hard in prose |
| A number or a comparison | Static graphic or a real table | A table in the page beats an image of a table |
| A process with many branches | Written guide with a decision tree | Video cannot be skimmed or re-entered halfway |
| Something built over time | Time-lapse or a photo series | Compresses a season into ten seconds |
| Expert reasoning and nuance | Audio, or written Q&A | Voice carries hedging and warmth that text flattens |

Two format failures are worth naming because they are so common. The first is **video that should have been a table** — a two-minute talking head reciting depths a reader wanted to scan. The second is **the graphic that should have been text**: a spreadsheet screenshot or a text-heavy "infographic" posted as an image, which cannot be searched, cannot be selected, cannot reflow on a phone, and cannot be read by a screen reader at all. If the content of your graphic is words, the correct container is usually words.

Leggy seedlings passes the test on the first row and the second: the difference between a leggy seedling and a healthy one is instantly visible, and the fix is a physical action. Video, and short.

## The creative brief

Same discipline as the content brief in lesson 02, different fields. Production is expensive in time, and every ambiguity you leave in the brief gets resolved badly at 4pm on shoot day.

```txt
CREATIVE BRIEF — Harvest Lane
Asset:        "Leggy seedlings" — 45s vertical video
Pillar:       When it goes wrong
Ships with:   Guide, "Why Seedlings Get Leggy - and the Fix" (wk 9)
Owner:        You (shoot + edit)   SME on set: Elena (30 min)
Dates:        Shoot Feb 24 · Rough cut Feb 27 · Publish Mar 3

CHANNEL AND SPEC
Primary:      On the guide page, above the fold
Also:         Vertical social feeds; YouTube Shorts
Aspect:       9:16, 1080 x 1920
Length:       40-50s. Hard ceiling 50.
Assume:       Sound OFF on first view. Must work silent.

THE JOB
One idea:     Leggy seedlings are a light problem, not a water problem.
              The fix is closer light and a fan.
Audience:     First-timer, 3 weeks into seed starting, worried they
              have already failed. Anxious, not curious.
Tone:         Encouraging, specific. Never "you should have."
Success:      A viewer moves their light closer today.

MUST SHOW
  - Side-by-side: leggy tray vs. compact tray. This is the hook.
  - The measurement: how close the light actually goes (2-3 in).
  - The fan, running, at low speed.
  - One 3-second on-camera moment from Elena for credibility.

MUST NOT
  - No product pitch. This piece is retention, not conversion.
  - Do not claim leggy seedlings can be fully reversed. They can be
    planted deeper and they recover. Say that, precisely.

ACCESSIBILITY (build in, do not retrofit)
  - Open captions burned in, plus an uploaded caption file.
  - Full transcript published beneath the video on the guide page.
  - Every spoken number also appears as on-screen text.
  - Text overlays: min 48px, high contrast, safe from UI overlays
    (keep the bottom 20% and top 10% clear).
  - Do not distinguish the two trays by color alone - label them.

CONSTRAINTS
  Kit:        Phone, window light, $25 clip mic, a tripod.
  Location:   Elena's propagation bench, north window.
  Budget:     $0 incremental. Music from the licensed library only.
```

The "must not" section is doing real work. Without it, someone would have added a seed-tray promo at the end, and the piece would have converted the anxious moment into a sales pitch — which is precisely how a retention asset becomes a reason to unfollow.

Write a two-column audio/video script. The left column is what is heard; the right is what is seen. Writing them in one column is how you end up with forty seconds of narration over one static shot.

## The shot list

The script says what the finished piece contains. The shot list says what you must physically capture, ordered for the room and not for the timeline. Shoot everything in one location before moving; re-lighting a bench twice costs an hour.

```txt
Light: north window, 9-11am is even. No overhead fluorescents.

                                                 the bench edge.

                                                 the motion complete.

                                                 at phone size.

                                                 before the click.

                                                 hands, clean pot rim.
9   Healthy tray, hero          Medium    2      B-roll / cutaway.

```

Two lines in there save more grief than the rest combined. **Room tone** — thirty seconds of the room doing nothing — is what lets you patch audio gaps in the edit without an audible drop to digital silence. And the three-second handles before and after each action are what make cuts land; without them every cut is tight against the motion and the edit feels like a hiccup.

## Producing with the kit you already have

**Sound matters more than picture.** Viewers forgive a soft image and abandon bad audio within seconds. A $25 clip-on microphone plugged into a phone beats an expensive camera's built-in mic every time. Record indoors, away from a fridge, an air vent, or a road, and record thirty seconds of nothing before you start talking.

**Light is free and directional.** Put your subject facing a window, not with their back to it. A north-facing window gives soft, even light for hours. Turn off overhead lights so you are not mixing color temperatures — mixed light is the single most common reason phone footage looks amateur, and it is free to fix.

**Lock the camera down.** A tripod, a stack of books, anything. Handheld reads as unplanned unless you are deliberately following a motion.

**Shoot in the aspect ratio you will publish.** Cropping a horizontal video into a vertical frame throws away half your pixels and usually the subject's head. If a piece needs both orientations, frame wide enough to crop, and know that you are compromising both.

**Lock exposure and focus.** Tap and hold on your phone until it locks. Otherwise the camera hunts every time a hand enters the frame, and every take breathes.

**Roll long, and slate.** Say the shot number out loud at the top of each take. In the edit, forty unlabelled clips named `IMG_4471` are an hour of your life.

## The edit

A "simple edit" is a real deliverable, and it is five operations, in this order.

1. **Select.** Watch everything once, mark the good takes, throw the rest out of the timeline. Editing is choosing, and most beginners never discard enough.
2. **Assemble to the script.** Lay the selected shots in script order with rough cuts. Do not add anything yet.
3. **Tighten.** Trim the top and tail of every clip until the piece has no dead air. A 45-second target usually starts at 70 seconds and is cut down. Cutting is where the pace comes from.
4. **Add text and graphics.** On-screen text at the moment it is spoken, not two seconds late. Hold each line long enough to read twice at a natural pace — roughly one second per three words, with a two-second floor.
5. **Balance audio.** Voice at a consistent level, music well underneath it, room tone patching any gaps. If you can hear the music while someone is speaking, it is too loud.

Two rules about text on screen. Keep it out of the bottom 20% and top 10% of a vertical frame, because platform interfaces — captions, usernames, buttons — sit there and will cover it. And make it large: 48 pixels at 1080 width is a sensible floor, and high contrast against whatever is behind it. White text over a bright window is unreadable, so add a shadow, a scrim, or move the text.

**Music and licensing.** Use only music you have a licence for: your platform's built-in licensed library, a subscription library, or a track under a licence you have read. Popular commercial music is not usable, and "it's only a small account" is not a licence. The practical consequence of getting it wrong is a muted or removed video, sometimes months later, often on the asset that was working. Keep a one-line record of where every track came from.

## Accessibility, built in

Everything below is part of production, not a post-production favour. Each one is fast when planned and expensive when retrofitted.

**Captions.** Every video, every time. A large share of feed viewing happens with sound off, so captions are also a reach decision — but they exist first for viewers who are deaf or hard of hearing, and that is the standard to meet. Auto-generated captions are a starting point and are wrong about exactly the words that matter: names, numbers, and jargon. `Elena Ruiz` becomes `Elaine A. Reece`; `three inches` becomes `3 in.` Correct them.

Prefer to ship both: burned-in **open captions** for feeds that ignore caption files, and an uploaded caption file where the platform supports it. A caption file is plain text with timecodes:

```txt
1
00:00:03,000 --> 00:00:07,500
One tray grew with its light eighteen inches away.

2
00:00:07,500 --> 00:00:11,800
The other, three inches away.

3
00:00:12,000 --> 00:00:15,400
If your seedlings look like this - tall, thin, falling over -

4
00:00:15,400 --> 00:00:18,000
that is not too much water. That is not enough light.
```

Two lines maximum per caption, broken at natural phrase boundaries, and on screen long enough to read. Captions that flash three words at a time are technically present and practically useless.

**Transcripts.** A caption file is for watching; a transcript is for reading. Publish the full transcript on the page under the video, as text. It serves people who cannot or would rather not watch, it is searchable, and it costs you one copy-paste because you already wrote the script. Include the meaningful visual information in it — a transcript that reads "and then you do this" describes nothing.

**Do not carry meaning in colour or motion alone.** The two trays in the split screen are labelled "18 in away" and "3 in away," not distinguished by a red border and a green one.

**Alt text for static graphics.** A graphic gets alt text describing what it communicates, and if it contains data, the data belongs in the page as text or a table as well. "Chart showing seedling height by light distance" is a start; the actual numbers must exist somewhere a screen reader can reach.

**Avoid rapid flashing.** Strobing effects and hard flashes can trigger seizures. There is no creative reason worth that risk in a gardening video.

## Static graphics, briefly

The same brief-and-done discipline applies to a graphic, with three rules that keep a non-designer out of trouble: use the brand's existing colours and typefaces rather than inventing new ones; put one idea on the canvas; and check contrast between text and background — light grey on white is a design cliché and a legibility failure. If the graphic exists to carry numbers, ship the numbers as text too.

## Naming, versioning, and handoff

Production ends with files someone else has to find in eight months. Name them so they sort and describe themselves:

```txt
hl_leggy-seedlings_45s_9x16_v3_final.mp4
hl_leggy-seedlings_captions_en.srt
hl_leggy-seedlings_transcript.txt
hl_leggy-seedlings_thumb_1080x1920.jpg
hl_leggy-seedlings_split-screen_still.jpg

brand _ topic _ length _ aspect _ version _ status . ext
Lowercase, hyphens inside a field, underscores between fields,
no spaces, no "final_FINAL_v2_real".
```

Keep the project file and the raw footage somewhere backed up, not only on the laptop that edited it. The reshoot you cannot do is the one where the seedlings have already grown up. Where these files then live — the media library, its alt-text field, and the folder structure that keeps it usable — is lesson 05.

## Definition of done

A produced asset is done when all of these are true:

- It delivers the one idea from the brief, and nothing the brief excluded.
- It is within the length ceiling.
- It works with the sound off.
- Captions exist and have been corrected by a human; a transcript is published with it.
- Every spoken number appears on screen.
- Text sits inside the safe area and passes a contrast check.
- Music is licensed and the source is recorded.
- Files are named to the convention and backed up.
- Someone who is not you has watched it once on a phone, at arm's length, in a normally lit room.

That last check catches more problems than any of the others.

## Practice

Produce one short multimedia asset end to end, from a brief you write, using only equipment you already own. Use the brand from lessons 02 and 03.

**Part 1 — Format decision.** Pick a topic from your editorial calendar and justify the format in three sentences using the format table. Explicitly state what the piece would have been in the wrong format and why that would have been worse.

**Part 2 — Creative brief.** Write a full creative brief using every section of the worked example, including "must not" and the accessibility section. State the channel and the aspect ratio before anything else.

**Part 3 — Script.** Write a two-column A/V script with timecodes. Count your spoken words and show the arithmetic against 130–150 words per minute. If the count exceeds your length ceiling, cut the script, not the pace.

**Part 4 — Shot list.** Write a shot list with shot number, framing, planned takes, and notes. It must include room tone and must note the light source and time of day.

**Part 5 — Shoot.** Capture everything on the list. Lock exposure and focus, use an external mic if you have one, and shoot in your publishing aspect ratio.

**Part 6 — Edit.** Cut the piece in the five operations, in order. Record the running time after step 3 and after step 5 — if the piece did not get shorter between assembly and final, you did not tighten it.

**Part 7 — Accessibility.** Produce a corrected caption file in the timecoded format shown above, burn open captions into the export, and write the full transcript including the meaningful visual information. Then check: every spoken number on screen, no meaning carried by colour alone, text inside the safe area, contrast legible on a phone in daylight.

**Part 8 — Package and review.** Name every file to the convention. Run the nine-point definition of done as a checklist with a pass or fail on each line. Then have one person watch it on a phone with the sound off and write down the first thing they say — and note what you would change because of it.

**Deliverable — Production Pack.** The creative brief, the A/V script with the word-rate arithmetic, the shot list, the exported video, the caption file, the transcript, the file-naming list, the completed definition-of-done checklist, and the two-sentence note on the sound-off review.
