## Publishing is not distribution

A published URL is a shop with the lights on and no sign on the street. Something has to bring people to it.

Most content teams spend 90% of their effort making the thing and 10% getting it seen, then conclude the thing was not good enough. The ratio that works for a small team is closer to 60/40, and the 40 is not an afterthought bolted on at the end — it is planned before production starts, because knowing where a piece will travel changes what you shoot, what you write, and what you cut.

This lesson is about that plan: turning one substantial asset into a set of channel-native derivatives, and scheduling them across a window of weeks rather than dumping them all on publish day.

**Scope.** Platform-by-platform social strategy, community management, and social analytics belong to dm250. Email campaign writing, automation, segmentation, and deliverability belong to dm350 — here, email is only one of the surfaces a piece of content lands on, and it gets a paragraph. The link-tagging convention and the one-asset-to-four-channels adaptation you practised in dm201 are assumed; this lesson builds the **system** around them: what to derive, in what order, over how long, and when to stop.

## The asset is the research, not the article

The most useful reframe in content distribution: **the article is one expression of the work, not the work.**

Harvest Lane's week 1 flagship — "How Deep Should a Raised Garden Bed Be?" — cost about eleven hours: Elena's crop-by-crop depth verification, a photo shoot at the bench, Priya's draft, two edit passes. What that bought was not 1,100 words. It bought a **verified crop-depth dataset, a set of photographs, an expert's reasoning, and a clear answer to a question thousands of people type every January.**

An article uses maybe 40% of that. The rest is sitting there, already paid for.

That is what repurposing actually is. It is not "post the blog link on four networks." It is asking, for each channel, *what part of this research is the right shape for that place?*

## The repurposing map

Write it as a table, per flagship, before production. Every row names a source — the specific part of the research it comes from — so nobody has to re-do work, and effort is estimated so the map stays honest about capacity.

```txt
FLAGSHIP: "How Deep Should a Raised Garden Bed Be?"  (guide, 1,100 words)
Published: Jan 6 · Pillar: Getting it built right · Owner: you
Research paid for: crop-depth table (40 crops, Elena-verified),
bench photography, patio-vs-grass finding, the settling-inch fact.
```

| # | Derivative | Channel | Source in the research | Job | Effort | Owner | Date |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 40s vertical video: "10 inches is enough" | Vertical feeds + Shorts | Depth table top 5 rows + bench footage | Reach a scrolling first-timer | 4h | You | Jan 8 |
| 2 | Carousel: 6 crops, 6 depths | Image feeds | Depth table, 6 rows | Save-able reference | 1.5h | You | Jan 13 |
| 3 | Pin set: 4 vertical pins | Pinterest | Photos + table stills | Long-tail evergreen discovery | 1h | Priya | Jan 14 |
| 4 | Printable depth chart (PDF) | Site gated-free + email | Full 40-crop table | Subscriber capture; kitchen-wall use | 2h | You | Jan 20 |
| 5 | Newsletter feature, 180 words | Email | The settling-inch fact + one photo | Re-engage last season's buyers | 1h | You | Jan 21 |
| 6 | Three forum/community answers | Gardening communities | Patio-vs-grass finding | Credibility; genuine help | 45m | Elena | Ongoing |
| 7 | Product-page module: depth selector | harvestlane.com | Table rows for 12" vs 18" | Convert the comparing reader | 2h | You + dev | Jan 27 |
| 8 | Partner blurb, 120 words + chart | 2 garden-centre newsletters | Chart + summary answer | Borrowed audience | 1h | You | Feb 3 |
| 9 | Sales one-pager | Partner and wholesale | Chart + product specs | Enable the partner conversation | 1h | You | Feb 10 |
| 10 | Q2 refresh: add 8 crops | Original URL | New Elena verification | Keep the flagship the best answer | 3h | Priya | Apr |

Total derivative effort: about 17 hours spread over nine weeks, against eleven hours for the original. That ratio is the point. It also makes the map self-policing: if the derivative column adds up to more hours than you have, rows get cut before they get promised.

Notice row 10. A refresh of the original is a repurposing decision, not a maintenance chore — it is usually the highest-return row on the map, because it improves the asset that everything else points at.

Notice also what is *not* on the map: a LinkedIn post, a podcast episode, a TikTok trend edit. Harvest Lane's audience is not on LinkedIn in gardening mode, nobody on the team can sustain a podcast, and trend edits are a different production discipline. A map that lists every channel that exists is a wish list.

## Rules of adaptation

**Never post the same words twice.** A blog paragraph pasted into a feed reads as a wall and gets no reach; a caption pasted into an email gets deleted. Same facts, native format, every time.

**Each derivative must be useful on its own.** The failure state of repurposing is nine posts that all say "we published a thing, go read the thing." Row 2 above — six crops and six depths in a carousel — answers the question in the feed, for free, without a click. That feels like giving away the reason to visit. It is not: usefulness in the feed is what earns the save, the share, and the eventual visit, and a person who got a genuine answer from you remembers where it came from.

**Take one idea per derivative, not a summary.** A 40-second video that summarises an 1,100-word guide is a bad guide. A 40-second video that makes one claim well — "ten inches is enough" — is a good video.

**Respect the channel's native shape.** Vertical video is 9:16, a carousel's first card is a headline, a pin is tall and text-legible at thumbnail size, an email is one idea and one link. These are not preferences; a piece that fights its container underperforms regardless of quality.

**Add something the original does not have.** The newsletter feature leads with the settling-inch fact, which is a footnote in the guide. Regular readers who already read the guide still get value, and you learn which framing lands.

**Match effort to expected return.** Row 9's one-pager takes an hour and serves nine partner conversations a year. That is fine. If it took twelve hours it would not be.

### What not to repurpose

Not everything deserves a map. Skip derivatives when:

- **The piece is genuinely time-bound.** A frost-warning post has a two-week life. Distribute it hard, once, and let it go.
- **The research is thin.** If the article was assembled from other people's articles, there is nothing underneath it to atomise, and nine derivatives will make that obvious nine times.
- **The channel is not yours.** Publishing to a platform you have no audience on, to say you are on it, costs real hours and returns nothing. Earn a channel deliberately or skip it.
- **The piece did not work.** Distribute the original first, and if nobody engaged with the primary asset, find out why before multiplying it.

## The distribution schedule

The single most common distribution mistake is treating publish day as the whole campaign. Content that fires everything on day one gets one spike and then silence — and it wastes the fact that most channels have almost no audience overlap.

Spread the same asset across six weeks instead:

```txt
DISTRIBUTION WINDOW - "How deep should a raised bed be?"

WEEK 1  (publish)
  Mon 6   Publish the guide. Internal links added from 3 pages.
  Wed 8   Vertical video, feeds + Shorts. Link in caption/bio.
  Thu 9   Answer the two open community threads that already
          ask this question. Link only where it genuinely helps.

WEEK 2
  Mon 13  Carousel: 6 crops, 6 depths.
  Tue 14  Pin set live (4 pins, staggered over 4 days).
  Fri 17  Reply pass: every comment on the video answered.

WEEK 3
  Mon 20  Printable chart PDF live on the guide page.
  Tue 21  Newsletter feature to the full list.
  Thu 23  Repost the best-performing derivative so far, with a
          different opening line.

WEEK 4
  Mon 27  Product-page depth module ships.
  Wed 29  Short follow-up video answering the top comment
          ("what about on a patio?") - links back to the guide.

WEEKS 5-6
  Feb 3   Partner newsletter blurbs (2 garden centres).
  Feb 10  Sales one-pager to the wholesale team.
  Feb 12  Review: what worked, what to cut next time. (Lesson 07.)

EVERGREEN LOOP (set now, not later)
  Every Jan and Mar  Re-share the video and the chart. The audience
                     for "how deep" renews every season.
  Apr                Refresh with 8 more crops; re-distribute as new.
```

Three things make this schedule work. **Nothing publishes on the day it was made**, so there is always a finished derivative in the queue when a week goes badly. **Week 4's follow-up video comes from the comments**, which means distribution is generating the next piece of content. And **the evergreen loop is scheduled now**, while you are thinking about it — an evergreen asset that is shared once is a timely asset with extra steps.

## Surfaces, and what each is for

| Surface | Control | Best at | Harvest Lane's use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Your site** | Total | Converting people who are already looking | The guide, the calculator, the plant library |
| **Email** | High | Reaching people who already chose you | Monthly feature; seasonal reminders |
| **Social feeds** | Low | New reach, saves and shares | Video, carousels |
| **Visual discovery** | Low | Long-tail evergreen traffic | Pins that still pay out three seasons on |
| **Communities** | None | Credibility with a sceptical audience | Elena answering real threads |
| **Partners** | Negotiated | Borrowed audience with existing trust | Two garden-centre newsletters |
| **Paid amplification** | Bought | Accelerating something already working | Rare; and it is dm230's subject |

**On email.** It is the highest-return surface most content teams have, because the audience opted in. In this course it is only a place a piece of content lands: one feature per newsletter, one idea, one link, written natively rather than pasted. Everything else about email — list building, segmentation, automation, deliverability, and campaign design — is dm350's, and this course does not try to teach it in a paragraph.

**On communities.** The rule that keeps this from becoming spam: **answer the question completely in the thread, and link only if the link adds something the answer could not contain.** Elena's forum answers give the depth numbers outright and link to the guide only for the full 40-crop chart. A drive-by link with no answer attached gets removed, and deservedly.

**On partners.** The ask is small and specific: 120 words plus the chart image, with a link. Small asks get said yes to. Offer reciprocity, agree the date, and send the copy written and ready rather than asking a partner to write about you.

**On paid.** Amplify what is already working, never what you hope will. If a derivative earns nothing organically, budget will buy it impressions and not much else. The mechanics belong to dm230.

## Accessibility travels with the asset

Every derivative is a new publication, and each one has to be made accessible in its own container. This is where accessibility work is most often dropped, because the original was done properly and everyone assumes it carried over.

- **Captions must be re-checked per platform.** Burned-in open captions travel; uploaded caption files do not follow a re-upload. Auto-captions regenerate and are wrong again on names and numbers.
- **Alt text has to be written in each platform's own field.** Every major network has one, most people never open it, and the platform's automatic description ("Image may contain: plant, outdoor") is not a substitute.
- **A carousel of text images needs its text in the caption too.** If the words only exist as pixels inside the image, they do not exist for a screen reader, and they are not searchable anywhere.
- **The printable PDF needs real text**, not a scan or a flat export of a design canvas, with a heading structure and a table that is a table.
- **Link text in posts and emails should say where it goes.** "Link in bio" is a platform convention and acceptable; "click here" in an email is not.
- **Do not carry meaning in colour alone** in a carousel or a chart image.

Budget about fifteen minutes per derivative for this. It is cheaper than any of the alternatives, including the one where you quietly exclude part of your audience.

## Tagging, briefly

Every link you publish outside your own site should carry the tagging convention you established in dm201 — a consistent source, medium, and campaign, lowercase and hyphenated, with the campaign name identical across every derivative of one flagship so the whole window can be read as one thing. Never tag internal site-to-site links.

The one addition for repurposing work: use the content parameter to distinguish derivatives of the same flagship on the same channel — the January video and the March re-share of it — or you will not be able to tell a working asset from a well-timed one when you get to lesson 07.

## Failure modes

**Publish-and-pray.** The piece goes live, a link goes on one network, and the team moves to the next piece. This is the default state of most content operations and the reason most content operations conclude that content does not work.

**The copy-paste blast.** The same paragraph on five networks within an hour. It signals automation, performs badly everywhere, and burns the piece — you cannot credibly re-share something that already appeared verbatim.

**Repurposing thin material.** Nine derivatives of a piece with no original research is nine reminders that you had nothing to say.

**Front-loading everything on day one.** One spike, then nothing, and no queue for the week your writer is ill.

**Never re-sharing.** Almost nobody saw it the first time. Evergreen assets should be re-shared on a schedule, with a new opening line, forever.

**The map with no owner or date.** A repurposing table where every row says "TBD" is a list of things that will not happen.

## Practice

Take the piece you published in lesson 05 and give it a full distribution treatment. Use the same brand throughout.

**Part 1 — Name the research.** In one short paragraph, state what the flagship actually cost and what it bought — the data, the assets, the expertise, the finding. Then list the parts of it the published piece does *not* use.

**Part 2 — Repurposing map.** Build the map as a table with all eight columns used in this lesson: derivative, channel, source in the research, job, effort estimate, owner, date, and number. Minimum **six** derivatives across at least **four** distinct channels, one of which must be your own site or email. Include one refresh row for the original. Sum the effort column and state whether it fits your capacity from lesson 02 — if it does not, cut rows until it does and say which you cut.

**Part 3 — Exclusions.** Name at least two channels or derivative types you deliberately excluded, with the reason for each. At least one reason must be "we have no audience there" or "we cannot sustain it."

**Part 4 — Write three of them for real.** Produce the actual copy or asset for three derivatives on different channels. Not descriptions — the finished text, or the exported file. Each must be useful standing alone, must not reuse sentences from the original, and must add at least one thing the original does not have. Under each, write one sentence naming what you added and why.

**Part 5 — Six-week schedule.** Build the distribution window in the format used above: dated, with owners, spread across at least six weeks, including a comment or community engagement pass, at least one re-share of the best-performing derivative, and a scheduled evergreen loop with the months named.

**Part 6 — Accessibility pass on the derivatives.** For each of your three produced derivatives, document: captions (and whether they are burned in, uploaded, or both), the alt text written for each image in that platform's field, whether any meaning is carried by colour alone, and the link text used. Write the actual alt text, not a note that you will write it.

**Part 7 — Tagging.** Produce the tagged link for every derivative in your map, in one table, using a single documented convention. Distinguish derivatives that will appear more than once on the same channel. State explicitly which links must not be tagged and why.

**Part 8 — The pitch.** Write the actual outreach message you would send to one partner or community — under 150 words, naming what you are offering them and what you are asking for. It must be something a busy stranger would say yes to in thirty seconds.

**Deliverable — Distribution Pack.** One document containing: the research paragraph, the repurposing map with the effort arithmetic and capacity check, the exclusions with reasons, the three finished derivatives, the six-week dated schedule with the evergreen loop, the per-derivative accessibility documentation, the tagged link table, and the partner pitch.
