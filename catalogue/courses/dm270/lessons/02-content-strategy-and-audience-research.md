## What a content strategy actually decides

Ask ten marketers for their content strategy and eight will show you a calendar. A calendar is a schedule; it tells you *when* things go out. A strategy tells you *why those things and not other things*, and it is the document that lets you say no.

A content strategy answers five questions in writing:

1. **Who** are we making this for, specifically enough to picture one person?
2. **What problem of theirs** does each thing we publish solve?
3. **What business outcome** does solving that problem move?
4. **What are we deliberately not making**, and why?
5. **What can we actually sustain** with the people and hours we have?

Question four and question five are the ones teams skip, and they are the two that decide whether the strategy survives contact with a busy quarter. A strategy that does not exclude anything is not a strategy; it is a wish list with a font.

The running brand for this course is **Harvest Lane** (harvestlane.com), a direct-to-consumer company selling cedar raised-bed kits, bagged soil blends, and a seasonal seed subscription. Average order value is $142. The seed subscription is $9/month and is the profitable part of the business, because a bed is bought once and seeds are bought every spring. The marketing team is you, a part-time writer (Priya, ten hours a week), and a horticulturist who will answer questions but will not write anything (Elena Ruiz). The founder, Marcus Bell, would like "more content."

That is the constraint you are planning inside: roughly one substantial piece a week, plus derivatives. Every decision in this lesson has to survive that.

## Start at the business goal, not at the blog

Content work goes wrong at the first step more often than at any other. Someone starts with "what should we write about?" instead of "what does the business need to be different, and can content move it?"

Work down the chain in this order, and write each rung down:

```txt
Business goal        Grow seed-subscription revenue 40% by Q3
        ↓
Marketing objective  Convert more first-season bed buyers into subscribers
        ↓
Content goal         Be the resource a first-time grower uses in the
                     6 weeks BEFORE and the 12 weeks AFTER their first bed
        ↓
Content KPI          Subscription signups attributed to guide traffic;
                     returning-reader rate in the post-purchase window
        ↓
Pillars              The four problem areas that window contains
```

Notice what the chain rules out. Harvest Lane could write about ornamental landscaping, about greenhouse construction, about heirloom seed politics. All are interesting to gardeners. None of them sit in the window between "I am thinking about a bed" and "my first season is going badly." So they are out, and the strategy document says so explicitly.

Two honest caveats about this chain. First, content is rarely the sole cause of a business outcome, and a strategy that promises it is will be embarrassed later; the honest claim is contribution, not causation. Second, some goals should not be given to content at all — if the checkout flow loses 60% of carts, no article fixes that. Part of the job is saying "this is not a content problem."

## Where audience evidence comes from

Audience research is not a research budget. It is the discipline of writing down what people actually said instead of what you assume they think. Seven sources, ordered by value per hour of effort:

**1. Customer conversations.** Eight to twelve calls, twenty minutes each, with people who bought in the last ninety days. Three questions carry the whole interview: What was happening in your life the week you started looking? What did you try before this? What nearly stopped you? Do not ask what they want; people are bad at predicting their own behavior and excellent at describing their past.

**2. Support and sales records.** Every pre-sale question is a piece of content someone needed and could not find. Export ninety days of tickets and tag them by topic. Harvest Lane's support inbox turned out to be 31% "what soil do I need and how much," which nobody on the team had guessed.

**3. Reviews — yours and competitors'.** Three-star reviews are the most useful text on the internet, because they name a specific trade-off instead of a mood. Two-star reviews of competitor beds gave Harvest Lane the phrase "the corners racked after one winter," which is now a product-page proof point.

**4. Search demand.** You did this work in dm201: keyword research and search-intent classification. Bring the output here as an input. The difference in emphasis matters — dm201 asks "how do I make this page rank for that query," dm270 asks "does enough of the audience have this question that it is worth writing at all, and what does the phrasing tell me about who is asking?" Search data is the only source here that tells you about *volume*; everything else tells you about *depth*.

**5. On-site search and analytics behavior.** What people type into your own search box is a list of things they expected you to have. Harvest Lane's top internal query was "zone chart," and the site had no zone chart.

**6. Community listening.** Subreddits, Facebook groups, forum threads, YouTube comments on competitor videos. Read for the arguments people have with each other; disagreement marks the places where the answer is genuinely unclear, and an unclear answer is a content opportunity.

**7. Sales-team and frontline anecdote.** Useful, and the least reliable of the seven, because it over-weights the loudest recent customer. Treat it as a source of hypotheses to check against the other six, never as the finding itself.

The rule that keeps this from becoming a reading exercise: **collect verbatim quotes, not summaries.** "Customers are confused about soil" is your paraphrase and it is useless. "I bought three bags because the website said cubic feet and the bags said quarts" is a customer's sentence, and it contains the problem, the audience's vocabulary, and half the headline.

## Turning evidence into a problem inventory

After research you will have somewhere between forty and two hundred fragments. The next step is mechanical and takes an afternoon.

Put every quote on its own row. Add three columns: the **problem** it reveals, the **moment** it happens in, and the **audience segment** it came from. Then sort by problem and count.

An abridged slice of Harvest Lane's inventory:

| Verbatim | Problem | Moment | Segment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| "I bought three bags because the site said cubic feet and the bags said quarts" | Cannot compute soil volume | Buying, week 0 | First-timer |
| "How deep does it actually need to be for carrots?" | Does not know depth requirements | Pre-purchase research | First-timer |
| "Everything bolted in July and I don't know why" | Cannot diagnose a failing plant | Season, week 10 | First-timer |
| "I put it where it looked nice, not where the sun was" | Chose a bad site | Assembly, week 1 | First-timer |
| "The corners racked after one winter" (competitor review) | Fears the product will not last | Comparison | Both |
| "I want to do a second bed but taller for my mom's back" | Accessibility / mobility need | Repeat purchase | Returning |
| "Do I have to buy new seeds every year?" | Does not understand the subscription | Post-purchase | First-timer |

Count the rows per problem and you have a demand-ranked list that came from people rather than from a brainstorm. Harvest Lane's top five by frequency were: soil volume math (19), plant-choice-by-zone (17), diagnosing a struggling plant (15), where to put the bed (11), and whether the cedar lasts (9).

Now cross-check frequency against **business value**. "Whether the cedar lasts" appeared only nine times but sits directly in front of a $189 purchase decision, so it outranks its count. "Diagnosing a struggling plant" happens in week ten, long after the sale — but it is exactly the moment a first-timer decides whether gardening is for them, which is the moment that decides the seed subscription. That is how a post-purchase problem earns a place in a strategy aimed at revenue.

## From problems to content pillars

A **content pillar** is a durable problem area that you commit to owning. Not a topic and not a keyword — a problem, stated from the audience's side, that you will still be publishing against in two years.

Good pillars have five properties:

- **Stated as the audience's problem**, not your product category. "Getting the soil right" is a pillar; "Soil products" is a shelf.
- **Deep enough for 15+ pieces** without repeating yourself. If you exhaust it in four posts it is a topic inside a pillar.
- **Tied to a business outcome** you can name.
- **Distinct** from the other pillars — if two pillars keep producing the same article, they are one pillar.
- **Ownable.** There is some reason a reader would come to *you* for this rather than to the nine sites already answering it. For Harvest Lane, the reason is Elena and the physical product: they can show a real bed in a real season.

Three to five pillars is right for most teams. Two is too coarse to guide anything. Seven means you are describing an industry rather than a strategy.

### Harvest Lane's pillar table

| Pillar | Audience problem, in their words | Business outcome | Journey stage | Primary formats | Lead channel |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Getting it built right** | "I don't know where to put it or how deep it should be" | Bed kit sales; fewer returns | Awareness → Decision | How-to guide, short video, checklist | Organic search |
| **Getting the soil right** | "I bought three bags and needed six" | Soil attach rate on bed orders | Decision | Calculator, guide, comparison | Organic search |
| **What to plant, where you live** | "I don't know what survives in zone 6" | Seed subscription signups | Awareness → Retention | Zone-filtered plant library, seasonal calendar | Organic search + email |
| **When it goes wrong** | "Everything bolted in July and I don't know why" | Retention; second-season repurchase | Retention | Diagnostic guide, short video, Q&A | Email + community |

Read across the rows and the strategy is legible: two pillars sell the first purchase, one sells the recurring revenue, one keeps people gardening long enough to have a second season. Elena's expertise is load-bearing in pillars three and four, which is why those are the ones a competitor cannot copy in an afternoon.

### What is deliberately excluded

Write the exclusions down in the same document, with the reason:

- **Ornamental and flower gardening** — large audience, wrong audience; it does not lead to a vegetable bed or a seed subscription.
- **Greenhouse and season-extension builds** — a real customer request, but the answer is a product Harvest Lane does not sell.
- **Composting** — genuinely adjacent and genuinely popular; excluded for one quarter only, because with ten writer-hours a week a fifth pillar would starve the other four. Revisit in Q3.
- **Company news and team posts** — nobody in the problem inventory asked.

The composting line is the most useful kind of exclusion, because it names a capacity constraint rather than pretending the topic is bad. That is the sentence that survives a meeting with Marcus.

## Choosing the format and the channel

Once a topic is inside a pillar, three decisions remain, and they are decisions rather than defaults.

**Format follows the shape of the answer.** If the answer is a number the reader computes, it is a calculator or a table, not eight hundred words of prose. If the answer is a physical motion, it is a video. If the answer is a judgment call with conditions, it is a decision tree or a comparison table. Harvest Lane's soil problem had been a 1,400-word article that nobody finished; the answer was always a calculator with three inputs.

**Length follows the question, not a quota.** "How deep should a raised bed be?" deserves a direct answer in the first sentence and then depth-by-crop detail for the people who need it. A word-count target is how you get padding, and padding is the most common reason a reader leaves.

**Channel follows where the question gets asked.** A question typed into a search engine is answered on a page you own. A question that only occurs to someone while scrolling is answered where they scroll. Search-demand topics are the ones that keep paying out for years; social-native topics spike and decay. A healthy plan has both, weighted toward search when your business depends on people who are actively looking.

Do not decide the derivative formats yet. Repurposing one asset across channels is lesson 06's subject, and deciding it now tends to warp the primary piece into something that serves no channel well.

## Auditing what already exists

Before planning new work, count what you have. Most teams are one merge away from a much better library.

Export every URL, and score each one on two axes: does it serve a pillar, and does it do its job? That produces four verdicts.

| Verdict | Criterion | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Keep** | In a pillar, current, performing | Leave it; schedule a review date |
| **Update** | In a pillar, stale or thin | Rewrite in place — same URL |
| **Merge** | Two or more pieces answering the same question | Combine into the strongest URL, redirect the others |
| **Retire** | Serves no pillar, no traffic, no purpose | Remove and redirect to the nearest useful page |

Harvest Lane's audit of 63 URLs: 22 keep, 14 update, 19 merge into 6, and 8 retire. Note the arithmetic — 63 URLs became 49, and the team's next quarter was better spent on the 14 updates than on 14 new posts, because an update inherits everything the URL has already earned. **Updating usually beats publishing.** It is also the least glamorous line in a plan and the first one cut, which is why it belongs in the calendar as scheduled work rather than as intention.

## Capacity: the calendar you can staff

Ten writer-hours a week is roughly one 1,500-word researched guide, or two shorter pieces, minus the weeks where Priya is on holiday. Add your own time for briefs, edits, publishing, and distribution, and the honest capacity is:

```txt
Per 12-week quarter, Harvest Lane can produce:
  6  new flagship pieces        (guide, calculator, or video-led)
  4  updates to existing URLs
  2  buffer weeks               (illness, reshoots, a founder request)
 --
 12  slots

NOT 12 new pieces. The buffer is the plan, not the slack in it.
```

Teams that plan twelve new pieces publish nine, none of them updated, and the buffer gets taken out of the editing pass. Plan the buffer explicitly and the quality holds.

## The editorial calendar

Now, and only now, the calendar. It is the output of everything above, not the starting point. Harvest Lane's Q1 runs January through March — the planning-and-building season for zones 5 to 8, when a first-timer is deciding whether this is the year.

| Wk | Publish | Pillar | Working title | Format | Owner | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Jan 6 | Getting it built right | How Deep Should a Raised Garden Bed Be? | Guide + depth table | Priya | Published |
| 2 | Jan 13 | Getting the soil right | Raised Bed Soil Calculator | Calculator + short guide | You + dev | In build |
| 3 | Jan 20 | What to plant | *Update:* Zone 5–8 Planting Calendar | Update in place | Priya | Drafting |
| 4 | Jan 27 | Getting it built right | Where to Put a Raised Bed: Sun, Water, Access | Guide + 60s video | Priya | Brief sent |
| 5 | Feb 3 | — | *Buffer* | — | — | — |
| 6 | Feb 10 | What to plant | What to Start Indoors in February (Zone 6) | Seasonal guide | Priya | Brief sent |
| 7 | Feb 17 | Getting the soil right | *Update:* Soil Blends Compared | Update in place | You | Not started |
| 8 | Feb 24 | Getting it built right | Assembling a Cedar Bed in 25 Minutes | Video-led + transcript | You + Elena | Pre-production |
| 9 | Mar 3 | When it goes wrong | Why Seedlings Get Leggy — and the Fix | Guide + 45s video | Priya | Not started |
| 10 | Mar 10 | — | *Buffer* | — | — | — |
| 11 | Mar 17 | What to plant | *Merge:* 4 tomato posts → Tomatoes in a Raised Bed | Merge + redirects | Priya | Not started |
| 12 | Mar 24 | When it goes wrong | *Update:* Frost Dates and What to Do About Them | Update in place | You | Not started |

Four things make this a working calendar rather than a wall decoration. Every row names a pillar, so a topic with no pillar has nowhere to sit. Updates and merges occupy real slots with real owners. The buffers are rows, not gaps. And the seasonality is deliberate — the indoor-seed-starting piece publishes in mid-February because that is when zone 6 gardeners start seeds, not when it was convenient to write.

One column deliberately absent: distribution. That belongs on the distribution plan in lesson 06, and cramming it in here makes the calendar unreadable at exactly the moment it needs to be scanned in ten seconds.

## The content brief

The brief is where strategy becomes an instruction. Without one you will get a draft that is about the right subject and answers the wrong question, and the fix costs more than the writing did.

A brief that works has these fields. Here is the real one Priya received for week 1:

```txt
CONTENT BRIEF — Harvest Lane
Working title:   How Deep Should a Raised Garden Bed Be?
Pillar:          Getting it built right
Owner:           Priya          Reviewer: Elena (facts), you (copy)
Due:             Draft Dec 30 · Review Jan 2 · Publish Jan 6

READER
Who:             First-time vegetable grower, zones 5-8, has decided
                 they want a bed and is now sizing it.
Moment:          Pre-purchase, sitting with a tape measure or a
                 product page open in another tab.
What they know:  Nothing about root depth. Knows what a carrot is.
What they fear:  Building the wrong thing and wasting $200.

THE JOB
One question:    How deep does my bed need to be for what I want to grow?
Answer up top:   "Ten to twelve inches suits almost everything most
                 people grow. Go deeper only for carrots, parsnips and
                 potatoes." Say it in the first 40 words. Do not
                 make the reader scroll for the answer.
Business goal:   Bed kit sales. Secondary: soil attach rate.
Journey stage:   Decision.

MUST ANSWER (from the problem inventory + support tickets)
  1. Minimum depth for common crops — table, by crop, in inches.
  2. Does depth change if it sits on grass vs. on a patio?
  3. Can I make it deeper later? (Yes — stackable tiers.)
  4. Does deeper mean more soil? (Yes — link the calculator, wk 2.)

MUST INCLUDE
  Format:        Guide, ~1,100 words. Depth table is the centrepiece.
  Evidence:      Elena reviews every depth figure before publish.
                 No number ships without her initials in the doc.
  Proof:         One photo of a 12" bed with the soil line visible.
  Internal:      Link to the bed kit page (12" and 18" tiers) and to
                 the soil calculator once it exists (wk 2 — add on
                 publish day, do not wait).
  CTA:           One only. "See the 12-inch cedar kit." Not a
                 newsletter signup, not both.

ACCESSIBILITY (not optional, not a final step)
  - Depth table is a real HTML table with header cells, not an image
    of a table. A screenshot of a spreadsheet is unreadable to a
    screen reader and unusable on a phone.
  - Alt text on the bed photo describes the measurement being shown:
    "Cross-section of a cedar raised bed with a ruler showing a
    12-inch soil depth."
  - Headings run H2 > H3 in order. No skipping to style a line.
  - Link text says where it goes: "see the 12-inch cedar kit",
    never "click here".

OUT OF SCOPE
  Bed materials and wood comparison (separate piece, Q2).
  Soil volume math (wk 2 calculator — link, do not duplicate).
  Anything about greenhouses.

SUCCESS LOOKS LIKE
  A reader with a tape measure gets their answer in 15 seconds and
  can still find the crop-by-crop detail if they want it.
```

Three things distinguish this from a topic assignment. It states the answer, so the writer is not researching a conclusion the team already holds. It names what is out of scope, which is what stops a 1,100-word guide becoming 2,600. And it puts accessibility in the brief, where it costs nothing, instead of in a review pass, where it costs a rebuild.

## Failure modes to recognize

**The pillar that is a product category.** "Soil Products" produces catalog copy. "Getting the soil right" produces a calculator, a comparison, and a diagnostic. Same subject, completely different output.

**The strategy with no exclusions.** If nothing is ruled out, every founder request is in scope by default, and the calendar becomes a queue of other people's ideas.

**Research theatre.** A persona document with a stock photo, an invented name, and a paragraph about the persona's hobbies, built from nobody's actual words. If you cannot trace a claim in your strategy back to a quote, a ticket, or a query, it is decoration.

**Volume as the plan.** "Three posts a week" is a production target masquerading as a strategy. It reliably produces thin content and an exhausted writer, and it is the fastest way to build a library you will spend next year merging.

**The unfinished chain.** A calendar full of good topics with no line back to a business outcome. It survives until the first budget review.

## Practice

Build a content strategy for a real organization — your employer, a client, a local business, or a fictional one you define in a paragraph. Use one organization throughout.

**Part 1 — The goal chain.** Write the five-rung chain: business goal, marketing objective, content goal, content KPI, pillars. One line each. The content goal must name a specific audience and a specific window of time in their life.

**Part 2 — Research.** Gather evidence from at least **four** of the seven sources. At minimum: three real conversations (twenty minutes, the three questions from this lesson), a review scan of at least twenty reviews including competitors', ninety days of support or sales questions if you can get them, and your dm201 keyword output. Record **at least 30 verbatim quotes**. Paraphrases do not count.

**Part 3 — Problem inventory.** Build the four-column table (verbatim, problem, moment, segment) for all 30+ quotes. Sort by problem and count. Then write two sentences on where frequency and business value disagree, and which one you are following.

**Part 4 — Pillars.** Define three to five pillars in the six-column table format from this lesson. Then test each against the five properties. Any pillar that fails "deep enough for 15+ pieces" gets merged into another; any pillar you cannot tie to a business outcome gets cut.

**Part 5 — Exclusions.** List at least four things you are deliberately not making, each with a one-line reason. At least one reason must be a capacity constraint rather than a relevance judgment.

**Part 6 — Audit.** Inventory the existing content (if there is none, state that and skip to Part 7). Assign every URL one of keep / update / merge / retire, and report the counts and the resulting URL total.

**Part 7 — Capacity and calendar.** State your honest weekly production capacity and show the arithmetic that turns it into a slot count for one quarter. Then build a 12-week editorial calendar with the columns used in this lesson. It must include at least two updates or merges and at least one buffer week, and the sequencing must reflect your audience's seasonality or buying cycle — write one sentence defending the placement of any two rows.

**Part 8 — One brief.** Write a complete content brief for week 1 of your calendar, using every field in the worked example, including the accessibility section and the out-of-scope section. State the answer in the brief.

**Deliverable — Content Strategy Document.** One file containing: the goal chain, the problem inventory table, the pillar table, the exclusion list with reasons, the audit counts, the capacity arithmetic, the 12-week calendar, and the week 1 brief. A reviewer should be able to trace any pillar in your table back to a specific quote in your inventory.
