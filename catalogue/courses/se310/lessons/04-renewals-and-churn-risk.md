## Churn is a slow event that looks sudden

Reps describe lost renewals as ambushes. "They were happy in January and gone in March." They were not happy in January; nobody was looking properly. Cancellation is a decision made over months by people who were, throughout, perfectly pleasant to you, and it becomes visible on a specific day only because that is the day someone has to sign something.

So the job splits in two. First, detect: build a repeatable read on every account that does not depend on how you felt after the last call. Second, convert what you detected into a conversation that a customer will engage with — which means arriving with evidence rather than with a quote and an argument.

Both halves depend on a third thing that is not glamorous. What you know about an account has to be written down where your company can see it, in a form that makes the forecast true. A renewal you privately doubt but publicly forecast at 90% is not optimism; it is a number your company will hire against.

## Signals worth scoring

The signals below are the ones a technical sales rep can see without a health-score product and without asking anyone's permission. They fall into four groups, and mixing the groups is the point — accounts rarely fail for one reason, and a single amber signal is usually noise.

**Adoption signals** — the ones that predict the seat count.

- Active users as a share of licensed seats, and the direction of travel over the last quarter.
- Adoption *by team or region*, never just the average. One region at zero is the shape most true-downs take.
- Depth of use: are they using the two or three capabilities they bought it for, or only the easiest one?
- Workarounds. A parallel spreadsheet is an adoption failure that the usage numbers will report as success.

**Relationship signals** — the ones that predict who will argue for you in the room you are not in.

- Champion responsiveness: two consecutive touchpoints rescheduled, or answered by a delegate.
- Champion status: promoted, moved, resigned, or newly reporting to someone who did not choose you.
- Executive coverage: whether anyone above your champion has heard a result from you in the last six months.
- The blocker's position: has the person who opposed the purchase gained authority?

**Value signals** — the ones that decide whether the price is defensible.

- Whether the agreed measures are still being produced at all. Nobody producing them means nobody believes them.
- Actuals against the baselines, including the ones that are behind.
- Whether the original business problem is still the customer's priority. Problems get solved, deprioritized, or reorganized away, and a product attached to a problem nobody cares about anymore is cancelled without malice.

**Commercial and organizational signals** — the ones with the shortest fuse.

- A spend review, budget freeze, hiring freeze, acquisition, or new CFO.
- Procurement contacting you before you contacted them.
- Invoice queries, late payment, or a request to change billing terms mid-term.
- Any mention of an alternative tool, a pilot elsewhere in the business, or a parent company's standard.
- A request to shorten the term, go month-to-month, or "just do a short extension while we think."

That last one deserves its own line. A short extension feels like a win because nothing was lost today. It is usually the customer buying time to run a process you have not been invited to.

## The scorecard

A scorecard exists so that your judgement is applied consistently across twenty accounts instead of vividly to the two you thought about this week. Score each account monthly, in fifteen minutes, from evidence you can point at.

Each factor scores **0 (healthy), 1 (watch), or 2 (at risk)**, multiplied by a weight. Maximum is 40.

| # | Factor | Weight | 0 — healthy | 1 — watch | 2 — at risk | Where the evidence comes from |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Overall active seat utilization | 3 | 80%+ and flat or rising | 60–79%, or falling | Under 60%, or fell 10+ points in a quarter | Usage report |
| 2 | Worst team or region | 3 | No team under 60% | One team 30–59% | Any team under 30% | Usage report, broken out |
| 3 | Depth of use | 2 | Using all bought capabilities | One unused | Using one capability of several | Usage report, account notes |
| 4 | Workarounds | 2 | None known | One tolerated | A parallel system in daily use | Account review notes |
| 5 | Champion engagement | 3 | Responsive, two-way, plans ahead | Slower, delegates some contact | Two touchpoints missed, or gone quiet | Your own calendar and inbox |
| 6 | Champion stability | 2 | Same person, same role | Role changed or new manager above | Departed, or leaving | Account notes, public profile changes |
| 7 | Executive coverage | 3 | Signer heard a result in last 6 months | Met once, over 6 months ago | Never met, or only met during the sale | Account record |
| 8 | Value evidence | 3 | Measures current, mostly on target | Measures stale or partly missing | No measures produced since kickoff | Value scorecard |
| 9 | Original problem still a priority | 2 | Explicitly confirmed this quarter | Unclear | Reorganized, deprioritized, or solved elsewhere | Account review notes |
| 10 | Commercial and organizational events | 3 | None known | One soft signal | Spend review, freeze, acquisition, or procurement-initiated contact | Account review notes, news, invoices |
| 11 | Competitive presence | 2 | None mentioned | Mentioned casually | Named alternative, pilot, or parent-company standard | Account review notes |
| 12 | Commercial requests | 2 | None | Term or billing questions | Shortening, month-to-month, or seat reduction requested | Inbox, procurement |

**Bands:** 0–7 green, 8–15 amber, 16–24 red, 25+ critical.

Two rules keep it honest. Score from evidence you can cite, not from your mood — if you cannot name the source, the factor is a 1 by default, because not knowing is itself a risk. And an account is never green if factor 7 is a 2; an account whose signer has never heard from you is one budget review away from a surprise regardless of how well it is being used.

## Scoring two accounts

**Tallgrass Facilities Group** — the account from lessons 2 and 3, in August, seven months in, renewal 5 January, 400 seats at $23.75 per seat per month.

| Factor | Score | Weighted | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Utilization | 1 | 3 | 68% active and flat |
| 2 Worst region | 2 | 6 | West at 11% — crews reverted to paper |
| 3 Depth | 1 | 2 | Scheduling and dispatch used; billing hand-off only in East and Central |
| 4 Workarounds | 2 | 4 | West spreadsheet keyed by Ruth, ~4 hrs/week |
| 5 Champion engagement | 0 | 0 | Dana responsive, met 14 Aug, gave candid answers |
| 6 Champion stability | 0 | 0 | Same role since kickoff |
| 7 Executive coverage | 1 | 3 | Marcus Bell met at day 45; nothing since |
| 8 Value evidence | 0 | 0 | Late-billed call-outs 12.4% to 4.1%, current |
| 9 Problem still a priority | 0 | 0 | Confirmed in the August review |
| 10 Commercial events | 2 | 6 | Software spend review in September, utilization is an input |
| 11 Competitive presence | 1 | 2 | Ray Nowak "talking to someone about a different scheduling app" |

That total is uncomfortable for an account whose headline results are good, and that is the scorecard doing its job. The value evidence is excellent and irrelevant to the mechanism that will actually harm this account: a September spend review whose central input is a utilization number dragged down by one region of 130 technicians. The arithmetic the customer will do is not complicated, and you should do it first — 130 seats at $23.75 a month is **$37,050 a year**, just under a third of the contract, available to Marcus as a saving with no argument required.

Contrast **Harbourline Group**, a quieter account eleven months in, renewal 1 October.

| 2 Worst region | 0 | 0 | Lowest team 78% |
| 3 Depth | 0 | 0 | All three modules in use |

Harbourline is the account every rep calls a safe renewal, because the usage is beautiful and nobody is complaining. There is nobody left who will argue for it: the champion has moved on, the signer has never met you, and no evidence of value has been produced in eight months. It scores worse than the account with a visible problem, and it deserves to.

Notice the different remedies. Tallgrass needs a product fix, a region rescued, and a meeting with Marcus before September. Harbourline needs a new champion and a value story rebuilt from scratch, starting with whoever inherited the problem — and it needs both within weeks, because it renews in six.

## From score to action

| Band | Cadence | Required actions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Green (0–7) | Quarterly review | Keep the measures current; confirm the executive relationship exists |
| Amber (8–15) | Monthly contact | Name the one factor driving the score; one dated action against it; tell your manager |
| Red (16–24) | Fortnightly | Written recovery plan with owners and dates on both sides; open the renewal conversation early; forecast reflects the risk |
| Critical (25+) | Weekly | Escalate internally with a named executive sponsor; recovery plan agreed *with the customer*, not just about them |

The one thing that must never happen is a score that changes nothing. A scorecard used as a reporting exercise is worse than no scorecard, because it manufactures the feeling of having handled something.

## Evidence before the conversation

By the time you open a renewal you should be carrying four things, and none of them is a price.

1. **The value scorecard** — the measures agreed at kickoff, their baselines, and the current actuals, with the misses stated and explained. Sourced from the customer's own systems wherever possible; a number out of their finance system ends arguments that a number out of your dashboard starts.
2. **The adoption picture, broken out** — by team, with the weak spot named before they name it, and with what has been done about it.
3. **The risk register with your side of it closed** — every issue they raised in reviews, what happened to it, and what is still open with a date. This is the document that makes you credible, because it proves you wrote things down and acted.
4. **The mechanics** — exact end date, notice period, auto-renewal language, who signs now (often not who signed before), and when their budget is set. se201 places these on the renewal runway; the point here is that you must know them before you speak, because a customer who tells you your own notice period has already out-prepared you.

Two habits that make the difference. **Close the gaps before you present them:** a miss with a fix already underway is a story about a working partnership, while the same miss presented cold is a story about a vendor who did not notice. And **write the one-page version first.** If your value story does not survive being one page, it is not a value story; it is a slide deck.

## Running the conversation

Open before their budget closes, and open on value. A workable shape for the first renewal meeting:

**Recap the promise.** Read back what they bought and why, in their words. Sixty seconds. It resets the frame from "renewing a subscription" to "continuing a thing we started for a reason."

**Present the evidence, misses first.** Leading with what did not land buys you the credibility to be believed about what did. "Two of the four measures are ahead, one is close, and one we have not moved — here is why, and here is what we are doing."

**Ask, then stop.** "Given all that — how are you thinking about next year?" Then silence. The answer is the most valuable thing in the meeting, and it will be a great deal more honest than an answer to "so, are we good to renew?"

**Deal with what they say, not with what you prepared.** If they raise the spend review, the meeting is now about the spend review.

**Close on a next step with a date and a name.** Not "I'll send something over." Who does what, by when, and when you speak next.

Four moments recur, and each has a wrong answer that feels natural.

- **"We need to look at the price."** The natural answer is a discount. The better first move is to find out what changed — a budget cut, a competing quote, a value story that never got made. Price objections in renewals are frequently value objections wearing a suit, and se203 covers what to do once you genuinely are negotiating.
- **"We're evaluating alternatives."** The natural answer is to defend. Better: ask what triggered the evaluation, who is running it, what the criteria are, and when it concludes. Then treat the renewal as a new sale, because it is one.
- **"We want to reduce seats."** The natural answer is to argue. Better: agree to look at the utilization together, and go region by region. A true-down you can see coming is often recoverable in part; one you contest without data is not.
- **"Can we just extend for three months?"** The natural answer is relief. Better: ask what the three months are for, and what has to be true at the end of them. If there is no answer, there is a process happening that you are not in.

## Keeping the record honest

Everything above only works at the scale of a book of accounts if it is written down where other people can act on it. Your CRM is where that happens, and this course consumes it rather than configures it — se301 teaches the mechanics of stages, pipelines, and forecast reports. What belongs to you is the discipline of what goes in.

Four things must be true of every account you own.

**The renewal opportunity exists, dated correctly.** Every contract has a renewal opportunity in the system from the day it starts, dated to the *decision* date — the notice deadline, not the contract end date. A renewal that appears in the system six weeks before it closes has already been invisible to your company for eleven months.

**The amount is what you actually expect, not what is on the contract today.** If West is going to true down, the expected amount reflects that, with a note saying why. Reps carry the full amount to avoid an awkward conversation with their manager, and thereby guarantee a much worse one later.

**The stage and forecast category are backed by an event, not a feeling.** Every stage change should be traceable to something that happened: a meeting held, a proposal sent, a verbal agreement given, a signature path confirmed. "I think it's going well" is not an event. The useful internal test before you move a stage is: *what would I show someone who asked me why?*

**The risk is visible.** The churn score, the band, the driving factor, and the next dated action belong on the account record, updated when you score it. Notes from each review — the Heard, Concluded, Committed format from lesson 3 — go in the same place, dated, with commitments recorded on both sides.

Three habits corrupt the retention picture, all of them common and none of them malicious. **Sandbagging the risk** — keeping a doomed renewal at full value because it is early and something might change. **Stale close dates** — pushing a date out a month at a time so the deal never technically slips. **Notes in your own head, or in your own notebook** — which means that when you are on leave in the week the customer calls, nobody can help them, and when you leave the company the account's entire history leaves with you.

The test to apply to your own accounts is simple and unforgiving: **if you were unreachable for two weeks, could a colleague open this account record and know what is true, what is at risk, and what was promised?** If not, the record is not maintained, whatever the fields say.

## Practice

Use **Selkirk Environmental**, a waste and recycling contractor. Contract: 220 seats, $27.50 per seat per month, twelve-month term ending 30 April, 60-day notice period, auto-renews unless notice is given. Today is 12 November.

What you know:

- Active seats: 214 in month two, 149 today. The Northern depot (62 seats) is at 9% after their depot manager was replaced in August.
- They bought it to cut missed collections, which were running at 3.1% of scheduled jobs. You have no current figure; the report that produced the baseline was owned by an analyst who left in June.
- Your champion, Kwame Aboagye, has rescheduled the last two reviews and sent an analyst to the one before that.
- The contract was signed by the Finance Director, Helen Ruskin. You have never met her.
- In September, accounts payable queried the invoice for the first time, asking for a breakdown by depot.
- At the last review the analyst mentioned the group "is standardising on one platform for logistics next year," and did not elaborate.
- No competitor has been named. Nobody has complained about the product.

1. **Score the account** against all twelve factors. Show weighted scores, the total, the band, and for each factor the specific evidence you scored from. Where you have no evidence, apply the default rule and say so.
2. **Name the mechanism.** In three sentences, state how this account most plausibly ends up cancelled, and calculate the annualized revenue at risk if the seat count is trued down to current active usage. Show the arithmetic.
3. **Write the recovery plan** required by the band you assigned: every action with an owner, a date, and what it is meant to change. It must address the Northern depot, the missing value evidence, the champion, and the fact that you have never met Helen Ruskin. Order it by what has to happen first given the notice date.
4. **Rebuild the value evidence.** The analyst who owned the baseline has left. Propose how you would produce a defensible before-and-after figure for missed collections in under three weeks, naming the source, and state which part of it a sceptical Finance Director would attack.
5. **Plan the renewal conversation.** Write the opening ninety seconds verbatim, the one question you will ask before going silent, and your prepared response to each of these three: "we're looking at consolidating onto the group platform," "we only need about 150 seats," and "can we do a three-month extension while the group decides?"
6. **Fix the record.** Write out exactly what you would change on the account and renewal opportunity today: close date, expected amount with the reasoning, stage and the event that backs it, the churn score and driving factor, and the next dated action. Then answer in two sentences what your manager's forecast would have said about Selkirk before you touched it, and what it says now.
