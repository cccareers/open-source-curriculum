## Growth inside an account is a diagnosis, not a pitch

There is a version of expansion selling that consists of asking a happy customer whether they would like to buy more things. It works occasionally, damages the relationship regularly, and teaches customers that your reviews are sales meetings in disguise — after which they send an analyst instead of attending themselves, and you lose the listening channel that lesson 3 spent three hours building.

The version that works is the same consultative motion you use on a new prospect, run on a customer you already know a great deal about. You find a problem the customer has, you establish that it is worth solving, you check honestly whether your product actually solves it, and you propose it at a moment when the customer has reason to believe you. The only real differences from a new sale are that discovery is cheaper — you have been in their operation for months — and that the cost of getting it wrong is higher, because you are spending a relationship you need at renewal.

This lesson comes after renewals on purpose. Selling more to an account that is quietly failing is the most expensive mistake in account management, and it is common precisely because the accounts that give you time are often the ones drifting.

## Where the signals actually come from

Expansion opportunities are not discovered by reading a price list. They arrive in four places, and three of them are things you already do.

**Usage patterns.** Teams hitting a limit, volumes trending toward a tier boundary, a capability used far beyond its expected scope, or a department that has quietly started using seats that were bought for someone else. Usage tells you where demand exists before anyone asks for anything.

**Unsolved pain heard in reviews.** This is the richest source and the most wasted. In every account review a customer describes a problem your product does not currently touch. Most reps either say nothing or immediately pitch. The correct third option is to write it down under a heading that means "adjacent problem, not yet qualified," and come back to it.

**Organizational change.** A new region, a new depot, an acquisition, a new team inheriting a similar process, a new executive with a mandate. Change creates budget and creates permission to reconsider how something is done.

**Their own stated plans.** "We're taking on the northern contract in April." Customers narrate their future constantly in the middle of conversations about something else. Lesson 3's habit of counting future tense pays here as well as at renewal.

Two vocabulary notes so the conversation with your manager is precise. An **upsell** is more of what they already have or a higher tier of it — seats, volume, a premium edition. A **cross-sell** is a different product or module solving an adjacent problem, usually for a partly different set of users. Upsells generally need no new decision-maker; cross-sells usually do, and are underestimated for exactly that reason. se201 sets out the strategic paths expansion can take; the work here is deciding whether the specific one in front of you is real.

## The gate: expand from proof

Before any qualification, one condition. **You do not propose expansion in an account that has not yet delivered what it already promised.**

The test is concrete, not a feeling. The measures agreed at kickoff are being produced, most are at or near target, adoption is healthy in every team that was in scope, and the open issues from your reviews have owners and dates. If any of those is false, the honest move is to fix it first and say so out loud: *"I think there's a real case for the inspections module, and I don't want to put it in front of you while West is still on paper — let's deal with that first."*

That sentence costs you a quarter and buys you the only thing that makes expansion easy, which is a customer who believes you when you say something will work. Reps under quota pressure skip it. Their expansion proposals convert poorly, and the ones that land arrive attached to a customer who has learned that your recommendations track your quarter.

There is one exception worth naming: expansion that directly fixes the thing that is failing. If a region is not adopting because they need a capability nobody bought, that is not an upsell dressed as a rescue — it is a rescue, and it should be priced and framed as one, often at nothing.

## From pain to capability, honestly

The core skill is unglamorous: take a problem the customer has actually described and check whether a capability you sell genuinely addresses it. Do this on paper, because doing it in your head is how reps end up promising things.

For each candidate, write five columns.

| Column | What goes in it |
| --- | --- |
| **The pain, in their words** | Quoted from a review note. If you cannot quote it, you have not heard it — you have assumed it. |
| **Who feels it** | The role and the number of people. Pain felt by one person who is tolerating it is not a purchase. |
| **What it costs them** | Hours, money, missed revenue, risk, or a named consequence. From their numbers, not your estimate. |
| **The capability that addresses it** | Specifically what the product does, in a sentence a customer would recognize. |
| **What it does not address** | The part of the pain that will still be there afterwards. |

That last column is the one that separates a consultative rep from a product enthusiast. Every capability leaves a residue, and naming it yourself does three things: it stops you overselling, it makes the rest of your claim credible, and it surfaces the deal-killer early — sometimes the residue is the entire point of the problem, and the honest conclusion is that you do not have the answer.

A quick worked pair, from the Tallgrass review notes in lesson 3:

| Pain (their words) | Who feels it | Cost | Capability | Does not address |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| "Compliance certificates live in a filing cabinet in Central and we get audited twice a year" | Dana plus 2 admin staff; 3 regional supervisors | ~2 weeks of preparation per audit, plus a failed audit finding last year | Inspections module: certificates captured on the mobile app at the job, stored against the asset, exportable as an audit pack | Historical certificates already on paper — someone must scan roughly four years of them, and that is their work, not ours |
| "Ruth keys the West spreadsheet, about four hours a week" | Ruth, one person | ~4 hrs/week and a week's lag in billing | None to sell — this is the offline logout defect on a product they already own | Nothing; charging for this would be indefensible |

The second row is there deliberately. A real pain, clearly stated, with a cost attached, that must not become an opportunity.

## The qualification bar

Six tests. An expansion candidate passes when all six are yes; anything less is a candidate with a date, not a deal. Write the answers down — the discipline is in being unable to hide a "no" from yourself.

1. **Proof.** Has the existing commitment been delivered and evidenced? (The gate above.)
2. **Pain.** Is there a specific, quoted problem with a named owner and a cost the customer stated themselves?
3. **Fit.** Does a capability you actually sell today address it, with the residue named? Roadmap does not count.
4. **Owner and authority.** Is there a person whose problem this is, and do you know who releases money for it? For a cross-sell this is frequently someone you have never met.
5. **Money and timing.** Is there budget, and does the timing line up with their cycle — not yours? Expansion asked for in the month after their budget is set is a request to displace something already funded.
6. **Retention risk.** What does the churn scorecard say? An account in amber or worse gets its risk fixed first, and the expansion conversation waits.

## Working two candidates at Tallgrass

Pick the story back up. It is late October at Tallgrass Facilities Group. Since the August review: the offline logout was fixed in September, West adoption has climbed from 11% to 74%, Ruth's spreadsheet is gone, and you presented East and Central results to Marcus Bell before his spend review, which concluded with the platform retained. The renewal on 5 January is now expected to hold at 400 seats. The churn score has fallen from 26 to 7.

Two candidates are on the table.

**Candidate A — the inspections module (cross-sell).** Dana's compliance certificates, from the table above. Roughly $9,400 a year at their asset count.

| Test | Answer | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Proof | Yes | Late-billed call-outs 12.4% to 4.1%; West recovered; all four measures current and shared with Marcus |
| 2 Pain | Yes | Dana, twice-yearly audits, ~2 weeks preparation each, one finding against them last year |
| 3 Fit | Yes, with residue | Module captures certificates at the job and produces an audit pack; four years of paper certificates remain their problem to scan |
| 4 Owner and authority | Yes | Dana owns compliance; Marcus releases spend over $5,000 and has now seen results twice |
| 5 Money and timing | Yes | Their budget is set in September and Dana confirmed a compliance line for next year; the next audit is in March |
| 6 Retention risk | Yes | Score 7, green |

Six for six. This is a real opportunity, and note what made it real: not enthusiasm, but a fixed defect, a rescued region, and an executive who has been shown a number.

**Candidate B — 130 seats for a fourth region (upsell).** Tallgrass is bidding for a contract that would add a Northern region of about 130 technicians, worth roughly $37,000 a year at current pricing. Dana mentioned it in passing.

| Test | Answer | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Proof | Yes | As above |
| 2 Pain | Not established | No problem stated — the region does not exist yet. The rep has inferred a need |
| 3 Fit | Yes | Identical use case to the three live regions |
| 4 Owner and authority | Unclear | Dana would own operations, but the bid is run by a commercial director you have never met |
| 5 Money and timing | No | Contingent on winning a bid that concludes in February; no budget exists until it does |
| 6 Retention risk | Yes | Score 7 |

Two clear noes. The verdict is **not yet**, and the difference between a good rep and a poor one is entirely in what happens next. A poor rep either forgets it or puts $37,000 into the forecast for Q1 because it is a big number and the account is happy. A good rep writes a trigger:

> **Expansion candidate — Northern region, ~130 seats, ~$37,000/yr.** Trigger: Tallgrass is awarded the Northern contract (decision expected February). Owner: Dana Whitfield, operations; commercial director not yet identified — action for me. Next action: ask Dana for an introduction to the bid owner at the January renewal meeting, positioned as implementation planning, not as a sale. Review date: 15 February. Not forecast.

That record is worth more than the optimistic forecast, and it is what makes the difference between remembering this in February and rediscovering it in June.

## Raising it in the conversation

Three things about the moment itself.

**Raise it from their words, not your product.** "You mentioned in August that audit preparation costs you a fortnight twice a year, and that there was a finding last time. Is that still the case?" You have opened on their problem and asked whether it is still live — and sometimes the answer is that it was solved another way, which saves everyone a wasted month.

**Separate the review from the ask.** If a review is the meeting where you also sell, the review is compromised. Either book a separate conversation, or ask permission explicitly at the end: "I've got one thing that isn't a review item — worth five minutes now or shall I set something up?"

**Make the ask small and specific.** The first ask is almost never for the money. It is for a defined next step: a conversation with the compliance admin, a look at how the audit pack would work with their asset list, an introduction. Expansion inside an account is a short sale, not a fast one.

And when the answer is no, take it cleanly, ask what would have to be true for it to be a yes, write that down with a date, and go back to the review. A rep who accepts a no gracefully gets to ask again. A rep who pushes gets an analyst sent to the next meeting.

## Recording it so the picture stays true

Expansion is where account records most often stop matching reality, because expansion is where hope lives. Three rules keep the retention picture accurate.

**A candidate is not an opportunity.** A qualified candidate — all six tests passed, with a real next step agreed by the customer — becomes an opportunity in the CRM. Everything else lives on the account record as a candidate with a trigger, an owner, and a review date. Candidates in the pipeline are how a forecast becomes fiction; candidates on a sticky note are how a real $37,000 gets lost.

**The amount, close date, and stage must each be backed by something that happened.** The amount is what you actually expect at the terms discussed, not list price times the maximum count. The close date is tied to the customer's decision process — their budget cycle, their audit date, their bid outcome — not to the end of your quarter. The stage moves when an event occurs, and the test from lesson 4 applies unchanged: what would you show someone who asked why?

**Expansion never hides the renewal.** Keep them as separate records with separate dates. A rep who has been talking to an account about a module all quarter often has an excellent feel for the relationship and a stale view of the renewal, and a forecast that shows $9,400 of new revenue against a renewal nobody has confirmed is the most misleading picture you can produce. When you qualify an expansion, re-score the account's churn risk in the same sitting.

One last discipline, from the customer's side of the table. Everything you learn while qualifying — the compliance finding, the audit dates, the bid, the commercial director's name — belongs on the account record, whether or not the deal happens. Half of it will be the reason a colleague saves this account in two years, long after you have moved on.

## Practice

Return to **Bayfront Marine Services** from lesson 2, now fourteen months in and renewed once. The account is healthy: 140 seats, 88% active, work orders now captured digitally at all four marinas, and the un-invoiced rate has fallen from an estimated 15% to 2.8% with the figure produced monthly from their own finance export. Priya Raman is still your champion and is now Head of Operations. Elena Brandt, once the sceptic, runs the largest marina and has become the most vocal internal advocate. Your last two reviews produced these quotes:

- Priya: "The one thing that still eats us is parts. A technician finds a job needs a part, phones the office, someone checks a spreadsheet, and half the time it's wrong. We probably lose two or three jobs a week to a return visit."
- Elena: "Customers ring us asking where their boat is in the queue. We just tell them we'll call back."
- Priya, in passing: "The Hallowell acquisition completes in March — that's a fifth marina, maybe thirty more staff. Tom's running it."
- Priya: "Finance want the annual maintenance-contract renewals chased automatically. I think we do that by hand in a spreadsheet."

Your product line includes: the core work-order platform they own; an **inventory and parts module** (about $14,000/yr at their size); a **customer portal** giving end customers job status (about $8,000/yr); and a **contract-renewals automation module** that exists only as a roadmap item for next year. Seats are $70.24 per seat per month. Their budget year starts 1 January and it is currently 8 November.

1. **Build the pain-to-capability table** for every candidate above, using all five columns. Quote the pain. Be specific about what each capability does not address. One of these candidates should not survive the table at all — say which and why.
2. **Qualify each surviving candidate** against all six tests, with evidence, and give each a verdict: proposed now, not yet, or dropped. Where a test is a no, say precisely what information you are missing and how you would get it.
3. **Handle the acquisition.** The fifth marina is roughly 30 seats. Decide whether this is a candidate or an opportunity today, defend the answer with the six tests, and write the trigger record for it in the format used in this lesson.
4. **Write the ask.** For the single candidate you would raise first, write the exact three or four sentences you would say in the next review — opening from Priya's or Elena's own words, and ending in a specific next step that is not a request for money. Then write the sentence you would use to keep the review and the ask separate.
5. **Update the record.** Write out what you would put in the CRM today: which items become opportunities with amount, close date, stage, and the event backing each; which stay as candidates with triggers and review dates; and what you would change on the renewal opportunity as a result of what you have learned. State in one sentence what your total forecast for Bayfront is after this exercise, and defend why nothing else is in it.
6. **Argue against yourself.** Pick your strongest candidate and write the three sentences a sceptical manager would use to say you are about to sell too early. Then answer them, or concede.
