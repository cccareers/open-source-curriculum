## Why an existing customer is harder to listen to than a prospect

In discovery, a prospect has an incentive to tell you what is wrong. They want it fixed, they are evaluating whether you can fix it, and describing the problem is how they find out. You learned in se101 how to open that up: mostly open questions, real silence, and a playback in their words before you move on. All of that still applies here, and this lesson assumes it.

The account review is harder, for reasons that have nothing to do with your questioning technique.

An existing customer is telling you about a decision they already made. If the product is disappointing them, saying so out loud means conceding that their own choice — often defended internally against someone like Simon or Elena — has not paid off. Add ordinary politeness, add that you have become someone they quite like, add that they have forty minutes and eleven other things on their mind, and you get the single most common sentence in account management: **"Yeah, it's fine."**

"Fine" is not information. It is the sound of a conversation that has not started. This lesson is about what you do next, and about how to hear the risk that the customer has not decided to tell you — sometimes because they are being polite, and often because they have not yet articulated it to themselves.

## The four things a review is listening for

Go in with categories rather than a script, because a script makes you listen for your next question instead of their answer. Four things are worth hearing, and none of them is usually volunteered.

**Unmet expectation.** Something they thought would happen has not. It rarely arrives as a complaint; it arrives as a small qualifier attached to praise. "The scheduling side is good — for the main crews, anyway."

**Effort.** The workaround they have quietly built to make your product fit. Customers almost never report workarounds as problems; they report them as how things are done. A workaround is an unpaid tax on your product, and the person paying it will not renew enthusiastically.

**Change on their side.** A reorganization, a new executive, a budget freeze, a merger, a departure. This is the highest-value information in the room and it is almost always mentioned in passing, once, as context for something else.

**Comparison.** Any mention, however casual, of another tool, another vendor, a pilot in another department, or "what we used at my last company." People do not raise comparisons idly.

Write those four words at the top of your notes. When one appears, you follow it — everything else on your agenda can wait.

## Techniques that work on people being polite

**Ask about the world, not about you.** "How are you finding the platform?" invites a verdict on your product, and politeness supplies a good one. "Walk me through what a Monday looks like for the dispatch team now" invites a description, and descriptions contain problems. The best review questions never mention your product at all.

**Ask about the exception, not the average.** "It's going well overall" is an average, and averages hide exactly the thing you are looking for. "Where does it work least well?" and "who's had the hardest time with it?" are permission slips. Ask them expecting an answer, not as a formality, and then be quiet.

**Use the contrast question.** "What's different about how your week runs now compared with January?" This forces a comparison to a real prior state instead of an abstract judgment, and it surfaces both the wins you can use and the parts of the promise that never landed.

**Label what you are hearing, rather than asking about it.** A label is a short observation of the other person's state, offered without a question mark: "It sounds like the billing hand-off is still costing you people." A label is easier to correct than an accusation and easier to expand on than an interrogative. Customers routinely respond to a good label with the sentence you needed.

**Follow hedges.** Real speech marks its own uncertainty. Words like *mostly*, *generally*, *at the moment*, *to be fair*, *in theory*, *we've been managing* are flags planted over something. Repeat the hedge back as a question — "managing?" — and stop talking.

**Listen for absence.** Who did not join a review they always join? Which region never comes up? Which measure from the value contract did they skip when reading the scorecard? Silence is data. "I noticed we haven't talked about West at all today" is a completely legitimate thing to say out loud.

**Count the future tense.** A customer describing next quarter using your product is a customer who intends to still have it. A customer who only ever describes the past has stopped planning with you. This is a subjective signal, and it is a good one.

**Leave one silence you find embarrassing.** The pause after "is there anything we should be worried about that we haven't covered?" is where the real answer lives. New reps fill it in two seconds. Count to five.

## Confirming: the playback, and the note that follows it

Surfacing a risk is worth nothing if you leave the call with a version of it the customer would not recognize. So you play it back, out loud, in their words — the same mechanic from se101 — but in an account review the playback does one extra job: it separates what they said from what you concluded.

That distinction matters because you will act on it. "Adoption in West is low" is your conclusion. "The West supervisor tells his crews to keep using the paper pads because the app logs them out in the yard" is what they said. If you escalate the first, your delivery team investigates adoption. If you escalate the second, someone looks at session timeouts on poor connectivity and the problem is gone in a fortnight.

Then write it down the same day, in the account record, in three parts:

- **Heard:** their words, quoted where you can.
- **Concluded:** your read of what it means, marked as yours.
- **Committed:** what you said you would do, by when, and what they said they would do.

That note is the raw material for the churn-risk assessment in the next lesson and for the renewal conversation after it. A review that produced no written record produced nothing; you will have a warm feeling in month four and no evidence in month ten.

## A worked account review, annotated

Month seven at **Tallgrass Facilities Group**, the account from lesson 2. Present: Dana Whitfield, Director of Field Operations, and you. Forty minutes. The planned day-45 executive touchpoint never happened; Marcus last heard an initial progress update on 12 January, over six months ago. On paper the account looks healthy — the East and Central regions are live, late-billed call-outs are down from 12.4% to 4.1%.

> **You:** Before I go through the numbers — walk me through what a bad week looks like for dispatch now. Not an average week, a bad one. *(asks about their world, and about the exception rather than the average)*
>
> **Dana:** Honestly it's much better. The bad weeks are mostly billing now rather than dispatch. *(a hedge: "mostly")*
>
> **You:** Mostly billing. *(repeats the hedge, then stops)*
>
> **Dana:** ...Yeah. Well — West is still on paper, so their call-outs come to us as a spreadsheet on Monday and someone here keys them in. That's about four hours of Ruth's week.
>
> **You:** So West going live at day 85 — that didn't hold. *(a fact, not a question; invites correction)*
>
> **Dana:** It went live technically. The crews just aren't using it. Ray runs West and he's never been convinced.
>
> **You:** *(five-second pause)*
>
> **Dana:** To be fair, he's got a point about the app in the yard. It logs them out and they lose the form. So they gave up.
>
> **You:** That's the first I've heard of the logout. Tell me exactly what happens — a technician's in the yard, they open the form, and then? *(escalates from a general complaint to a specific mechanism)*
>
> **Dana:** They fill it in, signal drops, and when it comes back they're at the login screen and the form's gone. Twice a day, Ray says.
>
> **You:** And Ruth is keying in a spreadsheet for four hours a week to cover for it. *(connects two things she said separately; makes the cost visible in her own numbers)*
>
> **Dana:** When you put it like that.
>
> **You:** What else has that changed? Does anything downstream depend on West's data being a week behind? *(consequence, not sympathy)*
>
> **Dana:** Marcus's month-end. He asked me about it in April, actually. He wanted to know why we're paying for four hundred seats when only East and Central are using it.
>
> **You:** *(writes; does not respond immediately)* Say more about that conversation. *(the single most important sentence in the meeting has just arrived, in passing, as context for something else)*
>
> **Dana:** It wasn't a big deal. He's doing a review of all the software spend before the budget goes in — that's September, I think. He asked me to have a number for what we're actually using.
>
> **You:** So there's a spend review in September, and one of the inputs is a seat-utilization number that currently looks bad because of West. Have I got that right? *(playback of the risk, in her framing, with an explicit invitation to correct)*
>
> **Dana:** That's about the size of it, yes.
>
> **You:** Then I'd rather work on West than on anything else I brought today. Is there anything else on your side I should know about before I take this back? *(clears the agenda for the real issue, then asks the open question and leaves the silence)*
>
> **Dana:** ...Ray's been talking to someone about a different scheduling app. I don't know how serious it is.

Look at what that conversation produced and where it came from. The seat-utilization exposure — the fact that could end this account — arrived because Dana used the word "mostly," because the rep repeated it and said nothing else, and because when Marcus's name came up the rep asked her to say more instead of reassuring her that it was fine. The competitive mention arrived in the last ten seconds, in the silence after a question the rep could easily have skipped.

Note also what the rep did not do. No defending the product when the logout was described. No promising a fix on the spot. No pivot to the good numbers, which were genuinely good and which nobody needed to hear about.

The note written that afternoon:

> **Heard (Dana Whitfield, 14 Aug):** "West is still on paper" — crews abandoned the app because it logs out when signal drops and the form is lost, "twice a day" per Ray Nowak. Ruth keys a West spreadsheet ~4 hrs/week. Marcus Bell asked Dana in April for a utilization number ahead of a **software spend review in September**. Ray "has been talking to someone about a different scheduling app," seriousness unknown.
> **Concluded (mine):** West non-adoption is a product-fit problem (offline session handling), not a training problem. Seat true-down risk at renewal is 400 to ~270 if West stays on paper. The September spend review is the real decision point, not the 5 January renewal date.
> **Committed:** I raise the offline logout with the delivery team by 16 Aug and come back to Dana with a date by 21 Aug. Dana gets me Ray's availability for a site visit. I ask Dana to introduce me to Marcus before the September review.

## How the same conversation goes wrong

For contrast, the version most reps have:

> **You:** Hi Dana, how's everything going with the platform?
>
> **Dana:** Yeah, good. It's been a big improvement.
>
> **You:** Great to hear. So late billing's down from 12.4% to 4.1%, which is ahead of where we said we'd be, and adoption's at 68% across the regions...
>
> **Dana:** That's great.
>
> **You:** Any issues at all?
>
> **Dana:** Nothing major.
>
> **You:** Perfect. I'll send this over. Same time next quarter?

Every sentence is pleasant, the meeting finishes early, and the account is now five months from a renewal that nobody has any evidence for. Note the mechanics of the failure: a verdict question at the start, a number recited without asking what it hides, "any issues" asked in a tone that requests the answer "no," and the 68% adoption figure read aloud without anyone asking which region is missing. The information was available in both conversations. Only one of them went and got it.

## Practice

Do this with a partner playing the customer. Give them the card and do not read it yourself.

**Customer card:** *You are Nadia Osei, Head of Service Delivery at a regional lift and escalator maintenance firm, nine months into a platform contract. You genuinely like the product. Two things are true that you will not volunteer: (a) your two largest engineering teams have started keeping a parallel spreadsheet because the platform cannot show a job's full service history on one screen, which costs them a few minutes per job and irritates them constantly; and (b) your company was acquired six weeks ago and the parent group has its own preferred field-service system, though nobody has told you to switch. If asked directly about problems you will say "nothing major." You will mention the acquisition only if the conversation turns to anything about next year, budgets, or your own role. If the rep repeats one of your hedges back to you, expand honestly.*

1. **Run the review, twenty minutes.** Rules: your first three questions must not contain your product's name; you must ask about an exception rather than an average at least twice; and you must leave one silence of at least five full seconds after asking whether there is anything you should be worried about.
2. **Keep two-column notes** during the call — their exact words on the left, your interpretation on the right. You are not allowed to write anything on the right that has no partner on the left.
3. **Deliver a playback** in the last three minutes: no more than four sentences, in her language, naming the risk rather than the symptom, ending with an explicit invitation to correct you. Write down verbatim how she corrects you.
4. **Write the account note** in the Heard / Concluded / Committed format, dated. Each commitment needs an owner and a date.
5. **Debrief on one question only:** at what point in the conversation did the second fact (the acquisition) become reachable, and what did you do or fail to do at that moment? If it never surfaced, identify the exact question you should have asked instead of the one you did.
6. **Re-run with a different partner and a hard constraint:** you may ask six questions in total, and you must spend at least half the meeting silent. Notice which six you keep.

## Check your understanding

1. Rewrite "How are you finding the platform?" as a question about their world. *(One good answer: "Walk me through what a bad week looks like for dispatch now.")*
2. A customer says "We've been managing." What do you do? *(Answer: repeat the hedge — "Managing?" — and stop talking.)*
3. In a Heard / Concluded / Committed note, where does "West adoption is a product-fit problem" go, and why does the distinction matter? *(Answer: Concluded — it is your interpretation. Keeping it separate from what the customer actually said stops you escalating the wrong problem.)*
