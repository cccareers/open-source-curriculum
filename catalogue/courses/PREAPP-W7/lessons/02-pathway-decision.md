## Enthusiasm is a signal, not a decision

Ask a room of participants which pathway they want on the Monday of Week 7 and most will answer in under three seconds. That speed should worry you. A three-second answer is a feeling, and feelings about a pathway are formed by things that have nothing to do with fit: which week had the best guest speaker, which cohort-mate you sat next to, which rotation happened most recently. Recency alone moves preference more than almost anything else, and your Digital Marketing rotation ended four days ago.

Enthusiasm still matters. Nobody survives an apprenticeship in work they find tedious, and a pathway you dread is a pathway you will quit. But enthusiasm is one input among four, and the other three are the ones that distinguish a defensible declaration from a guess. Today you assemble all four, look at where they disagree, and write a justification that a coach — or an apprenticeship employer — could check line by line.

The four pathways on the table are the same four you rotated through: Software Development, Technical Sales, Digital Marketing, and AI Prompt Engineering. You are choosing one to declare, and that declaration drives everything else this week: the capstone you assemble and the application you submit are both for the pathway you name today.

## The four evidence sources

**Rotation performance.** Each rotation produced numbers and delivered work. Outreach attempted and connected, meetings booked, tickets or builds completed, content shipped, prompts iterated to a working result. Pull the actual figures out of your tracker rather than remembering them — memory reliably inflates the weeks you enjoyed and deflates the weeks that were hard. Performance is not only volume: look at how quickly you got unstuck, how much rework a deliverable needed, and whether you finished what you started.

**Your own reflections.** You wrote a reflection at the end of every rotation, in the week it happened. That version of you had no idea which pathway you would end up declaring, which makes those notes far more trustworthy than anything you recall today. Read all four before you rank anything. Pay particular attention to what you wrote about the parts of the week that were *boring* rather than the parts that were hard — difficulty fades with skill, but tedium usually does not.

**Interview intelligence.** Your informational interviews gave you something the rotations could not: what the job looks like on day 400, not day 4. A practitioner telling you that half of technical sales is CRM hygiene, or that most junior development time goes to reading code rather than writing it, is describing the steady state you are actually signing up for. Go back through your interview notes and pull the specific statements about daily reality, entry-level hiring, and what the person wishes they had known.

**Coach input.** Your coach watched you work across all four rotations and has the one perspective you structurally cannot have — a comparison of you against other participants and against apprentices who came before you. Coach input is evidence, not a verdict; you are the one declaring. But if your coach's read differs from yours, that gap is the single most useful thing on the table today, and the memo you write has to address it rather than ignore it.

## Build the fit matrix

Put the evidence in one place before you weigh it. A simple matrix, one row per pathway, forces you to fill every cell — including the cells for the pathway you have already privately eliminated.

```text
Pathway            | Performance    | My reflections | Interview intel | Coach input
-------------------|----------------|----------------|-----------------|-------------
Software Dev       | 12 tickets,    | "lost track of | "80% of junior  | "strongest
                   | 3 needed       | time debugging"| work is reading | debugging
                   | rework         |                | existing code"  | persistence"
Technical Sales    | 41 outreach,   | "drained after | "CRM hygiene is | "best call
                   | 6 meetings     | cold calls"    | half the job"   | prep in cohort"
Digital Marketing  | 4 posts, 1     | "enjoyed the   | "measurement is | "good copy,
                   | campaign brief | analytics"     | most of it"     | thin analysis"
AI Prompt Eng.     | 9 prompt sets, | "felt like     | "roles still    | "fast iteration,
                   | 2 shipped      | puzzle-solving"| forming"        | weak documentation"
```

Fill it with quotes and numbers, not adjectives. "Good" in a cell means nothing a week from now; "41 outreach attempts, 6 meetings booked, 2 no-shows" means something to you and to a reader. If a cell is empty because you have no evidence, write "no evidence" rather than filling it with an impression — an honest gap is information, and it may be the thing you go collect before Wednesday.

## Read the disagreements

The matrix earns its keep where rows conflict. Four patterns come up every cohort:

- **High performance, low enthusiasm.** You were good at it and did not enjoy it. Ask whether you disliked the work or disliked being a beginner at it. Those feel identical in week 3 and are completely different over two years.
- **High enthusiasm, low performance.** You loved it and struggled. This is not disqualifying — it may mean you were exposed to the hardest material with the least support. Ask whether your struggle was in the pathway's core daily work or in something peripheral to it, and check the interview intelligence for how much of the job that struggle represents.
- **Your read versus your coach's read.** Neither of you is automatically right. Get specific: ask which observation produced their assessment. "You lost momentum whenever a task had no clear next step" is actionable evidence; "you seem more like a sales person" is not, and you should push for the observation behind it.
- **The rotation versus the real job.** A rotation is a curated week. Your informational interviews describe the uncurated version. Where they conflict, the interviews usually describe more of your future than the rotation does.

You do not need to resolve every conflict cleanly. You need to name them and explain how you weighed them — that reasoning *is* the justification.

## Write the declaration memo

The deliverable is one page, addressed to your coach, that a reader could evaluate without having watched you all program. Use this structure:

1. **The declaration.** One sentence naming the pathway. No hedging, no second choice tucked into a clause.
2. **The evidence, source by source.** One short paragraph each for performance, reflections, interview intelligence, and coach input, each citing something specific — a number, a dated quote from your own reflection, a named practitioner's statement.
3. **The strongest case against.** The best argument that you are choosing wrong, stated fairly enough that someone who holds it would recognize their view, and your answer to it.
4. **What would change your mind.** The specific evidence that would make you revisit this in six months.
5. **First 30 days.** What you will do to close the biggest gap the matrix exposed in your declared pathway.

Section 3 is the one that separates a real justification from a generic one. A memo that presents only supporting evidence tells a reader you did not look for the other kind.

Two failure modes to avoid. The first is generic claims: "I'm a people person, so I'm choosing Technical Sales" cites nothing and could have been written in Week 1. The second is fake precision — inventing or rounding numbers to make a case. Your coach can check your tracker, and an application built on numbers you cannot support is worse than one with modest numbers you can.

Once the memo is written, read it back with one question: if a stranger read this and nothing else, could they explain to a third person why you chose this pathway? If the answer is no, the memo is not finished.

## Practice

1. **Assemble the evidence.** Pull your rotation metrics from your tracker, re-read all four end-of-rotation reflections, and collect your informational interview notes. Do not rank anything yet.

2. **Build the fit matrix.** Complete all four rows and all four columns using numbers and direct quotes only. Mark any cell where you have no evidence as "no evidence" rather than filling it with an impression.

3. **Interrogate the conflicts.** List every row where two sources disagree. For each one, write two or three sentences on how you are weighing them and why. Bring your sharpest disagreement to your coach and ask for the specific observation behind their read.

4. **Write the declaration memo.** One page, all five sections, including the strongest case against your own choice. Cite at least one specific number and one dated quote from your own reflections.

5. **Defend it out loud.** Read the memo to a cohort-mate whose job is to find the weakest-supported claim in it and say so. Revise that claim — either strengthen the evidence or drop the claim — before you submit.
