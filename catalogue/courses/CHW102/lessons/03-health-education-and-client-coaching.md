## The C3 role set, in one view

Before going deeper into any single role, it helps to see the whole set. The Community Health Worker Core Consensus Project — C3 — asked CHWs, employers, educators, and state associations across the country to agree on what the job actually is. The result is a role list that most state certification programs, job descriptions, and training curricula now share:

1. **Cultural mediation** among individuals, communities, and health and social service systems.
2. **Providing culturally appropriate health education and information** — the first half of this lesson.
3. **Care coordination, case management, and system navigation** — lesson 04.
4. **Providing coaching and social support** — the second half of this lesson.
5. **Advocating for individuals and communities** — lesson 05.
6. **Building individual and community capacity** — lesson 05.
7. **Providing direct service**, such as basic screenings, first aid, or delivering supplies, within program protocol.
8. **Implementing individual and community assessments** — lesson 06.
9. **Conducting outreach** — lesson 06.
10. **Participating in evaluation and research** — lesson 06.

Two things to notice. First, the list is a scope statement: taken together these roles describe what a CHW does, and by implication what a CHW does not do. Nothing on the list is diagnosis, prescription, treatment decisions, or medical advice. Second, no CHW does all ten every day. Your employer's program design decides which roles dominate your week — a clinic-embedded CHW may spend most of it on coordination and coaching, while a public health department CHW may spend it on outreach and assessment. Recognizing which role a situation calls for is the skill this course builds.

## Health education: what the role is

The health education role means giving people accurate, usable health information in a form they can act on — one to one at a kitchen table, in a group class, at a table in a community center, or in a five-minute conversation in a clinic hallway. It is the role people picture when they hear "community health worker," and it is the one most often done badly, because doing it well is much harder than knowing the content.

Health education is not a lecture. The measure of a session is not what you covered; it is what the person can now do differently. A CHW who delivers twenty minutes of accurate information to someone who leaves unable to say when to take their pill has not educated anyone.

## Meeting people where their literacy is

Nearly half of adults in the United States have difficulty with the reading demands of ordinary health materials, and health literacy — the ability to find, understand, and use health information — is lower still. Health literacy is also situational: a person who reads well collapses under fear, pain, or a new diagnosis. Assume nothing from someone's job, appearance, or fluency.

Plain language is the baseline skill.

- Use everyday words. "High blood pressure," not "hypertension." "Water pill," not "diuretic." When a medical term must be used because the client will hear it again, say it once, define it, then use the everyday word.
- Keep sentences short and use the active voice: "Take one pill each morning," not "Medication should be administered daily."
- Lead with what to do. People remember the first thing and the thing you said was most important.
- Limit each conversation to three key points. More is not retained.
- Use concrete numbers and anchors instead of abstractions: "one pill when you wake up, before coffee," not "once daily as needed."
- Choose a format that fits. Pictures, demonstration, a filled-in pill box, a video, or a conversation may all beat a pamphlet, and for oral-tradition communities they usually do.

**Teach-back** is how you find out whether any of it worked. Ask the client to say, in their own words, what they will do — and frame it as a check on you, not a test of them: "I want to be sure I explained this clearly. When you get home, how will you take this medicine?" If the answer is wrong, that is a successful teach-back; you have found the gap while you can still fix it. Re-explain differently, then ask again. Never accept "yes, I understand" as evidence. It costs nothing to say and is what most people say when they are lost.

## Culturally appropriate education

Literacy is one fit; culture is the other. The same content lands differently depending on the examples, the images, the messenger, and whether the plan is possible in the client's actual life.

Practical moves: teach nutrition using the foods the community cooks, adapting portions and preparation rather than replacing the cuisine; use images that look like the audience; account for who in the household makes decisions and, with the client's permission, include them; work around fasting periods, shift schedules, and shared kitchens; and choose a messenger the community trusts when you are not that person yourself.

The strongest health education borrows what is already there. If neighbors already walk together in the evening, you have a physical activity program. If a congregation already runs a monthly meal, you have a nutrition venue. Building on an existing practice takes less effort and survives after the program ends.

## Evidence, opinion, and the scope line

Everything you teach must be evidence-based and free of your own bias. This sounds simple and fails in ordinary, well-intentioned ways.

**Separate what the evidence says from what you think.** Your own experience is powerful and it is not evidence. "I stopped eating bread and lost twenty pounds" is a personal story with the authority of a recommendation attached, and clients will hear it as advice. If you share a personal experience at all, label it clearly as yours and return immediately to the evidence.

**Keep your values out of it.** Contraception, vaccination, end-of-life choices, substance use, and body weight all attract strong personal beliefs. Your role is accurate information and support for the client's informed decision, not persuasion toward your own view. If you cannot present a topic without steering, tell your supervisor; that is a professional disclosure, not a failure.

**Use your program's approved sources.** Curricula and materials vetted by your organization, plus recognized public health sources, are the floor. Do not teach from social media, a friend's experience, or a supplement website, and do not update a client's plan based on a headline.

**Say "I don't know."** A CHW who says "I don't know, and I will find out and call you tomorrow" — and then does — builds more trust than one who improvises. Guessing is the one option that is always wrong.

**The scope line is bright.** A CHW never diagnoses, never recommends starting, stopping, or changing a medication or dose, never interprets a test result, and never tells a client what treatment to choose. When a client asks any of those, the answer is a referral: "That's a question for your doctor. Let's write it down so you don't forget to ask, and I can go with you if you want."

Notice what that answer does. It refuses the question, keeps the relationship, and converts the moment into preparation. Staying in scope is not a way of doing less; it is a way of being reliable to the client and credible to the clinical team.

Escalate immediately, rather than educate, when a client describes symptoms that could be an emergency, reports thoughts of harming themselves or someone else, discloses abuse or neglect, or says something that makes you uncertain. Know your program's escalation path before you need it.

## Coaching and client-owned goals

Education gives people information. Coaching helps them do something with it, over time, in a life full of obstacles. The coaching and social support role is where most lasting change actually happens, and its defining feature is that **the goal belongs to the client**.

The instinct to assign goals is strong. The referral says the target is an A1C below 7. The care plan says lose fifteen pounds. So the CHW arrives with a goal already written, the client agrees politely, and nothing happens — because the client never wanted it, or wanted it but not more than they wanted to keep their job, or agreed because agreeing is easier than explaining.

Elicit instead:

- "What's one thing about your health you'd like to be different in the next month?"
- "What matters most to you right now?"
- "What have you already tried? What worked, even a little?"
- "What would make this easier?"

Then help shape whatever they say into something workable. A goal a client can act on is specific ("walk to the corner store and back after dinner"), small enough to be believable, measurable in some plain way, genuinely possible in their circumstances, and tied to a time. "Eat healthier" is a wish. "Cook rice with beans instead of instant noodles three nights a week" is a goal.

Let the goal be theirs even when it is not the clinical priority. A client whose stated goal is sleeping through the night is telling you what they will actually work on. Success there builds the confidence and the relationship that make the next goal possible. A client who achieves a goal they chose has learned they can change something; that is the durable outcome.

**Tracking should be simple enough to survive a bad week.** A mark on a wall calendar, a photo of a plate, a text to you on Fridays, beans moved from one jar to another. If tracking requires an app, a login, and a charged phone, it will stop. Ask the client how they want to keep track, and use their answer.

Follow up on what they chose, in their words — "Last time you said you wanted to walk after dinner. How did that go?" — and treat setbacks as information rather than failure: what got in the way, what would make next week different, is the goal still the right size. Then adjust the goal, not the client.

Social support is the quiet other half of this role. Sometimes the most useful thing you do is take the isolation out of a diagnosis: sit with someone, believe them, connect them to a group of people managing the same thing. That is inside your scope, and for many clients it is the intervention that matters most.

## Practice

1. **Role identification.** For each situation, name which C3 role it primarily calls for, and say whether any part of it falls outside CHW scope.
   - A client asks whether she should cut her blood pressure pill in half because it makes her dizzy.
   - You run a 40-minute session on asthma triggers at a tenants' association meeting.
   - A client says he wants to stop smoking "at some point" and asks what people usually do first.
   - You sit with a newly diagnosed client for twenty minutes while she cries, and connect her to a peer group.
   - A client's discharge paperwork is in English and he reads only Haitian Creole.
2. **Plain-language rewrite.** Rewrite this for a client with low health literacy, keeping every clinical fact intact: "Administer metformin 500 mg PO BID with meals to optimize glycemic control; monitor for gastrointestinal adverse effects and contact the clinic if symptoms persist beyond two weeks." State your three key points in the order you would say them.
3. **Teach-back role-play.** In pairs, the CHW explains how to use a rescue inhaler; the client has been instructed to nod and say "yes, I understand" while having quietly misunderstood one step. The CHW must run teach-back, find the gap, re-explain differently, and check again. Swap roles with a new topic. Debrief: what phrasing made the client willing to admit confusion, and what phrasing made it a test?
4. **Scope sorting.** Sort these into "answer it," "answer part of it and refer," or "refer and do not answer": what does A1C mean; is my rash infected; should I take the vaccine; where do I get the vaccine; my chest hurts when I climb stairs; can I take my pill at night instead of morning; what does a sliding scale cost; is my son's fever high enough for the ER. For each, write the exact words you would use.
5. **Bias check.** Write a two-minute explanation of one contested topic (vaccination, contraception, or medication for opioid use disorder) with your own opinion completely absent. Have a partner read it and mark any sentence that reveals what you believe. Revise until nothing is marked.
6. **Goal elicitation role-play.** In pairs, the client has been referred for uncontrolled hypertension but cares most about being able to play with a grandchild without getting winded. The CHW must elicit a client-owned goal without ever naming a blood pressure number as the target, shape it into something specific and doable, and agree on a tracking method the client picks. The observer notes every moment the CHW slipped into assigning. Then write the goal and tracking plan in the client's own words, on one line.
7. **The follow-up call.** The client from exercise 6 has done nothing for two weeks. Write the opening three sentences of your follow-up call and the four questions you will ask. Then write what you would do if the reason turns out to be that his knees hurt too much to walk — including whether any part of your response leaves CHW scope.

**Deliverable:** your labeled role-identification answers, the plain-language rewrite with three key points, teach-back role-play debrief notes, the scope-sorting table with your exact wording, the bias-checked explanation with revision marks, the elicited goal and tracking plan in the client's words, and the written follow-up call plan.

## Check your understanding

1. A client asks, "Should I take my blood pressure pill at night instead of the morning?" Which part can you answer, and what do you say? *None of the dosing question is yours. Say something like: "That's a question for your doctor or pharmacist. Let's write it down, and I can call the pharmacist with you now." Then help them get it asked.*
2. Your teach-back reveals the client thinks an inhaler prescribed for daily use in their written asthma action plan is only for bad days. Is that a failed teach-back? *No — it is a successful one. You found the gap while you can still fix it. Re-explain the care team’s written instructions differently and check again; route uncertainties to the care team.*
3. The referral says "A1C below 7"; the client says she wants to sleep through the night. Whose goal do you work on first, and why? *Hers. Working on a client-owned goal may support engagement and trust; it does not guarantee progress toward a clinical goal. Keep the care team informed.*
