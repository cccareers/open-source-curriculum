## The role: standing between two worlds

The C3 framework names cultural mediation among individuals, communities, and health and social service systems as a core CHW role, and it is listed first for a reason. It conditions everything else you do. You can deliver flawless health education in a language the client does not read, arrange a perfect referral to a clinic the community has learned not to trust, and accomplish nothing.

Mediation means you stand between two worlds and carry meaning in both directions. In one direction you help a client understand what the system is asking of them: what a prior authorization is, why the clinic wants three months of blood sugar readings, what "fasting" means when the appointment is at two in the afternoon. In the other direction you help the system understand the client: that the reason she misses morning appointments is a shift that ends at 7 a.m., that his family makes health decisions together and a solo consent conversation will stall, that the neighborhood remembers a research study that took and never gave back.

There is a third direction people forget. You also mediate between the community and itself — surfacing what neighbors already know and do, so that a health plan builds on it rather than overwriting it.

Mediation is not translation of words alone, and it is not smoothing things over. When a clinic's policy is genuinely creating a barrier, naming that clearly is mediation too. What mediation never becomes is clinical: you explain what the provider said, you do not add to it, revise it, or supply the medical opinion the provider did not give.

## Cultural humility, and why it replaced "competence"

For years the field talked about cultural competence — the idea that a worker could learn a set of facts about a group and become skilled at serving it. The idea had value, and it had a flaw that eventually broke it: competence implies an endpoint. It suggests you can finish learning a culture, and that a person can be predicted from the group they belong to.

Cultural humility replaces that with three commitments.

**Lifelong learning and self-reflection.** You are never done. Every client teaches you something that revises what you thought you knew, and the appropriate posture is curiosity rather than expertise.

**Recognizing and addressing power imbalances.** You arrive attached to an institution, holding information and access the client does not have. Humility means noticing that gap and deliberately handing power back — asking rather than telling, offering options rather than instructions, treating the client as the authority on their own life.

**Institutional accountability.** Humility is not only a personal virtue. Organizations have culture too, and a CHW who notices that the intake form has no space for a chosen name, or that every wall poster shows one kind of family, has found something the organization should fix. Reporting it is part of the role.

The practical difference shows up in one sentence. Cultural competence asks, "What do I know about this group?" Cultural humility asks, "What does this person want me to understand about them?"

## Knowing your own lens

You cannot set aside a bias you have not noticed. Every one of us carries a cultural identity assembled from language, faith or its absence, region, generation, migration history, gender, family structure, education, and money — and that assembly quietly supplies our sense of what is normal.

Bias in CHW work rarely looks like hostility. It looks like assumptions that feel like common sense:

- Assuming a client who does not make eye contact is being evasive, when in their culture sustained eye contact with an authority figure is rude.
- Assuming a parent who declines a food referral is too proud to accept help, when the pantry's hours conflict with their only shift.
- Assuming a client who agrees with everything understood everything.
- Assuming an elder wants their adult child in the room, or assuming they do not.
- Assuming that because you share a language or a country of origin with a client, you share their beliefs about illness, family, or medicine.

That last one deserves emphasis. CHWs are hired for shared background, and shared background creates its own blind spot. Being from the community makes you more likely to be trusted and no less likely to assume.

Self-reflection is a habit, not an event. Experienced CHWs replay difficult encounters afterward and ask: what did I assume before the client told me anything, what surprised me and why was it surprising, whose way of doing things did I treat as the default. Supervision is the right place for the answers you do not like.

## Asking instead of assuming

The corrective for assumption is a question. A handful of open questions, asked with genuine interest, will tell you more about a client's health beliefs than any culture guide:

- "What do you think caused this?"
- "What worries you most about it?"
- "Who else helps you make decisions about your health?"
- "Is there anything you are doing for it now — teas, remedies, prayer, anything at all?"
- "What would a good outcome look like for you?"

Ask that fourth question without flinching, and do not react to the answer. A client who thinks you will disapprove of a home remedy will simply stop mentioning it, and you will lose the information the care team most needs. Your job is to learn what the client is actually doing and make sure the clinical team knows, not to rank practices as right or wrong.

Expect variation inside every group. Two people from the same town, the same faith, and the same family can hold opposite views about hospitals. Culture tells you what questions might matter. Only the person tells you the answers.

## The CLAS standards

The National Standards for Culturally and Linguistically Appropriate Services — CLAS — are the federal framework, issued by the HHS Office of Minority Health, that turns all of this into organizational obligation. There are fifteen standards, and you are not expected to recite them. You are expected to understand their spirit and recognize when your organization is falling short.

At the center is the **Principal Standard**: provide effective, equitable, understandable, and respectful quality care and services that are responsive to diverse cultural health beliefs and practices, preferred languages, health literacy, and other communication needs. Everything else exists to make that real.

The remaining standards cluster into three themes.

**Governance, leadership, and workforce.** Equity is a leadership responsibility, not something delegated to whoever happens to speak the language. Organizations are expected to build a workforce that reflects the community, and to train everyone — not just frontline staff — on culturally and linguistically appropriate practice. CHW positions themselves are partly a response to this theme.

**Communication and language assistance.** Language services must be offered and provided free of charge to anyone with limited English proficiency or other communication needs. People must be told, in their language, that those services exist and are free. The people providing them must be qualified. And print and multimedia materials must actually be understandable to the people they are meant for.

**Engagement, continuous improvement, and accountability.** Equity goals belong in the organization's plans and budget. Organizations should collect and analyze demographic and language data to find disparities, partner with the community in designing services rather than announcing them, maintain a grievance process that works for people who fear complaining, and communicate their progress publicly.

Two things about CLAS matter for your daily work. Language services being **free** is not a courtesy — telling a client they must bring their own interpreter is a compliance failure, and you should say so through your supervisor. And CLAS is written to organizations, which means the standards give you language for advocacy. "Our materials are only at a tenth-grade reading level in one language" is an observation. "Our materials do not meet CLAS" is a finding.

## Language access in practice

Adapting communication to language is the single most common mediation task you will perform, and it has firm rules.

**Use qualified interpreters.** Qualified means trained and assessed in interpreting, held to a code of ethics including confidentiality and accuracy, and competent in health vocabulary in both languages. Bilingual is not the same as qualified; being able to converse is not being able to interpret a diagnosis accurately under pressure.

**Never use children.** This is the brightest line in the whole topic. A child interpreting for a parent is asked to carry information they should not carry — a cancer result, a question about drinking, a domestic violence screen — and the family's roles invert in ways that damage both the child and the parent's authority. It also produces bad information, because children lack the vocabulary and will soften what feels frightening. Child interpretation is inappropriate in essentially every circumstance, and organizations receiving federal funds are obligated to provide an alternative.

**Be cautious with other family and friends.** Adults present the same problems in milder form: no training, no accountability, an interest in the outcome, and a natural instinct to edit. A relative may omit the question about depression to protect the client, or answer on the client's behalf. If a client insists on a family member, document the offer of a qualified interpreter, the client's decision, and — where the information is sensitive — have a qualified interpreter present as well.

**Know your options.** In-person interpreters are best for complex, emotional, or long encounters. Telephone interpretation is fast, available in many languages, and works when nothing visual is needed. Video remote interpretation sits between the two and is the standard for sign language when no in-person interpreter is available. Deaf and hard-of-hearing clients require qualified sign language interpretation; a written note exchange is not equivalent, and a client's family member signing is subject to all the cautions above.

**Working with an interpreter has its own etiquette.** Brief the interpreter beforehand on the purpose and any sensitive content. Speak directly to the client, not to the interpreter — "How long have you had the pain?" rather than "Ask her how long." Use first person. Speak in short segments and pause. Avoid idioms, which do not survive the trip. Let the interpreter interrupt to clarify or to flag a concept that has no equivalent. Confirm understanding through the interpreter, and never ask the interpreter to leave the room and "just summarize."

**Translation is different from interpretation.** Interpretation is spoken; translation is written. Reading an English document aloud in another language is sight translation, it is error-prone, and it is not a substitute for a properly translated document. If your organization has no translated version of a consent form or discharge instruction, that is a gap worth reporting.

## Adapting services to cultural context

Beyond language, mediation means adjusting the service itself so it fits the community. Four dials are worth knowing.

**Materials.** Reading level, images that show people who look like the audience, examples using foods and routines people actually have, and formats that work for oral-tradition communities — audio, video, or a conversation instead of a pamphlet.

**Setting.** A church basement, a barbershop, a laundromat, or a WhatsApp group may reach people that a clinic conference room will not, especially where the clinic itself is the source of mistrust.

**Messenger.** Who says it changes whether it lands. A respected elder, a promotora, a pastor, or a peer with the same diagnosis can carry a message that a stranger with a badge cannot.

**Timing and structure.** Sessions scheduled around shift work, prayer times, school pickup, and fasting periods. Childcare, food, and transportation are not amenities; they are the difference between attendance and absence.

Adaptation has a limit: the clinical content does not change. You may change how diabetes management is explained, in what language, by whom, and where. You do not change what the evidence says about it. Fitting the message to the community is mediation. Altering the medical substance is practicing outside your scope.

## Practice

1. **Own-lens inventory.** Write one page describing your own cultural identity across at least six dimensions (language, faith or its absence, region, generation, migration history, family structure, gender, money, education). Then name three specific assumptions that background makes it easy for you to carry into client work. Be concrete — "I assume people can read appointment letters" beats "I might be biased."
2. **Role identification.** For each situation, decide whether it is primarily cultural mediation, and if so, in which direction (client-to-system, system-to-client, or community-to-itself). Give a one-sentence reason for each.
   - A client's employer will not release her for a daytime appointment, and you call the clinic to ask about evening slots.
   - A father declines a mental health referral and you explain to the care team that the word used on the form translates as "crazy" in his language.
   - You explain to a client what a sliding-fee scale is and how to prove income for it.
   - Neighborhood residents avoid the mobile van because the last one that came took blood and never returned results.
   - A grandmother tells you she gives her grandson a herbal tea for his cough, and you record it and tell the nurse.
3. **Question redesign.** Rewrite each closed or assuming question as an open one that invites the client's own account: "You do take your medication every day, right?"; "You don't have anyone at home to help, do you?"; "Do you understand?"; "You're Muslim, so you'll be fasting, correct?"
4. **Interpreter role-play.** In groups of four — CHW, client, interpreter, observer — run a ten-minute encounter in which the CHW explains a referral to a clinic. The interpreter should deliberately introduce two problems: summarizing instead of interpreting, and answering one question on the client's behalf. The observer records every time the CHW speaks to the interpreter instead of the client. Debrief on what the CHW noticed, what they missed, and how they should have corrected it in the moment.
5. **The hardest language-access call.** A client arrives with her 11-year-old daughter and says the daughter always interprets and she is comfortable with it. The appointment includes a depression screening. Write out, word for word, what you say to the client, what you arrange instead, and what you document. Then write what you would do if the only qualified option available today is a phone line and the client says she does not trust phones.
6. **CLAS gap audit.** Choose one organization you know — a clinic, a school, a food program. List three specific ways it meets a CLAS theme and three ways it falls short. For each shortfall, write one sentence you could say to a supervisor that names the gap without blaming a person.
7. **Adaptation redesign.** You are given a diabetes education session designed for a hospital classroom: two hours, a slide deck at a twelfth-grade reading level, a handout of "healthy foods," Tuesdays at 1 p.m. Redesign it for a community where most adults work hourly shifts, three languages are common, and many people cook from a shared food tradition the handout does not mention. Change the four dials — materials, setting, messenger, timing and structure — and state explicitly what you did not change and why.

**Deliverable:** the own-lens inventory, your labeled role-identification answers with reasons, the rewritten questions, the observer notes and debrief from the role-play, the written language-access script and documentation plan, the CLAS gap audit, and the redesigned session with its unchanged-content statement.
