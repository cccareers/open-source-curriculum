## Turning outward

The roles in lessons 02 through 05 all begin with a client in front of you. The three roles in this lesson begin before that — with the people who never arrive — and end after it, with the question of whether any of the work changed anything.

Outreach finds people the system has not reached. Assessment establishes what individuals and communities actually need and already have. Evaluation asks whether the program is producing anything beyond activity. Together they are what makes CHW work public health rather than a series of kind acts.

## Outreach: going where people are

The outreach role means engaging community members in the places they live, work, worship, shop, and gather, rather than waiting for them to appear at a service location. It exists because the people with the greatest need are the least likely to walk in on their own.

They do not walk in for reasons that are usually rational: they cannot take unpaid time off, they have no transportation, they do not know the service exists or that they qualify, they cannot read the letter, they fear immigration enforcement or a bill they cannot pay, or they remember precisely how they or their neighbors were treated the last time. Mistrust of health institutions in many communities is not a misunderstanding to be corrected. It is a memory of research done on people without consent, of sterilization without consent, of pain reports disbelieved, of a mobile clinic that took samples and never returned results. Outreach that treats that history as ignorance fails immediately.

**Go where people already are.** Laundromats, barbershops and salons, corner stores, bus stops, churches and mosques and temples, food distributions, swap meets, school pickup lines, tenant meetings, encampments, day-labor corners, community festivals, and increasingly WhatsApp and Facebook groups. Choose the setting by the population you are trying to reach: an outreach plan for elders and one for young men who work nights are not the same plan.

**Enter through permission and relationship.** Almost every setting has a gatekeeper — a pastor, a store owner, a resident leader, a shift manager — and their endorsement transfers trust to you far faster than any flyer. Ask permission, explain plainly who you are and who you work for, and come back. Showing up once produces a leaflet handed over. Showing up on the same day for two months produces conversations.

**Lead with usefulness, not enrollment.** A blood pressure check, a bottle of water, a bus pass, an answer to a question about a bill. Give something before asking for something. Then be specific about what you offer, what it costs, what it requires, and what happens to their information — including, honestly, what you can and cannot promise about who sees it.

**Motivate honestly.** Connect the offer to what the person already cares about: staying at work, keeping up with a grandchild, not being in pain, a child's asthma. Do not oversell, and never promise an outcome you do not control. One broken promise circulates through a community faster than any success story.

**Look after yourself and your data.** Follow your program's field safety rules — know the area, work in pairs when indicated, tell someone your route, and leave a situation that feels wrong. And record contacts accurately in the field: who you spoke with, where, when, what was offered, what was agreed, and what follow-up you owe. Contacts written up from memory at the end of the week are the least reliable data your program collects, and the follow-up you promised is the one people remember.

## Individual and community assessment

Assessment answers "what is going on here?" and it operates at two levels.

**Individual assessment** identifies one client's needs, strengths, and circumstances. It ranges from a structured tool — a validated social needs screener, an intake instrument, a standardized questionnaire — to an informal but guided conversation. Structured tools give you comparability across clients and data your program can aggregate; guided conversation gives you the texture and the things no form asks about. Skilled CHWs use both: the tool for the record, the conversation for the truth.

Choose the approach by the situation. A structured instrument fits an intake, a program requirement, or a topic where consistency matters. A guided conversation fits a first meeting in a doorway, a client in crisis, a low-literacy or low-trust situation, or a setting where producing a clipboard would end the conversation. When you use a validated tool, administer it as written; when you use conversation, keep a mental checklist of the domains so you do not miss housing because the client talked about food.

Cover the domains that drive health outcomes: housing stability and quality, food security, income and employment, transportation, insurance and access to care, safety at home and in the neighborhood, social connection, legal and immigration concerns, education and literacy, and behavioral health. And **triage what you find** — separate what is dangerous now and needs action today from what matters over months. A client who is unsafe tonight does not need a wellness plan; they need tonight solved.

Two habits keep individual assessment honest. Ask about strengths in the same breath as needs, using lesson 05's questions, so the record reflects a person rather than a deficit list. And document clearly, factually, and confidentially — what the client said in their words, what you observed, what you did not ask — because someone else will act on it. Documentation standards are covered fully in CHW105.

**Community assessment** asks the same questions about a place. CHWs contribute to it in ways that no survey contractor can: walking or driving the neighborhood and recording systematically what is there (a windshield survey — grocery stores and their prices, pharmacies, clinics, parks, sidewalks, bus stops, vacant lots, liquor stores, signage languages); talking with key informants who know the community from the inside; running listening sessions where residents name their own priorities; helping design questions in the community's language and idiom; and interpreting quantitative findings against what you actually see. Data can say a neighborhood has a clinic. You can say the clinic closes at 4 p.m., has no evening hours, and no one on staff speaks the second-most-common language on the block.

## Asset mapping

A community assessment that records only problems produces a program done to a neighborhood. Identifying assets alongside gaps produces one built with it, and asset-based work is more durable because it survives the grant.

Map five kinds of asset:

- **Individuals** — residents with skills, knowledge, and time: a retired nurse, a woman everyone consults about benefits, a mechanic, a translator, a coach.
- **Associations** — informal and formal groups: congregations, tenants' associations, sports leagues, mutual aid networks, rotating savings clubs, parent groups, a block's WhatsApp thread.
- **Institutions** — schools, clinics, libraries, fire stations, community colleges, employers, and the nonprofits already present.
- **Physical assets** — parks, community rooms, church basements, gardens, transit lines, a kitchen with a health permit.
- **Economic assets** — local businesses, markets, informal economies, employers who would release staff for a health event.

Then use it. An asset map is a working document: it tells you who your messengers are, where to hold sessions, which organizations to partner with, and which existing practice to build a program onto rather than inventing a new one.

Two cautions. Asset mapping is not a way of arguing that a community needs nothing — the gaps are real and naming them is how resources arrive. And an asset list assembled in an office is a guess; residents identify assets that professionals do not know exist.

## Participating in evaluation

Evaluation is how a program learns whether it works, and CHWs are in it whether or not anyone explains why. Your encounter forms, contact logs, screening results, and referral outcomes are the program's raw data. If they are inaccurate, every conclusion drawn from them is wrong — including the conclusions used to decide whether your position continues to be funded.

The distinction to hold onto is **outputs versus outcomes**.

**Outputs are what the program did** — activities and counts. Number of home visits. Number of clients enrolled. Number of classes held. Number of participants attending. Number of screenings administered. Number of referrals made. Number of materials distributed. Outputs measure effort and reach. They are easy to count, and they are necessary: a program with no outputs cannot produce outcomes.

**Outcomes are what changed** — in knowledge, behavior, condition, or circumstance. Clients who can correctly describe how to take their medication. Clients who kept a specialist appointment. Blood pressure controlled at six months. Emergency department visits reduced. Families housed. Clients enrolled in insurance who were previously uninsured. Outcomes are harder to measure, often take longer to appear, and are the only evidence that the effort mattered.

Sort a few and the line gets clear. "Held 12 diabetes classes" is an output; "38 participants improved their A1C" is an outcome. "Made 74 referrals" is an output; "61 clients attended the appointment referred to" is an outcome — which is exactly why lesson 04 insisted a referral is not complete until the client connected. Outcomes are commonly grouped as short-term (knowledge, confidence), intermediate (behavior, service use), and long-term (health status, cost). CHW programs are frequently judged on long-term outcomes they influence only partly, which is a fairness problem worth understanding and one reason short-term and intermediate measures matter.

**Why this affects your daily work.** Programs that report only outputs sound busy and cannot demonstrate value; funders increasingly ask what changed. That question can only be answered from records you kept while doing something else.

So: record contemporaneously, record what actually happened rather than what should have, record refusals and no-shows and failed referrals as faithfully as successes, and use your program's definitions consistently — if "contact" means a two-way conversation, a leaflet through a door is not one. Ask what a field means when you do not know, rather than guessing; a guessed field looks identical to a real one in a spreadsheet.

**Flag data quality problems** rather than quietly working around them: a form with no option for what you keep seeing, a definition two staff interpret differently, a required field people leave blank, a measure that misses the results the community actually values. You are the only person who knows what the data leaves out. Raise it with your supervisor — that is participation in evaluation, and it is inside your role.

Finally, know the ethical line. Program evaluation asks whether this program works. Research asks a generalizable question and carries its own consent, oversight, and protections. If you are ever asked to collect data for research, it comes with informed consent and institutional review, not with a clipboard. Community-based participatory research and the CHW's role in it are covered in CHW103.

## How the roles fit together

One family, one story, every role in sequence.

You meet Ms. Reyes at a church food distribution because you are there most Saturdays — **outreach**. She mentions her son's asthma keeps him home from school. In conversation you learn about mold in the apartment, no inhaler refill in two months, and a landlord who does not answer, while also learning she has kept the same job for six years and has a sister two blocks away — **assessment**, needs and strengths together.

The clinic sends her paperwork in English; you explain what it asks and arrange a qualified interpreter for the visit, and you tell the nurse that the family's asthma plan assumes a car they do not have — **cultural mediation**. You show her how to recognize her son's triggers and use a spacer, and check with teach-back — **health education** — staying carefully away from any comment on his medication doses.

You match her to a pediatric asthma program that takes her insurance, has Saturday hours, and is on her bus line; you call together, book the appointment, and two weeks later you call to confirm they went — **navigation and closing the loop**. You get her permission and a signed release, then make sure the school nurse and the clinic have the same plan — **coordination**.

When the landlord ignores the mold, you help her write the complaint and rehearse the call, then step in yourself when the code office gives her wrong information — **coaching self-advocacy, then advocacy**. You notice she is the fourth tenant in that building with the same problem, and you document the pattern for your supervisor — **systems advocacy**.

Over the next months she calls the clinic herself, tracks her son's symptoms on the kitchen calendar she chose, and starts telling other mothers at the food distribution what to ask for — **capacity building**, and she has become a community asset.

Your records show one enrollment, six contacts, one class, and two referrals — **outputs**. They also show a child who returned to school, an asthma action plan the school and clinic both hold, and a mold violation cited — **outcomes**. Only the second set answers the question anyone is really asking.

## Practice

1. **Role identification across the course.** Name the primary C3 role in each, and the secondary role if there is one.
   - You spend Thursday afternoons at a laundromat with a blood pressure cuff and a folding table.
   - You walk a twelve-block radius recording every food store, bus stop, and park.
   - You tell a client's care team that her "noncompliance" is a night shift.
   - You count the number of classes held last quarter for a funder report.
   - You rehearse a phone call with a client until she can make it herself.
   - You notice nine clients this month were turned away from the same intake line.
   - You explain what a deductible is, in Spanish, at a kitchen table.
2. **Outputs or outcomes.** Sort each and say what it would take to measure the outcome version of every output: 240 home visits completed; 18 clients enrolled in Medicaid; 6 nutrition classes delivered; participants' self-reported vegetable intake increased; 92 blood pressure screenings; 14 clients with blood pressure controlled at six months; 1,200 flyers distributed; 3 families rehoused; 74 referrals made; 61 referrals confirmed attended.
3. **Design an outreach plan.** Pick a population that is not showing up — night-shift workers, older adults who do not leave home, teenage mothers, or day laborers. Write a one-page plan: three specific settings and why, the gatekeeper at each and how you approach them, what you offer before you ask for anything, how often you return before expecting results, exactly what you record about each contact, and one honest thing you will say about the limits of what you can promise.
4. **Trust repair.** A community avoids your program because a previous project collected data and never returned results. Write what you say in your first five minutes at a community meeting. Then write what you would say if someone asks, "What happens to what you write down about me?" and you know the answer includes sharing with a funder.
5. **Windshield survey.** Walk or ride a real ten-block area and record systematically: food sources and what fresh produce costs, pharmacies, health services and their hours, transit, sidewalks and lighting, parks and their condition, languages on signs, vacant properties, gathering places. Produce a one-page written picture of what it would be like to manage a chronic condition living there.
6. **Asset map.** From the same area, build an asset map across all five categories, with at least three entries in each. Then name three specific ways your program would use it — a messenger, a venue, and an existing practice to build on rather than replace. Mark which entries you inferred from the outside and would need to confirm with a resident.
7. **Structured or informal.** For each, choose a validated tool or a guided conversation, and justify it: an intake appointment for a program that must report social needs data; a first contact in a doorway with someone who is clearly wary; a client in acute crisis; a scheduled six-month check-in; a conversation at a bus stop during outreach. Then write the first three questions you would actually ask in the doorway situation.
8. **Data quality flag.** Take one form or field from your placement, or invent a realistic one, and write a report to your supervisor naming a specific problem with it, the effect on the program's conclusions, and a proposed fix. Two paragraphs, no blame.

**Deliverable:** the labeled role-identification answers, the sorted outputs and outcomes table with your proposed outcome measures, the one-page outreach plan, the written trust-repair statements, the windshield survey picture, the five-category asset map with intended uses and confirmation marks, the assessment-approach justifications with your three doorway questions, and the data quality report.
