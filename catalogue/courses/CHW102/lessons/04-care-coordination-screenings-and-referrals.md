## The navigation role

The C3 framework calls this role care coordination, case management, and system navigation. In practice it is the answer to a question people ask constantly and rarely get answered: *where do I go, and how do I get in?*

The systems a client has to cross — health care, insurance, housing, food assistance, behavioral health, immigration, schools, utilities — were each designed separately, each with its own eligibility rules, forms, hours, phone trees, and vocabulary. Navigating them well is a specialized skill, and someone who is working two jobs, is sick, or is new to the country cannot reasonably be expected to have it. That gap is the whole reason your role exists.

Two boundaries frame the role. You are not a licensed case manager or social worker; where your program distinguishes CHW navigation from clinical case management, know the line and respect it. And navigation is never only a phone number. Handing someone a phone number is not a referral; it is a wish.

## Screenings: what CHWs do and don't do

Screening is a common piece of this work and one of the easiest places to drift out of scope, so be precise about it.

CHWs commonly **administer** standardized screening instruments under a program protocol: social needs or health-related social needs screeners covering food, housing, transportation, utilities, and safety; brief depression or anxiety screeners; substance use screeners; and, where trained and authorized, basic measurements such as blood pressure, height and weight, or a blood glucose check.

What you do with a screening is fixed by protocol, not by judgment:

- **Administer it as written.** Do not reword items to be gentler, skip ones that feel awkward, or fill in what you assume the client would say. Changed wording invalidates the tool.
- **Record the result accurately**, including refusals and unanswered items.
- **Follow the protocol for the result.** Every screening tool your program uses should come with a written rule for what a positive result triggers, and an immediate-danger rule — a suicidal ideation item endorsed, a blood pressure in a critical range — that overrides everything else. Know these before you screen anyone.
- **Explain the purpose plainly and get consent.** "These questions help us find out what kind of help might be useful. You can skip anything you don't want to answer."

What you never do: interpret a score as a diagnosis, tell a client they have a condition, adjust a treatment on the basis of a reading, or reassure someone that a number is fine. A screening result is a signal that routes a person to the right next step. "Your reading today was 158 over 96. That's higher than we want to see, and I'd like to get you in with the nurse today" is inside scope. "You have hypertension" is not.

Screening also creates an obligation. Do not ask about needs you have no path to address. Asking a family whether they ran out of food and then offering nothing damages trust more than not asking.

## Matching the need to the resource

The first competency of this role is accuracy: identifying what a client actually needs and matching it to a resource that will actually work. Both halves fail routinely.

**Find the real need.** The stated need is often a symptom of a different one. "I keep missing my appointments" may be transportation, may be a work schedule, may be childcare, may be a receptionist who was rude in a language the client barely follows. Ask what happened the last time, in detail, rather than solving the sentence you were handed.

**Then run four filters before you name a resource.**

*Eligibility.* Does the client qualify — income, residency, immigration status, age, insurance, diagnosis, catchment area? Sending someone to a program that will turn them away costs them a day of work and costs you their trust.

*Access.* Can they physically get there and get in? Hours against their shift, distance and bus routes, whether the intake is online, whether they have a phone, an address, a document, a computer. A service is only real if the client can reach it.

*Capacity.* Is it accepting people this month? A six-month waitlist is a fact the client needs on day one, not in month five. Ask about waitlists explicitly, and have a second option ready.

*Fit.* Will the client use it? Language, culture, staff who look like them, past experience with the agency, whether asking for help there feels safe given their immigration status or history. A well-matched second-best resource beats a perfect one the client will not walk into.

**Keep your directory alive.** Resource information decays fast — programs close, funding ends, hours change, the person who took your calls leaves. Verify before you refer if you have not used a resource recently, note the date you last confirmed it, and keep a real contact name where you can. A CHW with a maintained directory and two working relationships at each agency is worth more than one with a printed list of two hundred.

## Making the referral actually happen

**Warm handoff beats cold referral.** A warm handoff means you make the connection while the client is with you: you call together, you introduce them by name to a person, you book the appointment before they leave, or you walk them down the hall. A cold referral is a name and number handed over with a hope attached. Warm handoffs succeed far more often, and the difference is largest for exactly the clients with the most barriers.

**Help with the mechanics — with them, not for them.** Sit beside the client while they call, with you ready to take over if the phone tree defeats them. Read the form aloud and let them answer. Explain what a document is for before asking for it. This is slower than doing it yourself, and it is the difference between a client who needed you once and a client who needs you every time. Do it for them when the barrier is real and immediate — a crisis, a disability, a language the agency cannot serve — and say out loud what you are doing and why.

**Prepare them for the encounter.** What to bring, what it will cost, how long it takes, what they will be asked, what to do if they are turned away, and how to reach you if it goes wrong. Uncertainty is itself a barrier.

**Document as you go.** What was referred, to whom, when, why, what the client agreed to, and when you will follow up.

## Closing the loop

This is the competency that separates a good navigator from a busy one: **a referral is complete only when the client actually connected.** Not when you gave the information. Not when you made the call. When the client got the service.

A closed-loop referral has four states, and your job is to move each one to the end:

1. **Made** — the referral was placed and the client agreed to it.
2. **Scheduled** — an appointment or intake date exists.
3. **Attended** — the client showed up and was seen.
4. **Resolved** — the need was met, or you know exactly why it was not and what happens next.

Track them. A simple log with the client, the need, the agency, the date referred, the appointment date, the follow-up date, the status, and the barrier found is enough, and it is not optional. Without it, referrals disappear silently and the ones that fail are exactly the ones you never hear about.

Follow up on a rhythm: shortly after the referral to confirm an appointment exists, shortly after the appointment date to find out what happened, and again if the need is still open. Then troubleshoot what you find, because the reasons repeat:

- No transportation — arrange a bus pass, a ride program, or a closer site.
- The number was wrong or nobody answered — call it yourself, then correct the directory.
- Turned away for eligibility — find out the exact rule, appeal it if it was applied wrongly, and refer elsewhere.
- Waitlisted — get the position and expected date, and bridge with something else in the meantime.
- Afraid to go — find out of what. Immigration enforcement, cost, past disrespect, and a diagnosis they do not want confirmed are all common and all answerable.
- Did not go and will not say why — ask without judgment, and ask whether the goal is still one they want.

When a referral fails for a reason that is not the client's — a program that never returns calls, an intake line that hangs up on non-English speakers, an eligibility rule applied inconsistently — you have found a systems problem. Document it and take it to your supervisor. That is the bridge into lesson 05.

## Coordinating across providers

Some clients are not navigating one system but five, each of which thinks it is the only one. Coordination is the work of making those services connect instead of collide.

**Consent comes first.** You may not share a client's information between agencies without their permission, and for health information that permission usually has to be a signed release specifying who may share what, with whom, and for how long. Explain in plain language what would be shared and why, get the release signed before you make the call, and honor the client's limits — including their right to keep one part of their life out of the conversation. Confidentiality rules are covered fully in CHW105; treat the release as non-negotiable here.

**Know who leads.** Where there is a care manager, a primary care provider, or a lead agency, your role is to support and inform, not to direct. Where there is nobody, the client is doing it alone and coordination is exactly what you are adding.

**Watch for duplication and contradiction.** Two agencies enrolling the same family in the same program, two providers giving different instructions, three staff asking a client to retell the same trauma. Naming these in one call saves the client hours and prevents the harm of contradictory advice.

**Sequence the steps.** Some things must happen in order — the physical before the enrollment, the identity document before the application, the housing intake before the deposit assistance. Lay it out for the client as a sequence with dates.

**Communicate concisely and in scope.** When you brief another provider, say who you are and your role, what the client consented to, the concrete facts, and what you are asking for. Report what you observed and what the client told you. Do not offer a clinical assessment.

**Keep the client at the center.** Coordination goes wrong when professionals begin arranging a life among themselves. The client should know who is talking to whom, why, and what was decided — and should be in the room whenever a decision about them is being made.

## Practice

1. **Role identification.** For each, say whether the primary role is screening, navigation, coordination across providers, or something outside CHW scope.
   - You administer a nine-item depression screener and the client endorses the self-harm item.
   - You call a housing agency and a behavioral health clinic to stop them scheduling on the same morning.
   - A client shows you a blood pressure of 96 over 58 and asks if she should keep taking her pill.
   - You sit with a client while she completes a food assistance application online.
   - You learn the client never went to the appointment you booked six weeks ago.
2. **Screening protocol drill.** Take one screening tool your program uses and write, on one page: the exact opening you say to obtain consent; the immediate-danger items and what you do the moment one is endorsed; the rule for a positive result; what you record; and three sentences you must never say about a result. Then have a partner throw two curveballs at you — a client who wants to skip a question, and one who asks "does this mean I'm depressed?" — and write your answers.
3. **Four-filter matching.** A client needs dental care. She works 6 a.m. to 3 p.m. Monday to Saturday, has no car, speaks Mixtec and some Spanish, is uninsured, and is undocumented. Take any three dental resources you can find or invent plausibly, and run each through eligibility, access, capacity, and fit. Pick one, name the risk you are accepting, and write the backup.
4. **Referral walk-through.** Using the client from exercise 3, write out the entire referral end to end: the words of the warm handoff call, what you tell her to bring and expect, what you document, your follow-up dates, and the four loop states with the evidence you would need to mark each one complete.
5. **Follow-up call role-play.** In pairs. The client did not attend. The observer assigns the real reason secretly from this list — no bus fare, afraid of immigration enforcement at the clinic, the receptionist was rude, she went and was turned away for a missing document, she decided she does not want dental care right now. The CHW has five minutes to find the reason without judgment and agree on a next step. Debrief on which questions opened the client up and which closed her down.
6. **Barrier troubleshooting.** For each reason in exercise 5, write the concrete next step, and mark which ones are systems problems to escalate rather than client problems to solve.
7. **Coordination map.** A client has a primary care provider, a behavioral health counselor, a housing case manager, a probation officer, and a child's school nurse. Draw who needs to talk to whom, mark exactly which conversations require a signed release, identify one likely duplication and one likely contradiction, and write the three-sentence briefing you would give the primary care provider. Then write the sentence you would use to tell the client what you are about to do.

**Deliverable:** the labeled role-identification answers, the one-page screening protocol with your curveball responses, the four-filter comparison with your choice and backup, the complete written referral walk-through, follow-up role-play debrief notes, the barrier table with escalation marks, and the coordination map with the briefing and the client-notification sentence.
