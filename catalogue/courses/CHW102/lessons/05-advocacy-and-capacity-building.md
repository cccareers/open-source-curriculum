## Two roles that need each other

The C3 framework lists advocating for individuals and communities and building individual and community capacity as separate roles, and they are. They are taught together because each one, alone, goes wrong.

Advocacy without capacity building produces clients who get results only when you are in the room. Capacity building without advocacy tells people to speak up inside systems that are not listening, and leaves the barrier standing. The professional judgment this lesson builds is knowing, in a given moment, which one the situation calls for — and knowing that the long-run goal of both is a client who needs you less.

## Individual advocacy: speaking up

Individual advocacy means acting on behalf of one client who is facing a barrier to care or services. It is the role that most often makes the difference between a client getting something and not getting it.

**Advocate when a barrier is unfair, wrong, or disproportionate**, and the client cannot remove it alone: a benefit denied against the agency's own rules; an appointment refused because no interpreter was arranged; a client dismissed, rushed, or spoken to disrespectfully; a rule applied to this person and not to others; a policy applied so rigidly that it defeats its own purpose. Advocate less quickly when the decision is correct and the client simply does not like it, when the client has not been asked whether they want you to, or when your first move should be teaching them to do it themselves.

**Always start with the client's consent and their goal.** Ask what outcome they want and what they want you to do. Some clients want you to lead, some want you beside them, some want nothing said at all because they must keep using this clinic after you leave. Advocacy done to a client rather than with them is a power move dressed as help, and the client bears the consequences.

**Escalate on a ladder, one rung at a time.**

1. **Clarify.** Half of apparent denials are misunderstandings. "Can you help me understand the rule here?" resolves more cases than any other sentence.
2. **Ask, with the specifics.** Name the client, the request, the rule, and the outcome you want: "Ms. Okafor was told she needs proof of address, but the policy lists a utility bill or a letter from a shelter, and she has the letter. Can we proceed with that?"
3. **Ask for the supervisor.** Politely, without heat, and after you have given the first person a genuine chance.
4. **Use the formal channel.** Appeals, grievances, patient relations, an ombudsman, a state complaint line, legal aid. Deadlines matter, so find them early.
5. **Bring your own organization in.** Your supervisor, and where relationships exist between agencies, a call at that level.

**Do it in a way that works next month too.** Be factual, calm, specific about the rule, and generous about the person in front of you — they are usually enforcing a policy they did not write. Anger occasionally wins a case and reliably costs you the relationship that would have won the next ten. Say what you want, not just what is wrong.

**Document everything**: date, who you spoke to, what was said, what was decided, what rule was cited. A record turns "they keep denying people" into a case, and it is the raw material for the systems work below.

**Stay in role.** You advocate for access, respect, accuracy, and fair application of rules. You do not argue clinical decisions, tell a provider what to prescribe, or represent a client legally.

## Systems advocacy: the pattern behind the case

The fourth time you fight the same barrier for a different client, you are no longer looking at a case. You are looking at a system, and the appropriate response is different.

Systems-level advocacy in a CHW's daily practice looks like this: you notice a pattern; you document it in a form somebody can act on — how many clients, over what period, with what consequence; you bring it to your supervisor, who has channels you do not; you supply the frontline evidence when your organization takes it to a policy table; and you represent the community's interest in the settings you are invited into, such as advisory boards, coalitions, and community meetings.

CHWs are uniquely positioned for this because you are the only person who sees the same barrier from twenty different households. A clinic manager sees complaints as individual events. You see the shape.

Three cautions. Report through your organization's process rather than freelancing — public advocacy on your employer's behalf usually has rules, and CHW105 covers the professional boundaries in full. Keep client-identifying details out of pattern reports unless you have explicit consent. And describe the effect, not the villain: "Eleven of the fourteen families I referred in March could not complete the online-only intake, and nine gave up" moves a system that "the intake process is discriminatory" will not.

## Coaching self-advocacy

The higher-value half of the advocacy role is the part where you do not speak. A client who can advocate for themselves carries that capability into every future encounter, including the ones you will never know about.

**Prepare before the encounter.** Sit down with the client and build a written list of their questions and their goal for the visit, in their words, in priority order — the first two are what will actually get asked. Write down what they need to say about their situation, and what they are afraid will happen. Rehearse: you play the receptionist or the provider, they say it out loud. The first attempt is usually inaudible; by the third it is not.

**Give them language.** Most people are not short of courage; they are short of sentences. Give them phrases they can borrow: "I don't understand that word, can you say it another way?"; "I need an interpreter, and I know it's free"; "Can you write down what I should do?"; "I can't afford that — is there another option?"; "I'd like to speak with a supervisor"; "Before we finish, I have two questions on this paper."

**Accompany without taking over.** If you go with them, agree in advance on your role: silent unless asked, note-taker, or a person who steps in only if a specific thing happens. Then hold to it. The temptation to rescue is strongest exactly when the client is doing the hard thing successfully. Sit slightly to the side, let silences last, and let them answer questions addressed to them.

**Debrief afterward.** What went well, what they would do differently, what to do next time. Name a specific thing they did well — people rarely notice their own competence — and ask whether they feel ready to do the next one alone.

Choose between advocating and coaching by asking three questions: is there time (an emergency is not a teaching moment), what does the client want, and what is the stake if it goes badly? High stakes and low time mean you advocate. Repeated, lower-stakes encounters mean you coach. Often you do both — you coach the client for the appointment and make one call yourself about the interpreter.

## Strengths-based practice

Capacity building starts with what people already have. Most systems a client touches are organized around deficits: intake forms record problems, referrals record needs, notes record risks. A person read entirely through that record looks like a collection of failures, and people who are treated as a collection of failures perform like one.

Strengths-based practice insists that every client and every community already has assets — skills, knowledge, relationships, resources, and a track record of surviving hard things. Those assets are the material a plan is built from, and naming them is not flattery. It is accuracy.

Find them by asking: What are you good at? What has gotten you through hard times before? Who is in your corner? What do people come to you for? What is already working, even a little? What did you do last time this happened?

Look across four kinds of asset — personal (persistence, humor, faith, bilingualism, budgeting, a job held for nine years), relational (family, neighbors, congregation, coworkers, a group chat), material (a car, a phone, insurance, a stable address, a garden), and community (a clinic that treats people well, a food program, a trusted pastor, an association that turns out for its own).

The reframe is a practical discipline, not a positive attitude:

- "Noncompliant with medication" becomes "manages three medications correctly and stops the fourth when it makes her too dizzy to work."
- "Chaotic household, unreliable" becomes "cares for two children and an ill parent with no second adult, and has kept every appointment scheduled after 4 p.m."
- "Low education" becomes "learns quickly by watching and remembers everything explained out loud."

Each reframe holds the same facts and points to a different plan. The first version of each ends at a label; the second tells you what to build on and what to change.

The same applies to communities. A neighborhood described only as high-crime, low-income, and underserved gets programs done to it. The same neighborhood described as having six congregations, a tenants' association, two corner stores that extend credit, and an informal network of grandmothers who watch each other's children gets programs built with it. Lesson 06 turns this into asset mapping.

## Building independence

The core of the capacity building role is teaching skills that reduce the client's dependence on you: making and keeping appointments, calling an agency and getting past a phone tree, reading a bill or a benefits letter, self-monitoring a condition according to their care plan, refilling a prescription, keeping their own documents in one place, knowing their rights and when to invoke them.

**Do with, not for.** This is the whole method. Doing it for the client is faster today and produces a client who cannot do it tomorrow.

**Use gradual release, and be honest about which stage you are in.**

*I do, you watch.* You make the call while the client listens, and you narrate what you are doing and why — the menu option, the words that work, what you write down.

*We do together.* The client makes the call with you beside them, prompting when they stall, ready to take over only if it collapses.

*You do, I watch.* The client makes the call while you sit there, silent, and let the pause be uncomfortable. Debrief afterward.

*You do.* They do it alone and tell you how it went.

Most CHWs get stuck in the first stage because it is faster and because clients ask them to. Say the plan out loud at the start: "Today I'll do this one and you'll watch how I do it. Next week you'll do it and I'll be right here."

**Build the client's own tools with them** — a card with their conditions, medications, allergies, and pharmacy; a folder for their documents; a written list of their agencies with a contact name; a plain-language explanation of their insurance in their language. Made together, these outlive the relationship.

**Then step back deliberately.** Reduce contact as capability grows, hand tasks over one at a time, and say what you are doing: "You've handled the last three yourself. Let's move to checking in once a month, and you call me if something comes up." Plan the ending from the beginning, and make sure the client leaves knowing who to call when you are gone.

Success in this role feels like being needed less. That is uncomfortable, especially when the relationship matters to you too, and it is the correct outcome. Watch for the dependency trap in yourself: if every client's problems still route through you after a year, you have been solving instead of building.

## Practice

1. **Advocate or coach.** For each situation decide whether you advocate, coach self-advocacy, or do both, and justify it using time, client preference, and stake.
   - A client is refused an interpreter at the front desk and her appointment starts in ten minutes.
   - A client is nervous about asking his doctor to explain a new diagnosis at a visit next Thursday.
   - A benefits application has been denied for a reason the agency's own policy does not support.
   - A client wants to ask a landlord to fix a mold problem she believes is worsening her son's asthma.
   - A client tells you the clinic staff talk down to her every single visit, and she has six more visits scheduled.
2. **The escalation ladder.** Take the benefits denial from exercise 1 and write what you say at each of the five rungs, including the exact wording of rung 2. Then write the sentence you use to ask the client's permission before starting, and what you would do if she says she does not want you to escalate.
3. **Pre-appointment coaching role-play.** In pairs. The client has ten minutes with a specialist next week and has never asked a doctor a question. Build the written question list together, give them three borrowable sentences, and rehearse the opening twice. Then run the appointment with the observer playing a provider who interrupts and uses jargon. Debrief: what did the client manage alone, and where did the CHW rescue them unnecessarily?
4. **Strengths reframe.** Rewrite each as a strengths-based statement that keeps every fact: "Client is disorganized and misses appointments"; "Family is resistant to the care plan"; "Client has poor health literacy and cannot follow written instructions"; "Neighborhood is unsafe and residents are disengaged." For each, write the one sentence describing what your plan would now build on.
5. **Asset interview.** Interview a partner about a real difficulty they handled — no health details required — using only strengths-finding questions. Produce a list of at least eight assets across the four categories. Read it back to them and record their reaction. Most people are surprised.
6. **Gradual release plan.** Choose one navigation skill (calling an agency, refilling a prescription, or reading a benefits letter). Write a four-session plan across the stages, including exactly what you say at each stage, what the client does, how you know they are ready to move on, and the sentence you use to announce the handover.
7. **Dependency audit.** Take one client relationship from your placement or a realistic composite. List everything you currently do for them. Mark each item as must-do-for-them, could-do-together, or should-have-handed-over already. For the third group, write when and how you will hand it over, and what could go wrong.

**Deliverable:** the labeled advocate-or-coach decisions with justifications, the five-rung escalation script with the consent sentence, coaching role-play debrief notes, the four strengths reframes with their plan sentences, the asset interview list and reaction, the four-session gradual release plan, and the dependency audit with a handover plan.
