## What Trauma-Informed Practice Is

Trauma-informed practice means designing every interaction on the assumption that the person in front of you may be carrying trauma you know nothing about, and that your service should not add to it.

It is not therapy. You are not treating trauma, processing it, or asking anyone to disclose it. It is not a specialty program for certain clients either — it is the way you run an intake, a home visit, a group session, and a phone call, for everyone, including the clients who seem fine and the ones who seem difficult.

The shift is best captured in a single question. Instead of asking, silently or aloud, "what is wrong with this person?", you ask "what happened to this person, and what do they need from me because of it?" That reframe changes what you do with a client who is hostile at the front desk, who cancels three times, who will not sit with their back to the door, or who goes flat and silent when you mention a specific clinic.

Trauma is common. Childhood adversity, community and interpersonal violence, medical trauma, incarceration, immigration enforcement, displacement, racism experienced over a lifetime, and the ordinary grief of loss all leave marks. You will never know who is carrying what, which is exactly why the approach has to be universal.

## The Five Principles

These five run through everything else in this lesson.

**Safety.** Physical and emotional. The room, the schedule, and the tone all count. Predictability is a form of safety: say what will happen, then do that.

**Trustworthiness and transparency.** No surprises. Explain what you are doing and why, what gets written down, who can see it, what you are required to report, and how long things take. Then keep every commitment you make, including small ones. A helper who is reliable in small things is believable in large ones.

**Choice.** Trauma takes away control; good practice hands it back in whatever quantity is available. Offer real options — where to sit, when to meet, what to talk about first, what to skip today, whether to have someone else present, whether to answer a question at all.

**Collaboration.** Work with the client rather than on them. They set the priorities; you bring the map of what is possible. Power in the relationship is real, and sharing it deliberately is the work.

**Empowerment.** Build on strengths that are already there. A person who survived what they survived has capabilities. Name them accurately, without flattery, and design services so the client leaves more able to act on their own than when they arrived.

Applied to an ordinary intake, that looks like: telling the client how long this will take and what you will ask about; letting them choose a seat; saying "you can skip anything you don't want to answer, and it will not affect what you get from us"; explaining what goes into the file before the first question rather than after; and ending with what happens next and exactly when.

## Recognizing Trauma Responses

Trauma responses are protective reflexes, not misbehavior. They show up in bodies and behavior before they show up in words, and they are most visible when a person feels cornered, evaluated, or unsafe.

The familiar patterns:

- **Fight** — irritation, argument, sharp questions, hostility toward staff. Frequently read as "difficult client," and frequently the last defense of someone who expects to be dismissed.
- **Flight** — cancellations, no-shows, leaving early, changing the subject, physically edging toward the door.
- **Freeze** — going quiet and still, flat affect, "I don't know" to everything, appearing to have stopped listening.
- **Fawn** — over-agreeing, saying yes to everything, apologizing constantly, working hard to keep you happy. This one is easy to miss because it looks like an ideal client, and it produces plans the person never intended to follow.

The physical and behavioral cues that precede those: breathing that gets shallow or fast, a jaw or fists tightening, hands shaking, a leg bouncing, eye contact dropping away or locking on hard, a voice going quieter or higher, sitting facing an exit, flinching at a raised voice or a sudden movement, sweating in a cool room, or a sudden shift from engaged to blank mid-sentence.

Read cues as signals, not diagnoses. What you notice is that something changed, not why. Two rules apply: interpret against this person's own baseline rather than a general standard, and remember that cue meanings differ across cultures — reduced eye contact may be respect, not withdrawal. When you are unsure, ask gently instead of concluding.

## Responding in the Moment

When you see a cue, the goal is to restore safety and control, not to keep the agenda on schedule.

Slow everything down. Drop your volume slightly and lengthen your pauses; people co-regulate with the calmest nervous system in the room. Increase physical space rather than closing it, and never touch a client to comfort them.

Then say something that returns choice. Some sentences worth having ready:

```text
"We can stop here. There's no rush."
"You don't have to answer that. We can skip it entirely."
"Would it help to take a break, or to keep going?"
"I noticed that got harder. Do you want to tell me what would help right now?"
"You're in charge of what we talk about today."
```

If someone is clearly overwhelmed, help them re-orient to the present: name where you both are and what time it is, invite them to notice their feet on the floor or the chair supporting them, offer water, offer the window. Keep it simple and optional.

And know your exits. If a client discloses trauma, expresses thoughts of harming themselves or someone else, or is in acute crisis, that is a referral to a licensed behavioral health professional or crisis service, following your organization's protocol — immediately, and warmly. Your role is to stay present, keep them safe, and connect them. It is not to counsel, and it is not to handle it alone.

## Not Making It Worse

Retraumatization is usually accidental and usually structural. The most common causes are worth memorizing because you can design around all of them.

Do not make people repeat their story. If the information exists in the record or came from a partner agency, read it before the visit. Asking someone to narrate the worst thing that happened to them for a fourth intake is a harm the system inflicts casually.

Do not ask for detail you do not need. You need to know what support is required, not what happened. "Is there anything I should know so I can set this up in a way that works for you?" gets you what you need without a demand for disclosure.

Warn before sensitive territory, and offer the option to decline: "The next few questions are about your housing history, and some of them are personal. You can pass on any of them."

Watch the environment. Long unexplained waits, locked doors, a chair with its back to an open corridor, someone standing over a seated client, raised voices in a hallway, uniformed security in a small space, a room with only one exit blocked by staff — each of these is a routine feature of service settings and a live threat cue for some people. You can move a chair, explain a delay, and sit rather than stand.

Watch the paperwork. Rigid rules with no stated reason, threats framed as policy ("if you miss again you'll be discharged"), and demands for documents a person cannot obtain all reproduce the experience of being at the mercy of a system. Where you cannot change the rule, explain it honestly and help them meet it.

Finally, do not force disclosure as a condition of help, and do not promise confidentiality you cannot deliver. Say what your reporting obligations are up front, before anyone tells you something they assumed was private.

## Empathy and Non-Judgment Under Pressure

The hardest moments are not the sad ones. They are the ones where a client is angry at you, has done something you find hard to accept, or is making a choice you believe will hurt them.

Non-judgmental language is a discipline, not a personality trait. Normalize rather than react: "A lot of people in that situation would have done the same thing." Reflect rather than evaluate: "That sounds exhausting" instead of "you should have called sooner." Stay with the person rather than correcting the choice: "I hear you. Can I tell you what I'm worried about?" — and then only if they say yes.

The things to strip out of your speech are advice that starts with "you should," why-questions that function as accusations ("why didn't you take it?" becomes "what got in the way?"), reassurance that erases ("at least it wasn't worse"), and any story of your own that moves the attention to you.

Anger directed at you is usually not about you. It is often the only safe expression of a much larger powerlessness. Do not defend, do not match volume, and do not take it personally in the moment. Acknowledge the substance — "you're right that you've been passed around, and that's not okay" — and stay.

## Carrying This Work

Hearing about trauma repeatedly changes you. Secondary traumatic stress is real and it is an occupational reality, not a weakness: sleep disruption, intrusive thoughts about clients, numbness, cynicism, dread before certain visits, or a sense that nothing you do matters.

Trauma-informed organizations extend these principles to staff. Use supervision for what it is for. Debrief hard visits rather than driving home with them. Hold boundaries around hours and contact. Notice which clients' stories stay with you and treat that as information about yourself worth attending to, ideally with professional support. A CHW running on empty cannot offer anyone safety.

## Practice

1. **Read the cues.** In pairs, run a five-minute intake. The client silently chooses one response pattern — fight, flight, freeze, or fawn — and plays it without announcing it. The CHW names, out loud and in the moment, what they are noticing and adjusts. Afterward the client says which pattern it was and what actually helped. Swap and repeat with a different pattern.

2. **Redesign an intake.** Take a real intake process you know. Walk through it step by step and mark every point where safety, trustworthiness, choice, collaboration, or empowerment is weak. Rewrite the opening ninety seconds — the exact words — so all five principles are present before the first question is asked.

3. **The angry client.** A client raises his voice in a waiting area because he was told to come back with a document nobody mentioned last time. Script your first four sentences. Then mark what each one is doing: de-escalating, acknowledging, restoring control, or offering a next step. Cut anything that defends the agency before acknowledging him.

4. **Environment audit.** Walk through the physical space where you will serve clients, or one you know well. List every feature that could read as a threat cue — sightlines, exits, seating, noise, signage, waiting time, staff posture. Mark which three you could change this week, and write the request you would make to a supervisor for one you could not change yourself.

5. **Know your limit.** Write out, word for word, what you would say if a client began disclosing childhood abuse in the middle of a benefits appointment. Your script must stay present with them, avoid asking for detail, name your role honestly, and end with a warm referral. Practice it aloud until it does not sound like a policy.
