## Your Scope, Stated Before Anything Else

Community health workers educate, support, and connect. You do not diagnose, you do not interpret test results, and you never advise anyone on what medication to take, how much, or whether to stop. Questions about dosing, side effects, interactions, or whether a drug is "working" go to a pharmacist or the prescribing provider — every time, without exception, no matter how confident you feel or how simple the question sounds.

This is not timidity. A CHW who guesses at a dosing question can cause real harm, and one wrong answer destroys the credibility that makes the rest of your work possible. The care team trusts you precisely because you hold this line.

What you *can* do is large: explain in plain language what a condition is and why management matters, help a client understand the questions they want to ask their provider, help them build routines that make a treatment plan survivable, teach self-monitoring skills, and notice when something needs escalation. That is the content of this lesson.

## Diabetes in Plain Language

Your body runs on sugar in the blood — glucose — which comes mostly from food. Insulin is the hormone that lets glucose move out of the blood and into cells where it gets used. In diabetes, either the body does not make enough insulin, or the cells stop responding to it well. Either way, glucose stays in the blood instead of getting used.

Why it matters: glucose sitting at high levels for years quietly damages small blood vessels and nerves. That damage shows up as vision loss, kidney disease, nerve pain and numbness in the feet, slow-healing wounds, and higher risk of heart attack and stroke. Almost none of this hurts early, which is exactly the problem — people feel fine while damage accumulates.

What support looks like: help the client keep appointments and lab work, understand the plan their provider gave them, take medication consistently, monitor when they have been asked to, keep moving, and get eyes and feet checked. Feet matter more than most people expect. A daily look at the bottoms of both feet, using a mirror if needed, catches sores before they become emergencies.

Escalate rather than manage: shakiness, sweating, confusion, or a very low meter reading may be low blood sugar and needs fast attention. Any wound on a foot that is not healing goes to a provider now, not at the next scheduled visit.

## High Blood Pressure in Plain Language

Blood pressure is the force of blood pushing against the walls of the arteries. It gets written as two numbers — the top number is the pressure when the heart beats, the bottom number is the pressure between beats. When that force stays too high over time, it wears out the arteries, forces the heart to work harder, and damages the kidneys, eyes, and brain.

Why it matters: hypertension is the clearest example of a condition with no symptoms and severe consequences. People routinely feel completely normal at pressures that are steadily raising their risk of stroke and heart failure. Clients will often say "I feel fine, so I stopped taking it." That sentence is the single most important thing you will hear in this work, and it is a normal, understandable conclusion from the evidence they have.

What support looks like: normalize that feeling fine is expected, help them see the numbers over time rather than one reading, support the routine of taking medication daily, and help with the practical side — sodium in packaged and restaurant food, movement, sleep, and stress. Do not lecture. The client already knows salt is a factor.

Escalate rather than manage: a very high reading with chest pain, trouble breathing, sudden severe headache, weakness on one side, or trouble speaking is an emergency.

## Asthma in Plain Language

In asthma, the airways in the lungs are easily irritated. When they react, they tighten, swell, and fill with mucus, so the person cannot move air well. That shows up as wheezing, coughing, chest tightness, and shortness of breath.

There are usually two kinds of inhaler and the difference matters enormously to clients: a rescue inhaler used to open the airways during an attack, and a controller used every day to keep inflammation down even when the person feels well. Clients frequently stop the daily controller because they feel fine, and then rely on the rescue inhaler more and more. Someone using their rescue inhaler frequently is telling you their asthma is not controlled, and that belongs in front of a provider. Some clients are prescribed a single combination inhaler that serves both purposes; the client's written asthma action plan, not a general rule, says which inhaler is used when, so ask to see it rather than assuming.

What support looks like: help identify triggers in the actual home and workplace — mold, pests, smoke, dust, cold air, strong cleaning products, a landlord's unrepaired leak — and connect the client to housing code or remediation help when the trigger is a housing problem, which it often is. Help them ask their provider for a written asthma action plan, and make sure they can read it.

Escalate rather than manage: difficulty speaking a full sentence, lips or fingertips turning blue, or a rescue inhaler that is not helping is an emergency.

## Supporting Medication Adherence

Start by dropping the word "noncompliant." When someone is not taking medication as prescribed, there is a reason, and it is almost never that they do not care.

Common reasons, all of which have different responses:

- **Cost.** They are splitting pills or skipping days to stretch a prescription. Response: connect to patient assistance programs, pharmacy discount options, formulary alternatives through the provider, or a benefits enrollment worker.
- **Side effects.** Something genuinely unpleasant is happening. Response: this is a clinical conversation — help them get to the pharmacist or provider quickly, because the plan can often be changed. Never suggest they push through it, and never suggest they stop.
- **Feeling fine.** Especially with hypertension. Response: explain why silent conditions still need treatment, and support them in raising it with the provider rather than deciding alone.
- **Complexity.** Nine medications at four different times is genuinely hard. Response: pill organizers, pharmacy blister packs, asking the provider or pharmacist whether the schedule can be simplified.
- **Access.** No transportation to the pharmacy, no refills left, a mail-order shipment that never arrived. Response: this is navigation work, which is squarely yours.
- **Beliefs and history.** Distrust of pharmaceuticals, a family member harmed by a medication, a preference for other approaches. Response: ask, listen, do not argue, and make sure the provider knows what the client actually believes so the conversation can happen with the right person.

Practical strategies you can offer without crossing scope: tie the dose to an existing daily anchor like coffee or brushing teeth, set a phone alarm, use a weekly organizer filled on the same day each week, keep a single up-to-date list of every medication including over-the-counter products and supplements, and bring all the bottles to every appointment.

And the boundary again, because clients will test it kindly: "Should I take this one at night instead?" and "Is it okay to skip this since I ran out?" both get the same answer — a warm one. "That's a great question for the pharmacist, and the pharmacist is free. Let's call together right now."

## Teaching Self-Monitoring

Self-monitoring is where you build independence rather than dependence. The rule is **do with, not for**. If you do the check, the client has watched a demonstration. If the client does the check while you watch, the client has a skill.

A workable teaching sequence:

1. Ask what they already know and have tried. Most clients have partial knowledge; start there rather than from zero.
2. Explain the why in one sentence: the log is not homework for you, it is evidence the provider uses to adjust the plan.
3. Demonstrate once, slowly, narrating each step.
4. Hand it over. They do it while you watch and stay quiet unless safety requires otherwise.
5. Use teach-back: "So I know I explained it well — walk me through how you'd do this tomorrow morning." Never "do you understand?"
6. Make the log usable. Paper, phone, or wall calendar — whatever they will actually use.
7. Plan the follow-up: when you will check in, and what they should do if a reading looks alarming.

For blood glucose, the skill includes clean dry hands, using the side of a fingertip, having supplies within reach beforehand, and writing down the number with the date, time, and whether it was before or after eating. For home blood pressure, technique drives accuracy: sit for five quiet minutes first, back supported, feet flat, arm resting at heart level, correct cuff size on a bare upper arm, no talking during the reading, and no caffeine or cigarettes in the thirty minutes before. Two readings a minute apart, both recorded.

What you never do with a log: interpret it. You do not tell a client their numbers look good, look bad, or mean their medication needs changing. You help them record it accurately and get it in front of the provider — and you flag anything in an alarming range for immediate clinical attention.

## Evidence and Opinion Are Different Things

Everything above should come from your organization's approved materials or a public health source your program endorses, delivered as it is written. Your opinions about diets, supplements, alternative treatments, or which drugs are overprescribed do not belong in a client encounter, even when a client asks directly and even when you feel strongly.

Three sentences to keep ready:

```text
"I don't know — and I'd rather find out than guess."
"That's outside what I can advise on. Let's get the pharmacist on the phone."
"Here's what the guidance says. What you decide is yours, and I'll support you either way."
```

Saying "I don't know" costs you nothing and buys you credibility with both the client and the clinical team.

## Practice

1. **Plain-language explanation.** Pick one of the three conditions. In sixty seconds or less, out loud, explain to a partner what it is and why managing it matters — no medical jargon, no anatomy lecture, no scare tactics. Your partner then repeats it back in their own words. Rewrite whatever did not survive the round trip.

2. **Adherence detective.** A client with hypertension has not filled her prescription in two months. For each of the six barrier categories above, write the single question you would ask to test whether that barrier is the real one, and the first action you would take if the answer is yes. Then decide which question you would ask first and why.

3. **Teach-back a home BP check.** In pairs, teach your partner to take a home blood pressure reading using the do-with-not-for sequence. Your partner deliberately makes one technique error — crossed legs, talking during the reading, cuff over a sleeve. Correct it without embarrassing them. Then swap roles.

4. **Hold the line.** Role-play these three requests and script a response to each that refers correctly while keeping the relationship warm: "My cousin takes half a pill and she's fine — should I try that?" "This one makes me dizzy, can I just stop it?" "My sugar was 210 this morning. Is that bad?" Read your answers aloud and cut any sentence that edges toward medical advice.

## Check your understanding

1. A client says, "I feel fine, so I stopped my blood pressure pill." Which barrier category is this, and what is your response? *"Feeling fine." Normalize that high blood pressure usually has no symptoms, and support the client in raising it with the provider rather than deciding alone. Do not tell them to restart or stop anything.*
2. A client using a rescue inhaler several times a day tells you it's "working great." What do you do? *Frequent rescue use signals asthma that is not controlled; help the client get this in front of their provider soon, and check their written asthma action plan with them.*
3. Your client's glucose log shows a number you think looks high. What do you say about it? *Nothing interpretive. Help them get the log to the provider, and if the reading or symptoms fall in the range their care team flagged as urgent, follow that escalation plan.*
