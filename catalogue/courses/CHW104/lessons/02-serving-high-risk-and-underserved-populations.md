## Mistrust Is Information, Not an Obstacle

A client who does not trust you is not being difficult. In most cases they are being accurate about their history.

People who have been surveilled by systems, denied care, billed for visits they could not afford, reported to agencies by a helper, or studied without consent have learned something real: that institutions can cause harm, and that a friendly face at the door does not guarantee safety. When someone gives you short answers, cancels twice, refuses to sign anything, or asks who is going to see their file, they are running a reasonable risk assessment on you. That is a skill that kept them safe. Your job is not to argue them out of it. Your job is to give them evidence.

This reframe changes what you do next. If you treat mistrust as a defect in the client, you push — more explanation, more urgency, more paperwork. If you treat mistrust as information, you slow down, you make yourself predictable, and you let the relationship accumulate proof over time.

Three practices earn credibility faster than anything you can say:

- **Do the small thing you said you would do.** Call back Tuesday because you said Tuesday. Bring the bus pass you promised. Trust is built out of kept micro-commitments far more than out of big gestures.
- **Say plainly what you can and cannot do.** "I can get you into the clinic's Thursday slot. I cannot make the housing list move faster, and I would be lying if I said I could." Clients have been over-promised before. Honest limits are more reassuring than optimism.
- **Be explicit about information.** Who sees your notes, what goes in the record, what is required by law, what stays between you. Never let a client discover an information flow they were not told about.

## Understanding Without Assuming

Cultural responsiveness is often taught as a list of facts about groups. That version fails immediately in practice, because the client in front of you may or may not match the list, and treating them as a representative of a category is its own kind of erasure.

The working version is simpler and harder: ask, then listen, then use what you heard.

Useful questions, asked in a relaxed way rather than as an intake battery:

```text
"What do you think is going on with your health right now?"
"Who else do you talk to about medical decisions?"
"Is there anything about how care usually gets delivered that doesn't work for you?"
"What has helped before? What made things worse?"
"Are there foods, practices, or remedies that matter in your family that I should know about?"
```

Notice that none of these ask the client to explain their culture to you in the abstract. They ask about this person's values, decision-makers, prior experience, and practices — the things that actually change how you deliver a service.

Then apply what you hear. If a client tells you her adult daughter is in every medical decision, you stop scheduling calls when the daughter is at work. If a client uses a home remedy alongside prescribed treatment, you note it without mocking it and make sure the provider knows, because interactions are a clinical question and clinical questions go to clinicians. If someone tells you the last agency talked to them like a child, you adjust your register.

Two failure modes to watch in yourself. The first is stereotype-based service decisions — assuming a client cannot read, cannot afford something, does not want a specialist, or will not follow through, based on where they live or what they look like. The second is the opposite overcorrection: refusing to notice context at all, and delivering an identical script to everyone. Cultural humility sits between those. You stay curious, you check your assumptions out loud, and you accept that you will keep learning about this community for as long as you serve it.

## Adapting the Service Itself

Once you understand context, you change something. Adaptation is not a warmer tone on the same handout — it touches four concrete things.

**Materials.** Reading level, language, images, and examples. A diabetes handout that uses only foods no one in the neighborhood eats will be politely accepted and never used.

**Examples.** Use the grocery stores, bus lines, clinics, and landmarks people actually use. Specificity signals that you know the place.

**Setting.** A church basement, a barbershop, a tenant association meeting, or a front porch will often produce a conversation that a clinic exam room will not. Meet people where they already gather and already feel safe.

**Messenger.** Who delivers the message changes whether it lands. A respected elder, a peer with lived experience, a promotora, or a pastor may carry information further than you can. Sharing the platform is not losing authority; it is using the community's existing trust rather than demanding it be transferred to you.

## Formerly Incarcerated Clients

People coming home face a stack of barriers that arrive all at once and interlock: no insurance active on day one, medications that ran out at the gate, a parole or probation schedule that competes with clinic hours, housing applications that ask about convictions, employment gaps, and often untreated chronic or behavioral health conditions. Any one of these is manageable. Together, in the first thirty days, they are crushing.

Their mistrust of systems is not abstract. Many have experienced medical care inside a facility that was delayed, dismissive, or used as leverage. Some had a helper on the outside share information with a supervising officer. Assume that a request for personal information will be read through that history, and explain the reason for every question you ask.

What actually helps:

- **Sequence the barriers.** Insurance reactivation and medication continuity usually come first, because everything else depends on being physically well enough to pursue it.
- **Know the calendar constraint.** Ask about reporting requirements before you schedule anything.
- **Never make them repeat the story.** You need to know what support is required, not what happened. If a detail is not necessary for the service, do not ask for it.
- **Watch your language.** Say "person who was incarcerated," not "ex-con," "felon," or "offender." Language that follows someone into a helping relationship tells them exactly how they are seen.
- **Expect and absorb testing.** Missed appointments early in reentry are often a test of whether you disappear. Follow up the same way you would have if they had come.

## Older Adults

Older adults are not a fragile category; most are managing complicated lives with considerable skill. What changes is the shape of the work.

Many are managing several conditions and several prescribers, which means medication lists that no single provider sees in full — a strong reason to encourage bringing every bottle to every visit. Some have hearing or vision changes that make phone-based service or small-print materials useless; face the person, speak at a normal pace rather than a loud one, and offer large-print or spoken versions without making it a comment on their capacity. Transportation is frequently the single largest barrier to care. Social isolation is a health issue in its own right and often surfaces only when you ask who they talk to during the week.

The most important behavioral rule is to speak to the older adult, not about them, even when a family member is present and answering. Ask them directly who they want in the room and what decisions they want to make themselves. Autonomy is often the thing that has been quietly taken, and returning it is a service in itself.

## Communities Carrying Trauma

Some trauma is individual. Some belongs to a whole neighborhood: a history of medical experimentation, immigration enforcement at clinics, displacement, community violence, a documented pattern of dismissal at the nearest emergency department. When a community has been harmed collectively, it responds collectively, and an individual outreach strategy will underperform.

Work at community scale. Partner with organizations that already hold trust. Show up repeatedly at events where you are not recruiting anyone. Be transparent about who funds you and what data you report. Give the community something back — findings, results, a report — rather than only extracting information. And be patient with the timeline, because a community that was harmed over decades does not recalibrate in one outreach cycle. Lesson 5 develops the individual-level practice; here the point is that the same principles scale up.

## Practice

Work through all three. Write your answers, then compare with a partner, because the differences are where the learning is.

1. **The reentry intake.** A client released eleven days ago comes in for help getting his blood pressure medication restarted. He answers in single words, will not give a phone number, and asks twice who reads the form. Write out the first three things you would say, in order, and one sentence explaining what each one is meant to prove to him. Then list what you will not ask about, and why.

2. **Ask, don't assume.** A colleague tells you a new client "won't do the diabetes class because of her culture." Write five open questions you would ask the client to find out what is actually going on. Then write two possible answers she might give that would lead to completely different service adaptations, and name the adaptation for each.

3. **Adapt an education session.** You have been asked to deliver a hypertension session originally designed for a hospital waiting room. Your audience is twenty older adults at a senior center, half of whom prefer a language other than English and several of whom have low vision. Redesign it across the four adaptation levers — materials, examples, setting, messenger — with one concrete change under each. Then name the one change you expect to matter most and defend the choice in two sentences.
