## What Weight-Inclusive Means

A weight-inclusive approach puts the focus on health behaviors — eating, movement, sleep, stress, connection, access to care — rather than on body size. It does not mean pretending weight has no relationship to health. It means recognizing that behaviors are what a person can actually work on, that they improve health markers whether or not body size changes, and that treating weight itself as the target has a poor track record and a high cost.

That cost is worth naming plainly. Clients who have been lectured about their weight often avoid care entirely, sometimes for years, sometimes with a serious symptom. Weight stigma is itself associated with worse health outcomes, and the shame it produces does not motivate anyone — it makes people hide. A client who stops coming to see you cannot be helped by the most accurate nutrition information in the world.

There is also a scope reason. You are not a dietitian and you are not the client's provider. Prescribing an eating plan, setting a weight goal, or advising on a therapeutic diet for kidney disease, celiac disease, or diabetes management is clinical work that belongs to a registered dietitian or the care team. What belongs to you is general nutrition education from your program's approved materials, practical support, connection to food resources, and referral.

So the frame you work in is: behaviors over body size, added habits over forbidden foods, and referral whenever the conversation turns therapeutic.

## Why Diet Moralizing Backfires

Food language is soaked in morality. "Good" and "bad" foods, "clean" eating, "cheating," "guilty pleasure," "being good today," "earning" dessert. Most of us absorbed it without choosing it, and it will come out of your mouth in a session unless you deliberately replace it.

The problem is that moral framing turns a meal into a verdict on the person. A client who ate the "bad" thing did not just eat something — they failed. People who feel they have failed at a plan usually do not adjust it; they abandon it and stop reporting honestly, which means your information about their life gets worse at exactly the moment you need it.

Replace verdicts with information and options:

| Instead of | Say |
| --- | --- |
| "You really shouldn't be eating that." | "Tell me about a normal day of eating for you." |
| "Bad foods / good foods" | "Foods that give you steady energy" and "foods that spike quickly" |
| "You just need more willpower." | "What gets in the way of that on a busy week?" |
| "Have you tried cutting out carbs?" | "What would be realistic to change first?" |
| "You've gained weight since last time." | "How have you been feeling? What's been going on since we last talked?" |

Two habits matter more than any script. First, do not comment on a client's body — not to praise weight loss, not to note weight gain, not at all. Praise for weight loss lands badly when the cause was illness, grief, or food insecurity, and it tells the client that their body is being evaluated at every visit. Second, do not comment on your own body or diet in front of clients. Your food anxieties are not a service.

## Nutrition Content You Can Teach

General, non-therapeutic nutrition education is squarely within scope, and it is more useful when framed as adding rather than restricting.

Things worth teaching, in plain language:

- **Adding, not subtracting.** More vegetables, more fruit, more fiber, more water. Additive goals produce less shame and better follow-through than a list of bans.
- **Fiber and whole grains** slow digestion and keep energy steadier, which is why they come up constantly in diabetes education.
- **Sodium is mostly hidden.** The majority does not come from the salt shaker; it comes from packaged, canned, and restaurant food. Rinsing canned beans, choosing lower-sodium versions where the price is comparable, and cooking with herbs, citrus, garlic, and chili are practical moves.
- **Sugary drinks** are the highest-yield single change for many clients, because liquid sugar arrives fast and does not make anyone feel full.
- **Reading a label** for serving size first, then sodium, added sugar, and fiber — in that order, so people are not trying to decode the whole panel.
- **Plate-style portion guidance** as a visual rather than a measurement exercise: half the plate produce, a quarter protein, a quarter starch, adapted to how the food is actually served.
- **Regular eating.** Skipping meals to "save calories" usually backfires and is especially risky for anyone on glucose-lowering medication.

Everything therapeutic goes elsewhere. Carbohydrate counting and insulin matching, renal diets, elimination diets, weight-loss medication questions, supplements, and anything for a pregnancy with complications: refer to a dietitian or provider.

## The Food Environment Is Usually the Real Story

Before you teach a single nutrition fact, find out what is possible. A client who cannot get to a store with fresh produce, has no working stove, has thirty minutes between two shifts, or is feeding six people on a fixed budget does not need to be told that vegetables are healthy. They know.

Ask about it without making it an interrogation:

```text
"Where do you usually shop, and how do you get there?"
"Who cooks in your house, and who decides what's for dinner?"
"What kitchen equipment do you have working right now?"
"How's the food budget holding up at the end of the month?"
"What does a typical weekday look like around meals?"
```

Then act on the answers. Frozen and canned vegetables are nutritionally solid, cheap, and shelf-stable. Beans and lentils are inexpensive protein. Store brands are usually identical products. A no-cook or microwave-only plan is a legitimate plan. And a large part of this work is not education at all — it is navigation: SNAP and WIC enrollment, food pantries, produce prescription and market-match programs, congregate and home-delivered meals for older adults, school meal programs, and summer food sites.

Screen for food insecurity directly and without judgment, because it changes everything downstream: "In the last year, did you ever worry that food would run out before you had money to buy more?" If the answer is yes, the resource connection comes before the education session.

## Culture Is Not a Barrier to Nutrition

A widespread failure in nutrition education is treating a community's traditional foods as the problem to be removed. Clients hear that message immediately, and it costs you the relationship — you have just told someone that their grandmother's cooking is a health hazard.

Work inside the cuisine instead. Nearly every food tradition contains vegetables, legumes, whole grains, and cooking methods that fit any nutrition guidance you would give. Learn what the community actually eats, ask which dishes matter to them, and build the education around those dishes: preparation adjustments, portion of the plate, what goes alongside. Bring in community cooks and elders as co-teachers when you can. And leave celebration foods alone — a holiday table is not a treatment plan, and no health outcome depends on policing it.

## Choosing Materials That Fit the Audience

The default clinical handout is written several grade levels above what most adults comfortably read under stress, in one language, with unfamiliar foods pictured. Reaching for it without checking is the most common failure in this competency.

Judge a material against the audience on five points:

1. **Reading level.** Short sentences, common words, one idea per line. Aim well below the audience's assumed level — everyone reads worse when anxious, tired, or unwell.
2. **Language.** Professionally translated, not machine-translated, and reviewed by a speaker from the community. Bad translation reads as disrespect.
3. **Images and food examples.** Do the pictured people and foods look like this community? Can images carry the meaning for someone who reads very little?
4. **Design.** Large type, real contrast, generous white space, no dense paragraphs, no glossy stock-photo clutter.
5. **Actionability.** Does it end with something a person can do this week, or only with facts?

Match the method to the audience too. A demonstration or hands-on tasting beats a lecture. A grocery-store walk beats a label diagram. A recipe card, a fridge magnet, a short video, or a photo-based one-pager may all outperform a four-page brochure. And if a handout does not exist in a usable form, build a simple one — a single page with three pictures and three sentences is a legitimate teaching material.

Two rules regardless of material: never hand a document to an adult without offering to go through it together, since that protects anyone with low literacy from having to disclose it. And always close with teach-back — "what are you going to try first this week?" — rather than "any questions?"

## Practice

1. **Rewrite the shame out.** Take these five statements and rewrite each as a non-judgmental, weight-inclusive alternative: "You need to lose about thirty pounds." "That's a really bad choice for someone with diabetes." "Wow, you look great — have you been losing weight?" "You just have to stop eating fried food." "If you were serious about your health you'd cut out soda." Read the originals aloud first, then your versions, and notice the difference in how they land.

2. **Audit a handout.** Bring or find a real nutrition handout. Score it against all five material criteria above and write one specific fix for every point where it fails. Then rebuild the single most important message from it as a one-page, three-image, three-sentence sheet for an audience with low literacy.

3. **Environment before education.** A client with prediabetes lives in a motel room with a mini-fridge and a microwave, works two shifts, and shops at a corner store. Write the five questions you would ask before teaching anything, then build a realistic one-week plan using only what that environment allows. Name every resource referral you would make and the order you would make them in.

4. **Adapt inside the cuisine.** Choose a dish that matters in a community you expect to serve. Without removing the dish, describe three ways the education could work with it — preparation, what shares the plate, what it is served alongside. Then write the two sentences you would use to introduce this so the client hears respect rather than correction.
