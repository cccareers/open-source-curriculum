## The system as a client experiences it

Health care in the United States is not one system. It is a loose collection of insurers, hospital systems, independent practices, county programs, pharmacies, labs, and community organizations, each with its own paperwork, phone tree, and rules about who it will see. Nobody designed it as a whole. Clients are expected to navigate it as though somebody had.

Picture what that looks like from the client's side. A woman notices a lump. She does not have a regular doctor, so she looks for a clinic that will take her insurance and is open when she is not working. She waits three weeks for an appointment. The provider orders imaging, which happens at a different building, requires a separate appointment, and needs prior authorization from her plan — a step nobody explains to her. The imaging center calls to cancel because the authorization has not come through. She calls the clinic; the clinic says call the plan; the plan says the clinic has to submit it. A bill arrives that says "this is not a bill." Somewhere in there she decides the lump is probably nothing.

That story is not unusual and it is not her fault. The failure points are structural: fragmentation between organizations, no single person accountable for the whole path, communication that assumes fluency in insurance vocabulary, and business hours that assume a job with flexible time off.

Add to that what many clients bring with them. Some have been treated badly in medical settings — dismissed, disbelieved, billed into collections, reported, or handled with visible contempt. Communities have longer memories still: research done without consent, forced sterilization, segregated hospitals. When a client says the hospital cannot be trusted, they are usually reporting evidence, not a misconception. Your job is not to argue them out of it. It is to be a person who does what they said they would do, on the day they said they would do it, until a different kind of evidence starts to accumulate.

## Coverage basics you will be asked about

CHWs are not insurance agents and are not certified enrollment counselors. You are also, very often, the first person a client asks. Knowing the shape of coverage lets you explain what is happening, ask the right question on a call, and hand off cleanly to the right specialist.

**Medicaid (Medi-Cal in California).** A joint federal-state program covering people with low incomes, and in expansion states most adults under a set income threshold. Eligibility and benefits vary by state; California's version is Medi-Cal. Enrollment is open year-round — there is no closed window — and coverage typically carries little or no premium and minimal cost sharing. Members must **renew** periodically, and renewal paperwork is one of the most common reasons people lose coverage while still being eligible. Many members are enrolled in a managed care plan, which means a specific network and a primary care assignment. California has expanded Medi-Cal eligibility to income-eligible residents regardless of immigration status, and has added services that CHWs are directly involved in delivering; because these rules change, verify current specifics with your county office or your employer rather than repeating what you learned in a class.

**Medicare.** The federal program primarily for people 65 and older, and for some people under 65 with long-term disabilities or end-stage renal disease. Its parts are worth memorizing at a plain-language level: Part A covers hospital and inpatient care; Part B covers outpatient care, provider visits, and durable medical equipment; Part C, or Medicare Advantage, is Medicare delivered through a private plan with its own network and often extra benefits; Part D covers prescription drugs. Original Medicare leaves cost sharing that people often cover with a supplemental policy. Enrollment is tied to defined periods, and missing an enrollment window can create lasting penalties — which is exactly the kind of consequence that makes a timely referral to a counselor valuable.

**Dual eligibility.** Some people qualify for both Medicare and Medicaid. They are often the highest-need clients you will serve, and their coverage rules are the most confusing. Learn who handles duals in your organization and route early.

**Marketplace plans.** The health insurance marketplaces created under the Affordable Care Act sell private plans to people without job-based or public coverage. California runs its own marketplace, Covered California. Plans are sold in metal tiers — bronze, silver, gold, platinum — that describe how costs are split between premium and out-of-pocket, not how good the care is. Income-based subsidies reduce premiums, and additional cost-sharing help is available on silver plans for lower incomes. Marketplace enrollment happens during an annual open enrollment period, or during a **special enrollment period** triggered by a qualifying life event such as losing other coverage, moving, marriage, or a birth. Learning to recognize a qualifying event in conversation is a genuinely high-value CHW skill.

**Employer coverage, CHIP, VA, IHS, and the safety net.** Many clients have job-based insurance with its own network and rules. Children's coverage programs cover kids in families that earn too much for Medicaid. Veterans may have VA care; Native and Alaska Native clients may have access to Indian Health Service or tribal health programs. And for people with no coverage at all, the safety net matters: federally qualified health centers and community clinics serve patients regardless of ability to pay, on a **sliding fee scale** based on income; counties often run programs for uninsured residents; hospitals have financial assistance or charity care policies that are frequently unadvertised and must be asked for by name.

## The vocabulary clients get stuck on

Clients rarely say "I don't understand my deductible." They say "they said my insurance didn't cover it" and go home. Translating is navigation work.

- **Premium** — what you pay monthly to keep the plan, whether or not you use care.
- **Deductible** — what you pay yourself before the plan starts paying its share.
- **Copay** — a flat amount per visit or prescription.
- **Coinsurance** — a percentage of the cost rather than a flat amount.
- **Out-of-pocket maximum** — the ceiling; after that, the plan covers the rest for the year.
- **Network** — the providers the plan has contracts with. **In-network** costs less; **out-of-network** can cost dramatically more or not be covered at all.
- **Primary care provider** — the assigned provider, often the required starting point for referrals in a managed care plan.
- **Referral** — permission from the primary care provider to see a specialist.
- **Prior authorization** — the plan's advance approval for a service. This is the step that silently derails more appointments than any other.
- **Formulary** — the plan's list of covered drugs, usually in tiers. If a drug is not on it, ask about an exception or a therapeutic alternative.
- **Explanation of benefits** — a statement showing what was billed and paid. It says "this is not a bill" because it is not one. Many clients pay it anyway.
- **Denial and appeal** — plans deny claims and services, and denials can be appealed. A surprising share of appeals succeed. Clients usually do not know appealing is possible.

## What navigation actually looks like

The competency here is concrete: help clients schedule appointments, complete referral forms, and move through systems — while building their ability to do it themselves next time. Both halves matter. Doing everything for a client solves today and leaves them exactly as stuck tomorrow.

A workable sequence:

1. **Establish where they actually are.** What coverage, if any. Whether they have a primary care provider. What has already been tried, and what happened. Ask for the card and the letters; do not reconstruct from memory.
2. **Name the goal in the client's words.** "Get the knee looked at so I can go back to work" is a goal. "Complete referral" is a task.
3. **Verify coverage and network before scheduling.** A confirmed appointment with an out-of-network provider is a bill waiting to happen.
4. **Make the call together, not instead.** Put it on speaker. Let the client hear the script. Write down the name of the person you spoke to, the date, and the reference number every single time — this record is what wins disputes later.
5. **Do the barrier check before the appointment day.** Transportation, work schedule, childcare, interpretation, documents needed, cost expectation. Solve these in advance, not in the waiting room.
6. **Secure language access.** Clients have the right to an interpreter at no cost in most health settings receiving federal funds. Ask for one on the client's behalf, and coach the client to ask for one. Family members, especially children, should not be interpreting medical information.
7. **Prepare the visit.** Help the client write down three questions and the one thing they most want the provider to understand. This single step changes visits more than almost anything else you do.
8. **Follow up to confirm the connection happened.** A referral is not complete when it is sent. It is complete when the client was seen and the result came back.
9. **Hand off what is not yours.** Enrollment decisions go to certified enrollment counselors or eligibility workers. Legal problems go to legal aid. Clinical questions go to the nurse line or the provider. Naming the right door is navigation; walking through it for them is not.
10. **Teach one step forward each time.** Next visit, the client makes the call while you sit beside them. Then they make it alone and tell you how it went.

Throughout all of it, the scope line from Lesson 2 holds. You can explain what a formulary is; you cannot tell a client which medication to take. You can explain how metal tiers work; you cannot tell a client which plan to buy. You can say "that reading is above the range your doctor gave you, let's call"; you cannot interpret it.

## Practice

1. **Translate the letter.** Find or write a realistic insurance denial letter. Rewrite it as a spoken explanation for a client with limited health literacy: what happened, what it means for them, what happens next, and what you two will do about it. Keep it under 150 words and read it aloud to a partner. Their job is to stop you at any word they cannot immediately picture.

2. **Run the referral walk-through.** In pairs, role-play a client with Medi-Cal managed care who was told to see a cardiologist. The CHW must, out loud: confirm the plan and primary care assignment, ask whether a referral or prior authorization is needed, check that the specialist is in network, identify two barriers to the appointment day, and close by naming one step the client will own next time. Swap roles. Debrief on where the CHW slipped into doing rather than teaching.

3. **Build a pocket guide.** Create a one-page reference for your own service area listing, with names and phone numbers: two clinics that serve uninsured patients on a sliding scale, the county or state coverage enrollment help line, a certified enrollment counselor or navigator you can hand off to, a medical transportation option, and the interpreter access process at the site where you will work. Verify at least three of the phone numbers by calling them.

4. **Spot the qualifying event.** Write down five sentences you could plausibly hear from a client — a job ending, a move, a new baby, aging onto Medicare, turning 26 — and for each, write the one question you would ask next and the person you would hand off to.

5. **Reconstruct a failure.** Think of a time you or someone you know got lost in a health, benefits, or housing system. Write the sequence as a numbered list of steps, marking each point where the system failed. Then mark the earliest point at which a CHW could have changed the outcome, and write what they would have said or done.
