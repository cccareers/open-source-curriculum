## Why a CHW needs to understand policy

Most of what frustrates your clients was decided in a room they were not in. Whether their state expanded Medicaid, how often they must reprove eligibility, which drugs their plan covers, whether their clinic gets paid for your time — all of it is policy. You do not need to be an expert. You need enough of a map to explain to a client why something is happening, and enough to recognize when a problem in front of you is actually a policy problem showing up one client at a time.

"Policy" here means more than laws. It includes federal and state statutes, the regulations agencies write to implement them, the rules a health plan sets, and the internal procedures of your own organization — which are often the ones you can change fastest.

## The Affordable Care Act

The Affordable Care Act, passed in 2010, is the largest change to U.S. health coverage in decades and still shapes the daily conditions of CHW work. Its provisions fall into a few groups.

**Expanding coverage.** The ACA gave states the option to expand Medicaid to nearly all adults below a set income level, with the federal government paying most of the cost. States that expanded — California among them — saw large drops in uninsurance; states that did not left a coverage gap where people earn too much for Medicaid and too little for marketplace subsidies. The law also created the health insurance marketplaces described in Lesson 4, with income-based subsidies, and allowed young adults to stay on a parent's plan until age 26.

**Consumer protections.** Insurers can no longer deny coverage or charge more because of a pre-existing condition, cannot impose lifetime or annual dollar limits on essential benefits, and must cover a defined set of **essential health benefits** including hospitalization, maternity care, mental health and substance use treatment, and prescription drugs. Recommended **preventive services** — many screenings, immunizations, and well visits — must be covered without cost sharing. That last one is directly usable in your work: a client avoiding a screening because of cost may not owe anything for it.

**Prevention and community.** The law created dedicated funding for prevention and public health, and required nonprofit hospitals to conduct periodic **community health needs assessments** and act on them to keep their tax-exempt status. Those assessments are public documents. They are one of the easiest ways to learn what your local health system has already admitted about the community's needs, and they are a legitimate place for CHWs and community members to press for priorities.

**Payment and delivery reform.** The ACA accelerated a shift away from paying purely for volume — every visit, test, and procedure billed separately — toward paying for value: outcomes, quality, and total cost of a population. Accountable care organizations, shared savings arrangements, and readmission penalties all pushed health systems to care whether a patient does well after discharge, not only whether the discharge was billed.

That last shift is the one that created jobs for people like you. When a hospital is financially penalized for readmissions, someone who can visit a patient at home, find out that the medication was never filled, and get it filled becomes worth paying for. Much of the growth in CHW employment, and the movement toward billable CHW services in state Medicaid programs, traces back to this change in incentives.

It is worth naming the risk in that too. When the case for your role is built on cost savings, the role becomes vulnerable to a spreadsheet. CHWs and their advocates generally argue the case on three grounds at once — better outcomes, lower cost, and greater equity — precisely so that the work is not judged on money alone.

## The Triple Aim

In 2008, the Institute for Healthcare Improvement proposed a framework that has organized health system strategy ever since. The **Triple Aim** says a health system should pursue three goals simultaneously:

1. **Improving the patient experience of care**, including quality and satisfaction
2. **Improving the health of populations**
3. **Reducing the per capita cost of care**

The word *simultaneously* is the point. Any one goal is easy to hit by sacrificing the others. Cut costs by denying care. Improve satisfaction by ordering every test a patient asks for. Improve population averages while the worst-off group gets worse. Pursuing all three together forces the harder question of what actually works.

Two extensions are now widely used. The **Quadruple Aim** adds a fourth goal: the well-being of the care team, added after it became clear that burned-out clinicians and staff cannot deliver on the other three. For CHWs this is not abstract — high caseloads, vicarious trauma, and being the person who absorbs a community's distress are real occupational hazards, and later coursework in this pathway returns to them. The **Quintuple Aim** adds health equity as an explicit fifth goal, on the argument that a system can improve every average while leaving disparities untouched, and that equity therefore has to be measured on purpose rather than hoped for.

CHWs sit at the intersection of all of these. Experience of care improves when someone explains things in a client's language and follows up. Population health improves when outreach reaches people the clinic never sees. Costs fall when a problem is solved before it becomes an emergency department visit. Team well-being improves when the person with community context is on the team instead of the clinician guessing. And equity improves when the workforce comes from the communities with the worst outcomes.

## From one client's problem to a pattern

Here is the competency this lesson is built around: identifying systemic or policy-level issues affecting the community and communicating them to supervisors. The skill has two halves — telling a one-off from a pattern, and routing the pattern to the level that can act on it.

**Distinguishing the two.** A single client missing an appointment because her car broke down is an individual problem; you solve it with a bus pass. Fourteen clients from the same housing complex missing appointments in a month is a pattern. Ask yourself:

- Has this happened to more than one client, and is there something they share — plan, clinic, neighborhood, language, immigration status, age?
- Did the problem start at a particular moment? A rule change, a staffing cut, a route change, a new form?
- Does the failure survive individual effort? If a determined, well-supported client still cannot get through, the design is the problem, not the person.
- Would fixing it for this one client leave the next client just as stuck?

If the answer to the last question is yes, you have a systemic issue.

**Documenting it.** A pattern is only actionable if you can show it. Keep a simple, boring log: date, what happened, which client (by ID, not name, in anything that leaves your system), the organization involved, and the outcome. Then when you report, you are not offering an impression; you are offering evidence. "I feel like the pharmacy is refusing people" invites a debate. "Nine clients since March 3, all with the same plan, all told the prescription needed prior authorization, average delay eleven days, three never filled" invites a decision.

**Routing it.** Match the problem to the level that can actually move it:

- **Your own organization** — scheduling, forms, hours, interpreter access, how referrals are tracked. Fastest to change; your supervisor is the door.
- **A partner organization or health plan** — network gaps, denials, phone systems. Usually requires your supervisor or a manager to raise it through an established contact.
- **County or state agency** — eligibility processing, renewal notices, transportation benefits. Often has a formal complaint or grievance channel, and community advisory boards.
- **Legislative or regulatory** — statute and rule changes. Slow, and reached through coalitions, professional associations, public comment, and testimony rather than a single report.

**Communicating it to a supervisor.** Keep it short and structured: what you observed, how many times and over what period, who is affected, what you think the cause is, what you recommend, and what you need from them. Bring one representative story to make it real and the count to make it credible. Ask explicitly what happens next and by when, and write down the answer.

Two cautions. First, follow your organization's rules about who speaks publicly on its behalf; escalating internally is your job, going to the press generally is not, and confusing the two can cost you the position from which you are useful. Second, protect client confidentiality absolutely when you aggregate — patterns are made of de-identified counts, not of stories that identify someone.

Advocacy done this way is not a heroic act. It is a habit: notice, log, aggregate, report, follow up. Done consistently, it is how the conditions your clients live in actually change.

## Practice

1. **Read your local evidence.** Find the most recent community health needs assessment published by a nonprofit hospital serving your area, or your county's community health assessment. Skim it and write a one-page response: the three top priorities named, whether they match what you observe, and one priority you believe is missing.

2. **Sort ten problems.** For each, decide whether it is an individual problem, a pattern, or not yet determinable, and name the next step you would take: a client's car broke down; four clients in one month lost Medi-Cal at renewal despite still qualifying; a client cannot read the appointment letter; the clinic's only Hmong interpreter left and was not replaced; a client's specialist referral was denied; six clients report the same specialist's office hangs up on them; a client cannot afford insulin; the marketplace subsidy amount changed for many of your clients at once.

3. **Keep a pattern log for a week.** Using a simple table with date, issue, organization involved, client identifier, and outcome, log every barrier you encounter in your field placement, volunteering, or your own life for one week. At the end, look for anything appearing three or more times.

4. **Write the issue brief.** Take the strongest pattern from exercise 2 or 3 and write a one-page memo to a supervisor: observation, scope and time period, who is affected, likely cause, recommendation, and the specific ask. Maximum one page. No client names.

5. **Trace policy to a person.** Pick one ACA provision described above — Medicaid expansion, preventive services without cost sharing, dependent coverage to 26, or pre-existing condition protections. Write a short scenario showing one specific client's day being different because of it, and a second scenario showing what that same day would look like without it.

6. **Argue the aims.** In a small group, take a real proposal — a clinic ending evening hours, a plan narrowing its network, a program cutting CHW caseloads to raise the number of clients seen — and assess it against each of the five aims in turn. Identify where a gain on one aim is being paid for by a loss on another, and say who bears the loss.
