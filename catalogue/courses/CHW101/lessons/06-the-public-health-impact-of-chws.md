## Why the evidence question keeps coming up

Every year, someone with a budget asks whether CHW programs are worth funding. The question is not hostile. It is the same question asked of every service competing for the same dollars, and it will be asked about the program that employs you. Being able to answer it — accurately, without overclaiming — is part of professional practice.

The honest short answer is that a substantial body of research finds CHW programs improve health outcomes and reduce avoidable, expensive care for the populations that need it most, and that the strength of the finding varies a great deal by how the program is designed, supervised, and staffed. CHWs are not a magic input. A well-supported CHW with a reasonable caseload, real training, and a defined role produces very different results from a poorly supported one handed an impossible caseload and no supervision.

## Outputs and outcomes

This is the distinction that makes or breaks a conversation about impact, and it is the one most often blurred.

An **output** is something the program did. It counts activity. Outputs are within your control and you generate them by working:

- 212 client encounters completed
- 18 health education classes held
- 340 blood pressure screenings performed
- 96 referrals made
- 74 clients enrolled in coverage

An **outcome** is something that changed for the people served. Outcomes are what the program is for, and they are only partly within your control:

- The share of enrolled clients with controlled blood pressure rose from 41 percent to 58 percent
- Emergency department visits among enrolled clients fell by a quarter over twelve months
- 62 clients who had no usual source of care completed a first primary care visit
- 31 clients moved from unstable to stable housing
- Clients' confidence in managing their own condition rose on a validated scale

The distinction matters because outputs can look excellent while nothing improves. A program can hold forty classes to empty rooms, make hundreds of referrals nobody completes, and screen thousands of people who never get follow-up. Every one of those is a number that goes up. None of them is health.

Outcomes are usually sorted by time horizon:

- **Short-term outcomes** — changes in knowledge, confidence, attitude, or intention. "The client can now describe what her medication does and why the timing matters."
- **Intermediate outcomes** — changes in behavior or access. "The client refills the prescription on time and attends her appointments."
- **Long-term outcomes** — changes in health status or the conditions of life. "Her blood pressure is in range." "Her housing is stable."

Beyond those sits **impact**: population-level change, such as a narrowed disparity in hypertension control between neighborhoods. Impact is rarely attributable to a single program, and claiming it usually costs you credibility.

A **logic model** is the standard tool that lines all of this up: inputs (staff, funding, training) lead to activities (outreach, education, navigation), which produce outputs (counts of those activities), which lead to outcomes (short, intermediate, long), which contribute to impact. If your program has one, read it. It tells you exactly why you are asked to record what you record.

## What the research shows

A few points about the evidence, stated at the level of confidence the evidence supports.

**Randomized trials exist, and this matters.** CHW research is no longer only satisfied clients and program reports. Standardized, well-defined CHW interventions have been tested in randomized controlled trials — the Penn Center for Community Health Workers' IMPaCT model is the most frequently cited U.S. example — with published findings of improvements in areas such as chronic disease control, quality of primary care, and reduced hospital use, along with reported returns on investment for the payer. Findings differ across sites and populations, and you should read the specific study rather than repeat a number from a slide, but the existence of trial-level evidence changes the conversation from "we believe this helps" to "this has been tested."

**The strongest and most consistent results cluster in a few places.** Chronic disease management, particularly hypertension and diabetes; maternal and child health, including prenatal engagement and birth outcomes; asthma, especially where home visits address environmental triggers; cancer screening completion; and reducing avoidable emergency department use and readmission among high-need patients.

**Cost findings are real but conditional.** Savings come mainly from care that did not have to happen — the admission avoided, the emergency visit prevented. They are largest in populations with high baseline utilization, and they take time to appear. A program serving a generally healthy population will not produce dramatic savings, and should not be evaluated as though it should.

**Equity is where CHW programs are distinctive.** CHW interventions reach people other approaches do not: people disconnected from care, people who mistrust institutions for good reason, people whose language or immigration status keeps them at the margins of every system. A program can improve the average without touching the gap; CHW programs often move the gap. That is an argument worth making explicitly, because it will not show up in a cost-per-member report.

**Design determines results.** Across reviews, the programs that work share features: a clearly defined role, adequate and ongoing training, manageable caseloads, real supervision from someone who understands the role, integration into a team rather than isolation at the edge of one, and sustainable funding rather than a two-year grant. When a program does not work, the failure is far more often in these conditions than in the CHWs.

## Your data is the evidence

Here is where this becomes daily practice rather than background reading. The outcomes above are built out of records that individual CHWs enter, one encounter at a time. If your documentation is late, thin, or inconsistent, the program cannot demonstrate what it did — and a program that cannot demonstrate what it did loses funding regardless of how well it served people.

What your data actually feeds:

- **Funding and contracts.** Grants and payer contracts require reported measures. In some arrangements, reimbursement depends directly on documented, billable encounters.
- **Quality improvement.** Patterns in the data show which populations are being missed, which referrals never complete, where the program is leaking.
- **Equity monitoring.** Only stratified data — by language, race and ethnicity, neighborhood, coverage type — can show whether the program is closing a gap or widening it. This requires the demographic fields many people skip.
- **The case for the workforce.** Every study cited above was assembled from records CHWs entered.
- **Care continuity.** The next person to work with this client reads what you wrote.

Practices that make data trustworthy:

- **Enter it the same day.** Recall degrades fast, and a week-old note is a guess.
- **Record what happened, not a summary of your effort.** "Called client, no answer, left voicemail, will retry Thursday" is data. "Followed up with client" is not.
- **Use the categories consistently.** If you code housing help as "housing" and a colleague codes it as "other," the program's housing numbers are fiction.
- **Do not leave demographic fields blank** when the client has answered — those blanks are exactly what makes equity analysis impossible.
- **Flag data quality problems rather than working around them.** A field that does not fit the work, a duplicate record, a form that forces an inaccurate answer — these are worth raising. Silently entering something wrong to make a form accept it corrupts the record for everyone.
- **Never inflate.** A padded encounter count is fraud in a billable program and self-sabotage in any program, because next year's target will be set from it.
- **Keep confidentiality intact.** Aggregate reporting uses counts and de-identified data. Stories used publicly require explicit, informed permission.

## Talking about impact honestly

You will be asked, in interviews, community meetings, and funder visits, what your program accomplishes. Three habits keep the answer credible:

Lead with an outcome, support with an output. "Fifty-eight percent of our enrolled clients now have their blood pressure in range, up from forty-one, and that came out of about two hundred home visits and monthly follow-up calls" is stronger than either half alone.

Attribute carefully. Say "clients enrolled in the program had fewer emergency visits" rather than "the program reduced emergency visits" unless the study design supports the causal claim. People who know the difference will notice, and your credibility with them is worth more than the stronger sentence.

Name the limits. Programs have populations they did not reach and outcomes they did not move. Saying so is what makes the rest believable — and it is usually the fastest route to fixing it.

## Practice

1. **Classify twenty items.** Mark each as output, short-term outcome, intermediate outcome, long-term outcome, or impact: 44 home visits completed; a client can name the three warning signs of a stroke; county-wide infant mortality declined; 12 clients enrolled in Medi-Cal; a client began taking her medication daily; 6 health fairs staffed; a client's A1C fell from 9.4 to 7.8; 200 flyers distributed; a client scheduled her own specialist appointment without help; 89 percent of surveyed clients said they felt respected; the disparity in colorectal screening between two neighborhoods narrowed; 15 referrals made to a food bank; a client reports feeling less alone; 3 CHWs completed training; a client's housing became stable; 30 clients attended a diabetes class; a client stopped using the emergency department for routine care; 500 blood pressure screenings; a client can explain her diagnosis in her own words; hospital readmissions among enrolled clients fell 22 percent.

2. **Build a logic model.** For a hypothetical CHW program addressing uncontrolled hypertension in one neighborhood, fill in all five columns: inputs, activities, outputs, outcomes (split short, intermediate, long), and impact. Then draw an arrow from each output to the outcome it is supposed to produce. Any output with no arrow is a candidate for elimination — say why you would keep or cut it.

3. **Audit an encounter note.** Write a deliberately bad encounter note — vague, late, missing fields. Then rewrite it well. In a short paragraph, list every downstream use of that record that the bad version would have damaged.

4. **Interrogate a claim.** Find a real CHW program's public report, fact sheet, or web page. Identify which of its numbers are outputs and which are outcomes, note anything presented as an outcome that is really an output, and write one question you would ask the program before repeating its headline number.

5. **Make the case in ninety seconds.** Write and deliver aloud a short answer to "why should we keep funding this CHW program?" that leads with an outcome, supports it with outputs, attributes carefully, and names one honest limitation. Practice it with a partner whose job is to ask "how do you know?" after every sentence.

## Check your understanding

1. Output or outcome: "74 clients enrolled in coverage." *Output — it counts an activity. "Clients who were uninsured a year ago now have continuous coverage" would be an outcome.*
2. Output or outcome: "A client can explain her diagnosis in her own words." *Short-term outcome — a change in knowledge.*
3. Your program's enrolled clients had fewer emergency visits this year than last year. Why should you say "clients enrolled in the program had fewer emergency visits" instead of "the program reduced emergency visits"? *Without a comparison group or trial design, you cannot rule out other causes; careful attribution protects your credibility.*
