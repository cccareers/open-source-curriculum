## What a community health worker is

A community health worker is a frontline public health worker who is a trusted member of the community being served, or who has an unusually close understanding of it. That trust is not a nice extra on top of the job — it is the qualification. A nurse earns authority through a license. A CHW earns it through relationship: shared language, shared neighborhood, shared experience of what it is like to sit in a waiting room and not understand what anyone is saying to you.

Because trust is the credential, the work looks different from other health roles. A CHW is the person who can get a phone call returned when the clinic's automated reminder system has failed six times. The person who notices that the reason a client keeps missing appointments is not "noncompliance" but a bus route that was cut. The person who can say "I know, I have been through that office too" and have it land as true.

The field has settled on a shared description of what CHWs actually do, developed through the Community Health Worker Core Consensus Project — usually shortened to the C3 Project — which worked with CHWs themselves to name the roles and skills the job requires. The roles cluster into recognizable buckets:

- **Bridging** between community members and health and social services
- **Outreach and engagement**, meeting people where they live, work, worship, and gather
- **Health education and information sharing**, in plain language and in the client's own language
- **Care coordination and system navigation**, including follow-up that confirms the connection actually happened
- **Advocacy** for individual clients and for the community as a whole
- **Direct service and basic support**, such as help with forms, benefits applications, or a blood pressure check within an approved protocol
- **Capacity building**, teaching people the skills to do things for themselves next time
- **Assessment**, both informal and structured, of individual and community needs
- **Participation in evaluation and research**, contributing the data that shows what a program is doing

The skills underneath those roles are the ones you will spend the rest of this pathway practicing: communication, interpersonal relationship building, service coordination, capacity building, advocacy, education, individual and community assessment, professional conduct, evaluation, and knowledge of the specific health issues your community faces.

## The line you do not cross

CHWs educate, support, navigate, and advocate. CHWs do not diagnose, do not prescribe, and do not give medical advice. This is not a technicality — it is the boundary that keeps clients safe and keeps your role credible with the clinical team.

In practice the line shows up in wording. "Your blood pressure reading today is 158 over 96, which is higher than the number your doctor said to watch for. Let's call the nurse line together" is CHW work. "That is high, you should double your blood pressure pill" is prescribing, and it is outside your scope no matter how confident you feel. "Here is what the pharmacist told me about how to ask for a lower-cost version" is education. "You probably don't need that medication anyway" is medical advice.

Some CHWs do take vital signs, run point-of-care screenings, or support medication adherence — always under a written protocol, always with clinical supervision, and always within what their employer and state allow. When you are unsure whether a task is yours, the correct move is not to guess. It is to ask your supervisor and to document the answer.

## Where the role came from

The work is old. Long before public health departments existed, communities designated people to carry health knowledge — midwives, herbalists, elders, mutual aid societies. The modern professional role grew out of several traditions that converged.

**The promotores de salud tradition.** Across Latin America, community members trained as *promotores* or *promotoras de salud* carried health education, maternal and child health support, and disease prevention into neighborhoods that clinics did not reach. The model traveled with migration into the United States, particularly in the Southwest and in agricultural regions, and remains one of the strongest roots of CHW practice today. Much of what the field now calls "cultural humility" and "meeting people where they are" was standard promotora practice decades before it had a professional name.

**Community health aides and representatives.** In Alaska, a formal Community Health Aide Program was built in the 1960s to provide care in villages hundreds of miles from a hospital, training local residents to deliver services under remote clinical supervision. Tribal Community Health Representative programs grew across Indian Country in the same era. Both established something important: that a trained community member could carry real responsibility inside a health system, not merely hand out pamphlets.

**International primary care movements.** Programs in China, India, and across Africa demonstrated at national scale that trained lay workers could deliver immunization, maternal health, and disease control in places with very few physicians. The 1978 Alma-Ata declaration on primary health care gave the approach global legitimacy.

**U.S. neighborhood health centers and the civil rights era.** The 1960s neighborhood health center movement, tied to the War on Poverty and the civil rights movement, hired local residents into health roles on purpose. Community control was the point: the people served should have a hand in how services were designed and delivered. Today's federally qualified health centers descend from that movement, and so does the CHW expectation of speaking up when the system is not working.

## From informal work to a recognized profession

For most of that history, CHWs were funded by short grants, given inconsistent titles, and left out of the payment systems that fund the rest of health care. That has been shifting.

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics established a distinct occupational classification for community health workers (Standard Occupational Classification 21-1094, added in the 2010 revision), which mattered more than it sounds — it made the workforce countable. Professional associations, state CHW networks, and CHW-led advocacy pushed for training standards written with CHWs rather than about them.

In California, the movement toward recognition has been visible. State-approved training and certificate pathways have expanded through community colleges and community-based organizations, and in 2022 the state added community health worker, promotor, and representative services as a covered Medi-Cal benefit — meaning that, under defined conditions, the state's Medicaid program will pay for CHW services rather than requiring them to be grant-funded. Related initiatives have built CHW roles into enhanced care management and community support programs. The details of who can bill, what supervision is required, and what training qualifies change as the rules are updated, so treat anything specific you hear about billing as something to verify with current state guidance and with your employer.

Recognition brings a tension worth naming now. The value of the role comes from being of the community; formalization brings certificates, documentation, productivity targets, and supervision structures. Held well, formalization protects CHWs — stable pay, career ladders, a seat on the team. Held badly, it turns CHWs into low-paid appointment schedulers and strips out the relational work that made the role effective. Part of your professional job is to keep articulating what the role actually is.

## Where CHWs sit on a team and in a community

You will find CHWs in clinics and federally qualified health centers, hospitals and emergency departments, managed care plans, public health departments, community-based organizations, housing and homeless services, schools, jails and reentry programs, home visiting programs, and faith-based organizations. Titles vary widely: promotora de salud, community health representative, patient navigator, outreach worker, peer support specialist, health coach, community health advocate, care guide.

On a care team, think of the roles as different vantage points on the same person:

- The **physician or nurse practitioner** diagnoses and prescribes.
- The **nurse** manages clinical care, education about the condition, and triage.
- The **social worker** handles psychosocial assessment, crisis intervention, and services requiring clinical licensure.
- The **case manager or care coordinator** manages the plan and the paperwork across providers.
- The **CHW** holds the relationship and the context — what the home looks like, what the client actually believes about the diagnosis, which barrier is really in the way, and what the client wants.

CHWs are usually the only member of the team who regularly sees the client outside a clinical setting. That is why the team needs you, and why what you document and report back matters so much. Information travels in both directions: clinical guidance out to the client in language that lands, and lived reality back to the team so the plan has a chance of working.

In community settings, the positioning is different again. There you are often not attached to a single client at all but to a place — a housing complex, a swap meet, a congregation, a corner. That work builds the trust that individual encounters later draw on, and it is where mistrust of systems shows up most plainly. When someone tells you they do not trust the hospital, the professional response is not to defend the hospital. It is to take the mistrust seriously as information, because it was usually earned.

## Practice

1. **Map your own credibility.** Write a half-page inventory of the communities you have real standing in — language, neighborhood, faith community, workplace, lived experience with a condition or a system. For each, note one thing you understand that an outsider would miss. Bring this to your instructor-led session; it is the beginning of your professional self-awareness, not a personal essay.

2. **Sort the scope.** For each of the following, decide whether it is inside CHW scope, outside it, or "inside only with a written protocol and supervision," and write one sentence defending each answer: taking a blood pressure reading; telling a client which of two medications to take; explaining what a prior authorization is; helping a client write down questions for their doctor; interpreting a lab result; driving a client to an appointment; telling a client their symptoms sound like diabetes; helping a client apply for food benefits.

3. **Rewrite the out-of-scope answers.** Take the three items you marked "outside scope" and rewrite each as something a CHW could legitimately say or do in that same moment. The skill being practiced is redirecting without abandoning the client.

4. **Trace a lineage.** Pick one of the historical roots described above — promotores, community health aides and representatives, international primary care, or the neighborhood health center movement. In one paragraph, name one specific practice from that tradition that you can see in the CHW job descriptions posted in your area today. Find and skim one real job posting to ground it.

5. **Interview a role.** If you have access to a working CHW, promotora, or patient navigator, ask three questions: what does your day actually look like, what part of the job is not in the job description, and where does your supervisor draw the scope line. If you do not, prepare the questions and bring them to the session for a group discussion.
