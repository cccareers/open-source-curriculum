## Equality, equity, and why the difference matters at work

Equality means everyone gets the same thing. Equity means everyone gets what they need to reach the same outcome. Both sound fair; only one of them works when people are starting from different places.

A clinic that mails the same appointment reminder letter to every patient is being equal. If a third of those patients read at a level the letter is not written for, another third moved in the last year, and another share speak a language the letter is not printed in, the clinic will end up with very different show rates across groups while insisting it treated everyone the same. An equity approach asks a different question: what would each of these groups need in order to actually make the appointment? Maybe that is a phone call in Spanish, a text instead of a letter, or a bus pass.

Two more terms you will hear constantly:

- A **health disparity** is a measurable difference in health between groups. Group A has more uncontrolled diabetes than Group B. It is a description.
- A **health inequity** is a disparity that is avoidable, unfair, and rooted in how systems and resources have been distributed. It is a judgment about cause.

Not every difference is an inequity — some conditions really are more common at older ages, for example. But when you see a difference that tracks with income, race, immigration status, language, disability, or zip code, and there is no biological reason for it, you are almost certainly looking at an inequity. **Health equity**, then, is the state in which everyone has a fair and just opportunity to be as healthy as possible, which requires removing obstacles like poverty, discrimination, and their consequences.

This matters at work because the language you use shapes what gets fixed. If a team describes a patient as "noncompliant," the implied fix is to lecture the patient harder. If the same situation is described as "the medication costs $80 a month and she is choosing between that and her electric bill," the fix is a different medication, a patient assistance program, or a utility assistance referral. CHWs are frequently the only person on the team with the information that turns the first framing into the second.

## Social determinants of health

Clinical care matters, but it is not what mostly determines whether people are healthy. The **social determinants of health** are the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work, and age — the circumstances that shape health long before anyone walks into a clinic. A widely used grouping, from the federal Healthy People 2030 framework, sorts them into five areas:

**Economic stability.** Income, employment, job quality, poverty, debt, food security, housing costs. A person working two part-time jobs with unpredictable scheduling cannot reliably attend a Tuesday-morning appointment, no matter how motivated they are.

**Education access and quality.** Early childhood education, high school completion, language and literacy, health literacy. Health literacy in particular is not a measure of intelligence; it is a measure of how well a system explained itself.

**Health care access and quality.** Insurance coverage, having a usual source of care, distance to providers, provider availability, language access, and whether care is delivered with respect. Access is not only whether a clinic exists but whether a person can get to it, afford it, understand it, and be treated decently in it.

**Neighborhood and built environment.** Housing quality and stability, transportation, air and water quality, food retail, parks and safe places to walk, neighborhood violence, exposure to environmental hazards. Where someone lives is one of the strongest predictors of how long they live — life expectancy can differ by a decade or more between neighborhoods a short drive apart in the same city.

**Social and community context.** Social connection and isolation, family and caregiving responsibilities, civic participation, discrimination, incarceration, and immigration-related fear.

Running underneath all five are structural forces: racism, poverty, and policy decisions about where highways were built, which neighborhoods lenders would finance, which jobs came with insurance, and which did not. These are not distant history — the maps drawn generations ago still line up with today's asthma rates and life expectancy in many cities.

## Upstream and downstream

There is a parable public health people tell. You are standing by a river and a person floats past, drowning. You pull them out. Another comes, and another. You get very good at rescue, and you are exhausted. Eventually someone walks upstream to find out who is throwing people in.

**Downstream** work treats what has already happened: the emergency room visit, the amputation, the eviction. **Midstream** work changes the immediate conditions: getting a client a housing voucher, a food benefit, a working inhaler. **Upstream** work changes the conditions that produced the problem for everyone: a housing policy, a bus route, an air-quality regulation.

CHWs work at all three levels, often in the same day. You will pull people out of the river — that is real, necessary, and not a lesser form of the work. You will also be one of the few people positioned to notice that eleven of your clients this month lost coverage because of the same paperwork problem. Noticing that pattern, and routing it upward, is upstream work. Lesson 5 returns to how to do that formally.

## Identifying social determinants in a real encounter

The competency is not "can list five categories." It is: when a client presents a problem, can you recognize the social determinants sitting behind it, and can you do that without turning the client into a case file of deficits.

A useful habit is to ask "what would have to be true for this to be easy for them?" and then check what is missing.

Consider a client whose diabetes is not controlled. Downstream, the record says the A1C is high. Upstream of that, any of the following may be operating: no refrigerator for insulin; a shut-off notice on the electricity; a food environment with no fresh produce within two bus transfers; night-shift work that makes consistent meal timing impossible; a copay that forced rationing; a diagnosis explained only in English; a fear that seeking help will surface immigration status; a family caregiving load that leaves no room for a two-hour clinic visit.

None of that is visible in a lab value. Most of it becomes visible in a conversation with someone the client trusts.

A few practices that make identification accurate rather than assumed:

- **Ask, don't infer.** Screening questions exist for exactly this reason. Ask them the same way for everyone, and ask them because you can act on the answer, not to fill in a form.
- **Ask permission and explain why.** "I'd like to ask a few questions about things outside of health — food, housing, getting around — because they're often what's actually in the way, and we may have help available. You can skip anything."
- **Do not screen for what you cannot act on.** Asking a client about food insecurity and then offering nothing does damage. Know your referral options before you ask.
- **Watch for the disclosure risk.** Questions about immigration, substance use, or intimate partner violence carry real consequences. Know your documentation and confidentiality rules before you invite those answers, and know your mandated reporting obligations.
- **Document plainly and factually.** "Client reports no reliable transportation to the clinic; nearest bus stop is three-quarters of a mile" is useful to the team. "Client is unmotivated" is not.

## Community assets, not just community deficits

Here is where a lot of well-meaning health programs go wrong. They arrive with a needs assessment, produce a long list of everything a neighborhood lacks, and hand it to funders. The community reads that list and recognizes itself as a problem to be solved. Trust does not survive that for long.

Every community has **assets** — the resources, relationships, and capacities already present and working. Asset-based practice means you map what is there alongside what is missing. Categories worth looking for:

- **Individuals**: elders, respected neighbors, barbers and stylists, coaches, promotoras, people who already act as informal connectors.
- **Associations**: churches, mosques, and temples; sports leagues; tenant associations; mutual aid groups; parent groups; neighborhood watch; burial societies; rotating savings clubs.
- **Institutions**: schools, libraries, clinics and FQHCs, community colleges, fire stations, parks, WIC offices, county programs.
- **Physical assets**: a park with shade, a community garden, a corner store willing to stock produce, a church basement large enough for a class, an accessible bus line.
- **Economic assets**: local businesses, employers who will host a screening event, food vendors, credit unions.
- **Cultural assets**: languages spoken, food traditions, healing traditions, festivals, radio stations, informal networks that move information faster than any flyer.

Assets are not decoration on a needs list. They are what your interventions run on. A blood pressure screening event held in a church basement by someone the congregation knows will reach more people than a perfectly designed campaign from a health plan nobody has heard of. If you already know which barbershop the men in a neighborhood are in on Saturday morning, you have a distribution channel no marketing budget can buy.

Holding both truths at once is the professional skill: this community faces real, unjust barriers, **and** this community has real strength, knowledge, and organization. Describe it only as needy and you insult the people you serve while missing your best tools. Describe it only as resilient and you let the systems producing the harm off the hook.

## Practice

1. **Sort a case.** Take this scenario: a 58-year-old client with congestive heart failure has been readmitted to the hospital twice in four months. He lives alone in a second-floor unit with no elevator, uses a walker, gets $1,100 a month, and has not filled his diuretic in six weeks. List every social determinant you can identify, grouped under the five domains. Then mark each one downstream, midstream, or upstream, and note which ones a CHW could realistically affect.

2. **Rewrite deficit language.** Rewrite each of these chart notes into a factual, non-judgmental note a CHW could defend in a team meeting: "Patient is noncompliant with diet." / "Family is disengaged." / "Client refuses to use the bus." / "Poor health literacy." For each, name the question you would need to ask to write the better version honestly.

3. **Build an asset map.** Choose one real neighborhood you know — ideally the one you expect to serve. Draw or list at least fifteen assets across the six categories above, with names, not just types. Then mark: which three would you approach first to reach older adults, and why does each of those have standing?

4. **Pair the map with a gap list.** For the same neighborhood, list five genuine gaps. Then, for each gap, name at least one asset from your map that could be part of addressing it. The exercise fails if any gap has no asset paired to it — keep looking until each one does.

5. **Write your screening script.** Draft the exact words you would use to introduce a social needs screening to a client who is guarded and in a hurry: the permission ask, the reason, the opt-out, and one sentence about what you can actually do with the answers. Keep it under 60 seconds when read aloud, and practice it out loud with a partner.
