## You cannot write everything at once

By now your gap log probably has 20, 30, or more entries. If your team tries to write a procedure for every one of them, you will finish none. Procedure writing competes with caseloads, monitoring visits, and enrollment targets. The skill in this lesson is choosing the few procedures that buy the most protection for the least effort, and writing those first.

## Four questions for every gap

Score each gap in your log on four factors. Use a simple 1 to 3 scale so the team can score quickly and argue about the right things.

| Factor | Ask | 1 | 3 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Compliance risk** | If this goes wrong, could it produce a monitoring finding, a data validation failure, or a disallowed cost? | Unlikely | Likely |
| **Customer impact** | Does failure delay, lose, or harm a customer? | Minor inconvenience | Customer loses a service or drops out |
| **Knowledge concentration** | How many people know how to do this? | Most of the team | One person |
| **Frequency** | How often does this step happen? | A few times a year | Daily or weekly |

Add the four scores for a total between 4 and 12. Then look at **effort**: how hard is the procedure to write? A one-page checklist is low effort; a procedure that first needs a policy decision from the board is high effort.

Sort by total score, then pull forward any high-scoring item that is also low effort. Those are your first procedures.

## The single-person test

One factor deserves extra weight: knowledge concentration. A step that only one person can do is a risk even when everything is going well, because it fails completely on the day that person is gone. If a gap scores 3 on knowledge concentration *and* 3 on compliance risk, write it first, whatever else is on the list.

Ask the room: "If [role holder] won the lottery and left tomorrow, which steps would stop?" The answers are usually your top three.

## Worked example

The fictional Valley Regional AJCC team scored five gaps from their log:

| Gap | Compliance | Customer | Concentration | Frequency | Total | Effort |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| G-07 Eligibility to case manager assignment | 2 | 3 | 3 | 3 | 11 | Low |
| G-12 CalJOBS correction for wrong exit date | 3 | 1 | 3 | 1 | 8 | Low |
| G-03 Training start date in four places | 3 | 1 | 1 | 2 | 7 | High |
| G-15 Follow-up call tracking in personal spreadsheet | 3 | 2 | 3 | 2 | 10 | Medium |
| G-09 Greeter routing for walk-ins | 1 | 2 | 1 | 3 | 7 | Low |

**Their first three procedures:**

1. **G-07** — highest score, low effort. A half-page procedure: who assigns, within what timeline (set by the program manager), and how the technician confirms the handoff.
2. **G-15** — high score; one person holds it, and follow-up affects performance reporting. They will replace the personal spreadsheet with a shared tracker and a written schedule.
3. **G-12** — moderate score, but only one person knows the fix and errors affect reported data. A short step-by-step with screenshots.

G-03 scored well but needs decisions about which system is the record and who owns updates. The team logged it as a **policy question for management**, not a procedure to write this month.

## What "written down" means

A procedure does not need to be long. For most AJCC steps, the right format is one page that a new person could follow on their first day:

```text
Procedure: Assigning a new DW participant to a case manager
Trigger: Eligibility determination completed in CalJOBS
Owner: Program supervisor (backup: senior case manager)
Steps:
  1. Technician sends the assignment request using [team inbox / form]
  2. Supervisor assigns a case manager within [timeline set by program manager]
  3. Case manager contacts the customer within [timeline]
  4. Technician confirms assignment in [tracker]
Governing rule: [your board's service-flow policy, section]
Last reviewed: [date]   Map step: 7
```

Every procedure should name its map step, its owner, its backup, and the rule behind it. Leave a bracketed placeholder where the local policy sets a value and fill it in from your board's policy, not from memory.

## Watch for policy gaps disguised as procedure gaps

Sometimes the reason a step is undocumented is that nobody ever decided how it should work. You cannot write a procedure for a decision that has not been made. When the team keeps arguing about *what* the step should be, stop, log it as a policy question, and send it to the person who can decide. Then come back to it.

**Discussion prompt:** Which item on your list would your director be most surprised to learn is held by one person?

## Practice

1. Take your gap log from Lesson 3 and score every entry on the four factors.
2. Mark each entry's effort as low, medium, or high.
3. Apply the single-person test and flag any gap that scores 3 on both knowledge concentration and compliance risk.
4. Choose your top three procedures to write and write one sentence for each explaining why it made the cut.
5. Separate any gaps that are really policy questions, and name who in your organization should answer each one.
6. Draft the first procedure using the one-page template.
