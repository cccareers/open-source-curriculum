## From knowing the roles to evidencing them

You learned the Community Health Worker roles earlier in this program as a framework. In your internship they stop being a framework and become a description of your afternoon. A phone call to a clinic scheduler, a walk to a food pantry with a client who has never been, fifteen minutes spent listening to someone who is frightened about a diagnosis — each of these is a role in action, and each one is evidence you can use.

Most interns finish their hours with far more evidence than they can describe. The work happened; the record of it did not. Three months later, in an interview, they say "I helped clients with resources," which tells a hiring manager almost nothing. This lesson is about closing that gap while it is still open: reading your own daily activities for the roles they demonstrate, and keeping a log that lets you speak about your practice precisely and without ever exposing a client.

## The roles, in the language of your placement

The Community Health Worker Core Consensus (C3) Project describes ten roles that define the occupation nationally. Employers write job descriptions from them, and supervisors evaluate against them. Read each one below alongside what it actually looks like where you are placed.

- **Cultural mediation** between individuals, communities, and health and social service systems. In practice: explaining to a nurse why a client keeps missing morning appointments, and explaining to the client what the clinic's no-show policy actually means.
- **Providing culturally appropriate health education and information.** In practice: teaching a client to read a medication label in the language they think in.
- **Care coordination, case management, and system navigation.** In practice: the referral you made, the form you helped complete, the follow-up call you placed a week later.
- **Providing coaching and social support.** In practice: sitting with someone as they decide, without deciding for them.
- **Advocating for individuals and communities.** In practice: calling a benefits office back when a client was told "no" by someone who applied the wrong rule.
- **Building individual and community capacity.** In practice: showing a client how to make the next call themselves — and then letting them make it.
- **Providing direct service.** In practice: a blood pressure reading, a food box, a bus pass, a warm handoff to a case manager.
- **Implementing individual and community assessments.** In practice: the intake screen, the social needs questionnaire, the informal read you did while walking someone to their car.
- **Conducting outreach.** In practice: tabling at the community center, knocking on doors on a street the clinic never reaches.
- **Participating in evaluation and research.** In practice: the encounter data you enter, the pilot survey your program asked you to administer.

You will not demonstrate all ten equally, and you are not supposed to. A placement in a clinic care team may generate heavy coordination and direct service evidence and almost no outreach. A placement in a community-based organization may invert that. What matters is that you can name which roles your placement exercises, name the gaps honestly, and ask your field mentor for one or two experiences that fill them.

## Reading an activity for the role it evidences

Take a single activity and ask three questions of it.

**What did I actually do?** Not what the program does — what you did, in verbs. "Called the pharmacy," "translated the discharge instructions," "waited with the client."

**Which role does that action belong to?** Often more than one. Walking a client through the online benefits portal is capacity building if they drive and you coach, and direct service if you drive and they watch. The difference is not cosmetic; it is the difference between two roles, and only one of them leaves the client more able than before.

**What changed because I did it?** The appointment got scheduled. The client made the next call alone. The denial got reversed. If nothing changed yet, say that too — "referral placed, connection not yet confirmed" is honest and it tells you what your next task is.

That third question is where interns most often shortchange themselves, because they stop at the action. The action is the input; the change is the evidence.

## Three roles worth watching closely this term

Three of these roles account for most of the supervised competency you will be signed off on, and each has a common failure mode.

**Referrals are not complete when they are made.** A referral is complete when the client connected. Anything short of that is a referral in flight. Set yourself a follow-up date at the moment you make it, and when you follow up, ask what actually happened rather than whether they went — "Were you able to get through?" surfaces the disconnected phone number that "Did you call?" does not. When the connection failed, treat the barrier as the real work: no transportation, a form that required a document the client does not have, an intake line that only answers between nine and eleven. Fixing that barrier, or routing around it, is the competency. Closing the loop in your notes so the next person knows the state of the referral is part of it.

**Goals belong to the client.** Supporting someone in setting and tracking their own health goal means eliciting what they want, not assigning what you would want. It is genuinely harder than it sounds, because you often can see a better goal than the one they name. The discipline is to ask what they would like to be different, to reflect it back in their words, to help them make it small enough to be real, and to build a tracking method they will actually use — a mark on a calendar, a note in their phone, a check-in when you call. Then you review it with them and let them revise it. A goal you wrote and they agreed to is your goal wearing their name.

**Advocacy is respectful and specific.** Speaking up for a client who has been wrongly denied or delayed does not mean escalating in anger. It means knowing what the client is entitled to, naming the specific decision you are questioning, asking who has the authority to review it, and documenting the exchange. It also means telling your field mentor before you do it, especially early in your placement — your program has relationships with these agencies, and your mentor may know that the fastest route to a reversal is a named contact rather than the main line. Advocacy that burns a partnership costs the next twenty clients.

## Keeping an evidence log

An evidence log is a short, dated, de-identified record of your practice, kept by you, for you. It is not the program's chart, it is not a substitute for your required documentation in the agency's system, and it never leaves your own control.

One entry per meaningful activity. Keep it to four labeled fields — date, role(s), what you did, outcome and follow-up — each a line or two long.

```text
Date: 2026-03-14
Role(s): Care coordination / system navigation; Advocacy
What I did: Placed a specialty referral for a client whose prior referral had
  lapsed. Called the scheduling line twice, then asked the referral coordinator
  to review the lapse. Appointment scheduled for 3/28.
Outcome / follow-up: Client confirmed appointment by phone 3/21. Reminder call
  set for 3/27. Supervisor notified of the intake line's two-week callback lag.
```

Write it the same day. An entry written a week later is a memory, and memories drift toward whatever story you have started telling. Aim for a handful of entries a week rather than every encounter — you want the ones that show something, not a duplicate log of your shifts.

Over a term this file becomes three things at once: the raw material for your resume bullets in lesson four, the agenda for your supervision meetings, and the source of the specific examples you will be asked for in an interview. It is also how you notice a gap in time to fix it. If you reach week six with no entry under education or outreach, that is a conversation to have with your mentor while there are still weeks left.

## De-identification is not optional, and it is not hard

Nothing in your log identifies a client. No name, no initials, no date of birth, no address or cross street, no medical record or case number, no employer, no diagnosis paired with any other detail that narrows the field, and no photograph. Not in the log, not in your portfolio, not in class discussion, not in a text to a classmate, not in a story told at dinner.

Write the role, not the person. "A client managing a chronic condition" carries every bit of the professional meaning that a name would, and none of the exposure. Be especially careful with combinations: in a small community, "a pregnant teenager at the Thursday drop-in" identifies someone as surely as a name does, even though no field in that sentence is a name. Apply the same test to details that feel incidental — the language someone speaks, the shelter they stay at, the fact that they are the only person on a street with a certain circumstance.

If you are unsure whether an entry is de-identified, ask whether a coworker at your placement could read it and know who you meant. If they could, cut detail until they could not. And if a de-identified version would no longer be useful evidence, the answer is not to keep the identifiers — it is to write about what you did rather than about the case.

## Practice

1. **Map one week.** List every distinct activity from your last week of placement hours. Beside each, name the C3 role or roles it evidences and the change it produced. Mark any activity where you cannot name a change; those are your open loops.
2. **Build your log.** Create your evidence log file and write four entries in the four-line format above, drawn from that week. De-identify as you write, not afterward. Read each entry back and apply the coworker test.
3. **Find your gaps.** Compare your week's roles against the list of ten. Name the two roles with the least evidence and write one sentence each proposing an experience your placement could realistically give you.
4. **Close one referral loop.** Pick a referral you placed that you have not confirmed. Follow up with the client, ask what actually happened, record the barrier if there was one, and log the outcome.
5. **Bring it to supervision.** Take your gap list and one advocacy question to your next field mentor meeting. Write down what your mentor tells you about how your organization prefers advocacy to be raised with its partner agencies, and add that to your log as its own entry.
