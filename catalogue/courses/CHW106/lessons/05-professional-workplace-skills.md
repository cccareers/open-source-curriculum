## The skills that decide whether you keep the job

Community Health Workers are rarely hired for their clinical knowledge and rarely let go for lacking it. They are hired because someone believed they would show up, and they are let go because the notes were late, the handoff never happened, or a supervisor could not predict what would get done.

These are usually called soft skills, which is a poor name for them. In this occupation they are the skills the whole role rests on, because a CHW's product is trust — the client's trust that you will do what you said, and the team's trust that what you wrote is accurate. This lesson treats four of them as practice you can improve deliberately: reliability, documentation, teamwork, and receiving feedback.

## Reliability is a trust issue, not a scheduling one

When you are ten minutes late to a client's home, the client does not experience a scheduling problem. They experience being deprioritized — and many of the people you serve have a long history of being deprioritized by institutions. That history is exactly what makes a CHW valuable in the first place: you are the person who is not the system. Every kept commitment is evidence for that claim, and every broken one is evidence against it.

The consequences compound in ways that are easy to miss. A client who was stood up once is less likely to answer the second call. Less contact means less information, which means a less accurate picture for the care team, which means a plan built on a gap. Meanwhile your colleagues absorb the work — a missed home visit becomes a nurse's phone call, and the third time it happens they stop assigning you the visits that matter.

Reliability is built from three habits, none of them complicated.

**Capture every commitment the moment you make it.** One list, one place, always with you. Paper notebook or phone task list — the tool is irrelevant, the singularity is not. Two lists means the commitment recorded on the one you left at the office. Write what you promised, to whom, and by when. Write it in front of the client.

**Protect a documentation and follow-up block.** Hold thirty to forty-five minutes in your day that belongs to closing loops rather than opening them. Interns who improvise this time never find it, because client work expands to fill everything available. The block is what makes same-day documentation possible.

**Communicate slippage before the deadline, not after.** A schedule will break — a client is in crisis, a partner agency's line is down, you are ill. What separates a professional from an unreliable colleague is not that the plan held. It is that everyone affected heard the new plan from you, in advance, with a new date. Call the client, tell your supervisor, update the record. Then meet the new date, because a second slip on the same commitment costs far more than the first.

One more, for illness and emergencies: know your site's exact call-out method and window, and use it. "I texted a coworker" is not a call-out. Follow the policy even on the day when following it feels excessive.

## Documentation: accurate and on time

Your notes are not paperwork about the work. They are part of it. The next person who touches that client — a nurse, a case manager, the CHW covering your vacation, a reviewer confirming that a funded service was delivered — knows only what you wrote. What you remember is invisible to them and disappears entirely when you leave.

**Timeliness has a definition, and it is your site's.** Learn it precisely: same shift, within twenty-four hours, before the end of the billing period. Then beat it. Notes written from memory the next morning are shorter, more general, and quietly wrong in details you were sure about. Late documentation also creates a window in which the record actively misleads — a colleague reading Tuesday sees no contact and calls a client you already reached, or worse, does not act on a need you already identified.

**Accuracy means facts and observations, not conclusions about people.** Write what you saw, what was said, what you did, and what happens next. Distinguish what the client reported from what you observed. Keep judgment out of the record.

```text
Weak:  Client is non-compliant and unmotivated about her medications.

Better: Client reports she has not taken her evening medication for the past
        4 days. States, "I can't get to the pharmacy since my sister moved."
        Pharmacy is 3.2 miles from home; client reports no vehicle access.
        CHW provided bus route information and requested a 90-day mail-order
        fill through the care team. Follow-up call scheduled 4/18.
```

The second version is longer, and it earns the length. It is factual, it quotes the client where the exact words matter, it names a barrier the team can act on, and it records a next step with a date. The first version is a character assessment that a future reader will carry into their own encounter with that person — and it is the kind of line that reads badly in a record request years later.

**Timely and accurate documentation is what makes your work count.** Programs are funded on documented encounters. Grant reports, quality metrics, and the case for keeping a CHW position on staff are all assembled from what was written down. An encounter that happened and was not documented did not happen, as far as the organization's survival is concerned.

**Cover the essentials every time.** Date, time, and type of contact. Who was present. What the client reported. What you observed. What you did, including education provided and referrals placed. Barriers identified. The plan, with a date. Your name and role.

**Correct errors properly.** Every system has an amendment procedure — a correction entry, a strikethrough with initials, an addendum. Use it. Never overwrite a record to hide a mistake, and never backdate an entry. A documented correction is routine; a record that was quietly altered is a serious matter in an audit.

**Confidentiality applies inside the record too.** Document the client you are documenting. Naming a third party's private information in someone else's chart, or recording detail that no one caring for this client needs, is a breach even though it happened in an authorized system.

## Teamwork and the handoff

Your placement runs on a team you are the newest member of, and most of what makes a good teammate here is unglamorous.

**Learn who does what, and route accordingly.** Interns lose hours to problems the front-desk coordinator solves in ninety seconds. Ask early who owns which decision and write it down.

**Communicate up, before it becomes urgent.** Supervisors do not mind questions; they mind surprises. A situation raised on Tuesday is a two-minute conversation. The same situation raised on Friday, after you tried to fix it, is a problem for someone else's weekend.

**Make the handoff explicit.** When you pass work to a colleague — coverage during your absence, a warm handoff to a case manager, an escalation to a clinician — say who is picking it up, what exactly they are picking up, and what the client has been told. Confirm they received it. Then document the handoff. Silent handoffs are where clients are dropped, and the client rarely finds out until the appointment they thought was scheduled arrives and nobody comes.

**Cover in the direction you would want covered.** Notice when a colleague is buried and offer something specific rather than a general "let me know if you need anything." Specific offers get accepted; general ones do not.

**Keep the team's discussion where it belongs.** Clinical or program disagreements go to the person involved and to your supervisor, not to the break room and never anywhere a client could hear. This is a boundary as much as it is teamwork.

**Respect the different training in the room.** You will sit with nurses, social workers, care managers, and administrators whose training differs from yours. You bring something none of them have — the client's reality, the community's context, the reason the plan will not work. Say it plainly and with the evidence you gathered. Being the newest person in the room is not a reason to stay quiet when you know something the plan depends on.

## Receiving feedback

Feedback is the fastest instrument you have for improvement and the one most people flinch from. Practice a response that removes the flinch.

**Listen all the way to the end.** Do not begin composing the explanation while they are still talking. Most of what feels like criticism is information, and you lose the information while you rehearse.

**Ask for the specific instance.** "Can you tell me which visit you're thinking of?" moves the conversation from a judgment about you to a situation you can both examine. It is not a challenge, and supervisors experience it as engagement rather than defensiveness.

**Say what you will do differently, and put a date on it.** "I'll document before I leave the site, starting this week" is a commitment your supervisor can observe. "I'll try to do better" is not.

**Separate explanation from excuse.** Context sometimes matters — you were pulled into an emergency, the system was down. Give it once, briefly, after you have acknowledged the point. Give it first, at length, and it reads as an excuse regardless of its truth.

**Follow up unprompted.** Two weeks later, tell them what changed. Almost nobody does this, and the ones who do are the ones who get recommended.

**Ask for feedback before it is offered.** At each supervision contact, ask one narrow question: "What is one thing I could do better in my documentation?" A narrow question gets a usable answer; "How am I doing?" gets "fine."

When feedback is unfair — and occasionally it will be — the professional move is still to hear it fully, ask for the specific instance, and then state your account calmly and factually. If it remains unresolved, take it to your program coordinator through the channel your program specifies. Arguing in the moment converts a correctable misunderstanding into a reputation.

## Practice

1. **Consolidate your commitments.** Move every outstanding commitment you are carrying — in your head, on sticky notes, in three apps — into one list with owner and due date. Work only from that list for two weeks.
2. **Schedule the block.** Put a daily thirty-to-forty-five-minute documentation and follow-up block on your placement calendar. At the end of two weeks, count the days you actually used it and what got closed in it.
3. **Rewrite three notes.** Take three of your own recent notes and rewrite them to the fact-and-observation standard: quoted client report, observation, action, barrier, plan with a date. Note what you had to leave out because you no longer remember it.
4. **Time yourself.** Record how long after each client contact you completed documentation, every day for one week. Chart the gap. If any entry exceeds your site's required window, write one sentence naming what got in the way.
5. **Audit one handoff.** Take a handoff you made recently. Was the receiver named, did they confirm, was the client told, was it documented? Write the version you should have done.
6. **Ask a narrow question.** At your next supervision contact, ask for one specific improvement in your documentation. Write down the answer verbatim, act on it for two weeks, then report back to your mentor unprompted.
7. **Learn your call-out policy.** Write down, from your site's own documentation, exactly whom you notify for an absence, by what method, and by what time. Confirm it with your mentor.
8. **Draft your professionalism self-assessment.** Rate yourself honestly on punctuality, commitment follow-through, documentation timeliness, documentation accuracy, handoffs, and response to feedback. Bring it to your final evaluation and compare it with your mentor's assessment. Where you differ is where your next term of growth is.
