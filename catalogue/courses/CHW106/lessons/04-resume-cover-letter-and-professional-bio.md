## You have more experience than your resume says

Interns consistently undersell themselves, and the reason is structural rather than modest. The work of a Community Health Worker is made of small acts — a call placed, a form completed, a person walked to a door — and small acts do not announce themselves as achievements. So the resume says "assisted clients with resources and referrals," which describes the job description rather than the person who held it, and a hiring manager reading forty applications cannot tell you apart from anyone else who wrote the same line.

Your evidence log from lesson two is the fix. It holds actions and outcomes, dated, in your own words. This lesson turns that raw material into three documents that do different jobs: a resume that gets you screened in, a cover letter that explains why this role and this organization, and a professional bio that represents you in program materials, partner introductions, and online profiles.

One constraint governs all three, and it is not negotiable. Client information never appears in them. Not a name, not a photograph, not a story specific enough to identify someone, not a "case example" from your placement. You will learn here how to write powerfully about your own practice while the people in it remain invisible — which is itself a skill employers in this field notice, because the applicant who leaks a client detail in an interview has told them everything they need to know.

## The quantified, verifiable statement

Strong resume bullets have a shape. Action verb, what you did, scale, and result — where every element is something your supervisor would confirm.

Watch a weak bullet become a strong one.

```text
Weak:    Assisted clients with referrals and follow-up.
Better:  Coordinated referrals to community health and social service agencies
         and followed up to confirm connection.
Strong:  Coordinated 60+ referrals to clinical and social service partners over
         a 12-week internship; followed up on every referral and confirmed
         client connection in 4 of 5 cases, resolving transportation and
         documentation barriers on the remainder.
```

Three things changed. A number appeared. An outcome appeared — connection confirmed, not referral placed. And a barrier appeared, which tells the reader you understand that the referral is not the finish line.

**Quantify from what you actually tracked.** Number of clients carried. Number of contacts, referrals, or home visits. Number of education sessions delivered and roughly how many attended. Number of intakes or screenings completed. Hours of supervised field practice. A percentage or a ratio when you genuinely counted; a plain count when you did not. Your evidence log and your site's own reports are where these come from.

**Never invent or inflate a number.** Every figure on your resume is a claim your reference will be asked to support. A round number you estimated is fine if it is honest and you say so in an interview ("roughly sixty over the term"); a number you improved for effect is a fireable discovery. If you did not count it, use scale words that are true — "a caseload of four to six clients at a time" is verifiable and specific enough.

**Verifiable means your supervisor would recognize it.** Before a bullet goes on the resume, ask whether your field mentor, reading it cold, would say yes, that is what happened. If they would hesitate, rewrite it until they would not. This is also a good reason to show them the draft — most mentors are glad to, and some will remember outcomes you did not know about.

**Use the field's vocabulary.** Care coordination, system navigation, health education, outreach and engagement, needs assessment, social determinants of health, motivational interviewing, client-centered goal setting, advocacy, de-identified documentation, HIPAA-aware practice, mandated reporting, cultural humility, language access, warm handoff, closed-loop referral. These are the words in the job postings and, increasingly, in the software that screens resumes before a human sees them. Use the ones that are true of you.

## The resume

For an entry-level CHW role, one page, in this order.

**Contact line.** Name, city and state, phone, professional email address, and a profile link if you maintain one. No photo, no date of birth, no full street address.

**Professional summary, two or three lines.** Who you are, your credential status, your field hours, and your focus. Concrete beats aspirational.

```text
Community Health Worker with 200+ hours of supervised field experience in
outpatient care coordination and community outreach. Bilingual English/Spanish.
Experienced in closed-loop referrals, client-centered goal setting, and accurate
same-day documentation. Seeking a CHW role serving high-need populations.
```

**Certifications and training.** Your CHW program completion, plus anything else you hold: CPR/first aid, HIPAA training, mental health first aid, motivational interviewing, food handler, valid driver's license and insurance where the posting asks for it. Put a date on each. If your state certification is pending, say "in progress, expected [month year]" rather than implying you hold it.

**Experience.** Your internship goes here as a real job, not under a heading that apologizes for it. Organization, your title — "Community Health Worker Intern" — city and state, and dates. Then three to six bullets in the shape above. Include prior employment even when it is unrelated: retail, food service, caregiving, and warehouse work all demonstrate reliability, teamwork, and customer contact, and a hiring manager reading a resume with a gap will wonder about the gap instead of your bullets.

**Skills.** A short scannable block. Languages spoken and your honest fluency level. Software you have actually used, named specifically — the electronic health record or case management system by name, resource directory platforms, spreadsheets, telehealth tools. Skills you can demonstrate, not skills you have heard of.

**Education.** Degrees and dates. Relevant coursework only if it adds something.

**Volunteer and community involvement.** Genuinely valued in this field, because community ties are part of the job. Keep it brief.

Two formatting rules that matter more than they should: save and send as PDF unless the posting demands otherwise, and name the file with your own name in it, not "resume_final_v3.pdf."

## The cover letter

Three or four short paragraphs, on one page, rewritten for each application. A generic letter is worse than no letter, because it demonstrates exactly the lack of attention you would bring to a caseload.

**Open with the specific role and one honest reason for this organization.** Something you know about them — the population they serve, a program they run, a value in their materials that matches how you work. One sentence, and it must be true.

**Second paragraph: your strongest evidence.** Take the best two or three items from your resume and tell the story the bullets could not. Keep the numbers. Keep the client invisible.

```text
During my internship at a community clinic, I carried a caseload of four to six
clients at a time and coordinated more than sixty referrals across twelve weeks.
The part of that work I am proudest of is the follow-up: I confirmed connection
on four out of five referrals, which usually meant finding out why the first
attempt failed and solving a transportation or paperwork problem the referral
form never captured.
```

**Third paragraph: fit for what they asked for.** Read the posting's requirements and answer two or three of them directly, in their order. If they ask for bilingual capacity, home visiting, a specific population, or documentation in a named system, address it plainly.

**Close with availability and a clear next step.** When you can start, and that you would welcome the chance to talk. Then stop.

Address a person if you can find one. "Dear Hiring Manager" is acceptable; "To Whom It May Concern" reads as a form letter, because it is one.

## The professional bio

A bio is written in third person and used where a resume would not fit: a program roster, a partner introduction, a conference or workshop listing, a profile page. Write two versions and keep both current.

**The short version, forty to sixty words**, for a roster line or a byline.

```text
[Your Name] is a Community Health Worker serving [community or region], with
field experience in care coordination, outreach, and health education. [She/He/They]
completed [program name] and 200+ hours of supervised practice at a community
clinic, where [she/he/they] focused on closing referral loops for clients facing
transportation and language barriers.
```

**The long version, 120 to 180 words**, adds where you come from and why you do this work. This is the one place a sentence of personal motivation belongs — "grew up in the community she now serves," "began this work after navigating the health system for a family member" — provided it is yours to tell. It is not the place for a client's story, ever, in any form.

Update both when something changes. A bio that still says "currently completing an internship" six months after you finished is a small signal of the same thing a missed commitment signals.

## The portfolio, and what stays out of it

If your program asks for a portfolio, it holds artifacts you created and de-identified: a resource guide you assembled, an outreach flyer, an education session outline, a blank version of a form or workflow you designed, your reflection on the capstone project, your certificates. It may hold a de-identified description of a challenge you worked through.

It never holds a completed client form, a chart excerpt, a screenshot of a record system, an intake with fields filled in, a photograph of a client or of a workspace with a visible screen, or a de-identified narrative so specific that someone at your site would recognize the person. When you want to show your documentation skill, create a sample record for a fictional client and label it as fictional. That demonstrates the skill exactly as well and carries none of the risk.

## References

Ask three people before you need them: your field mentor, a program instructor, and a supervisor from prior employment. Ask directly whether they can serve as a strong reference — the phrasing matters, because it gives someone a graceful way to decline rather than damn you politely later. Send each of them your final resume and a note about the kinds of roles you are applying for. Give them the numbers you used, so they are not surprised by them. Keep their preferred contact details current, and tell them when you have listed them for a specific role.

## Practice

1. **Mine the log.** Go through your evidence log and pull every activity with a countable scale or a confirmed outcome. Build a plain list of raw facts — no polish yet.
2. **Write ten bullets, keep six.** Turn those facts into ten bullets in the action-scale-outcome shape. Then cut the four weakest. Cutting is where the quality comes from.
3. **Run the verification test.** For each surviving bullet, write beside it the person who could confirm it and how. Any bullet without a confirmer gets rewritten until it has one, or gets dropped.
4. **Assemble the one-page resume** in the section order above, and export it as a PDF named with your own name.
5. **Answer a real posting.** Find an actual CHW job posting in your area. Write the cover letter for it, addressing at least three of its stated requirements in the order the posting lists them.
6. **Write both bios,** short and long, and read the long one aloud. If a sentence is hard to say, rewrite it.
7. **Run the confidentiality audit.** Read all three documents and your portfolio as if you were your field mentor. Highlight anything a person at your placement could trace to a specific client, and remove it.
8. **Get it reviewed.** Send the resume to your field mentor and ask one question: is there anything here you would not confirm? Revise on the answer.
