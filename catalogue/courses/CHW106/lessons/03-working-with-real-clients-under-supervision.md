## The goal

Carry a small, real caseload at your placement for a sustained block of your internship hours, and finish it with a supervisor who will say three things about you without hesitation: you kept every client's information confidential, you held professional boundaries and brought the hard ones to supervision early, and you did what you said you would do when you said you would do it.

This is the heart of the capstone. It is not a simulation and there is no reset. The people you work with are managing real illness, real housing instability, real fear, and they will experience your reliability or your absence directly. The deliverable is your conduct across the block, evidenced by a supervision record and a de-identified reflection — not a paper about clients.

Plan on roughly twelve hours of placement time inside this block, arranged with your field mentor. Your program's hour requirements and your site's own expectations both still apply and take precedence over anything here.

## Choosing your caseload

Work with your field mentor to identify a small set of clients — typically three to six, depending on your site — whom you will follow for the whole block rather than meeting once. The caseload must be:

- **Assigned, not self-selected.** Your mentor decides who is appropriate for an intern to carry, and can remove someone at any time.
- **Continuous.** You are the consistent point of contact across at least two contacts per client, so that reliability and follow-through are observable.
- **Within scope.** Every activity is one your placement authorizes interns to perform. If you are not sure whether a task is in scope, it is not, until your mentor says it is.

Write the caseload scope down in one paragraph and have your mentor confirm it before your first contact. Name the number of clients, the kind of work you will do, and the tasks explicitly excluded.

## Requirements

Numbered so your field mentor can confirm them one at a time.

**R1 — Confidentiality holds on every channel.** Client information stays inside the systems and conversations your placement authorizes. No names, identifiers, photos, or identifying combinations of detail in texts, personal email, personal notes, social media, group chats, classroom discussion, or conversations with family and friends. No client information travels on a personal device beyond what your site's policy explicitly permits.

**R2 — No hallway breaches.** Client discussion happens in private spaces with people who have a role in that client's care. Waiting rooms, elevators, break rooms, transit, and the parking lot are not private, and neither is a video call taken where a household member can hear.

**R3 — Every commitment is tracked and met.** You keep a written commitment list — what you promised, to whom, by when. Nothing lives only in your head. If you will miss a commitment, the client and your mentor hear it from you before the deadline, not after.

**R4 — Punctual and prepared.** You arrive on time for every shift and every client contact, with the materials, forms, and resource information the contact requires. Lateness or absence is communicated to your site by the method and within the window your placement specifies.

**R5 — Boundaries are stated plainly and held.** You work within the CHW role. You do not lend or give money, transport clients in your personal vehicle, connect on personal social media, share your personal phone number or address where your site provides a work channel, accept gifts beyond what site policy allows, take on tasks belonging to a licensed role, or make promises about outcomes you do not control.

**R6 — Boundary pressure is reported early, not managed alone.** The first time a boundary is tested — a request for your personal number, a client who begins contacting you outside hours, a gift, an invitation, a relationship that starts to feel like friendship — you raise it with your field mentor at the next supervision contact or sooner. Early means before you have decided how to handle it.

**R7 — Escalation is immediate for safety.** Anything involving risk to life or immediate safety goes to your field mentor or the on-site designated staff member right now, during the same shift, by the fastest channel your site specifies. This includes suicidal or homicidal statements, disclosure of abuse or neglect, threats, weapons, medical emergencies, and any situation where you feel unsafe. You do not wait for a scheduled meeting, and you do not decide alone whether it was serious enough.

**R8 — Mandated reporting duties continue during your internship.** Being a student does not suspend them. Learn, in writing, before your first client contact: whether your role is a mandated reporter under your state's law and your site's policy, who at your site you notify, the timeframe for the notification and for any written report, and whether the report is filed by you, jointly, or by a designated staff member. Reasonable suspicion is the standard — it is not your job to investigate or confirm before reporting, and a report is never something you talk yourself out of because you might be wrong.

**R9 — Supervision is active, not passive.** You come to each supervision contact with a written agenda: what you did, what you are unsure about, what you need decided. You leave with the decisions written down. Waiting to be asked is not supervision.

**R10 — Documentation is same-shift and factual.** Every client contact is documented in your site's system, in your site's format, before you leave — or within whatever window your site requires, whichever is shorter. Facts and observations, not conclusions about character. Quote the client where the exact words matter.

**R11 — Scope refusals are handled and logged.** When you are asked for something outside your role, you say what you cannot do, say what you can do or who can, and tell your mentor it happened. This includes requests from clients, from family members, and from staff.

**R12 — De-identified reflection.** You keep a short reflection across the block: two or three entries per week, each naming a decision you made about confidentiality, a boundary, or a commitment, and what you would do differently. Fully de-identified, per the standard in lesson two.

## Constraints

- **Your site's policy always wins.** Where anything above conflicts with your placement's policy, your supervisor's direction, or applicable law, follow the site and tell your program's coordinator about the conflict.
- **No independent practice.** You act within your assigned scope, under supervision. You do not take on a client, task, or setting your mentor has not approved because the need looked urgent.
- **No clinical judgment.** You do not diagnose, advise on medication, interpret test results, or contradict clinical instructions. You surface what you observe to the appropriate professional.
- **No client information in the deliverable.** Nothing you submit for this course contains an identifier or an identifying combination. This is a hard gate: a submission containing client-identifying detail is returned unmarked and treated as a confidentiality incident.
- **No recording.** No audio, video, or photography of client encounters, on any device, for any purpose, including your own study.
- **Personal channels stay personal.** Client communication runs through the channels your site provides.

## Definition of done

Every statement must be true and confirmable by your field mentor.

- You completed the agreed block of placement hours with the assigned caseload, with at least two contacts per client.
- Your mentor confirms zero confidentiality incidents across all channels for the block.
- Your commitment list shows every commitment made during the block and its outcome; each missed commitment shows a communication that preceded the deadline.
- Attendance and punctuality for the block meet your site's standard, with any absence communicated by the site's required method and within its window.
- At least one boundary situation — real or, if none arose, one you identify as foreseeable in your setting — was raised in supervision, with your mentor's guidance recorded.
- You can state your mandated-reporting pathway from memory: your status, your internal notification contact, the timeframe, and who files.
- Every client contact in the block is documented in your site's system within the required window.
- You brought a written agenda to every supervision contact, and the decisions from each are recorded.
- Your reflection covers the full block and contains no client-identifying detail.
- Your field mentor signs a short evaluation of confidentiality, boundaries, and reliability.

## What to hand in

1. Your one-paragraph caseload scope, confirmed by your mentor before the block began.
2. Your commitment list for the block, de-identified — clients referred to as "Client A," "Client B," and so on.
3. Your supervision agendas and the decisions recorded from each contact, de-identified.
4. Your written mandated-reporting pathway for this placement.
5. Your de-identified reflection entries.
6. Your field mentor's signed evaluation.

## Hints

**Write the commitment down in front of the client.** It signals that you take it seriously, it fixes the details while they are fresh, and it protects you from the softest failure mode there is — a promise you meant sincerely and then forgot.

**A missed commitment is recoverable; a silent one is not.** Clients forgive delays far more readily than they forgive being left wondering. Call before the deadline, name the new date, and meet that one.

**Boundary drift is gradual and it always feels like kindness.** Staying twenty minutes late once, answering one text on a Sunday, one small favor. Nobody crosses a line in a single step. The signal to watch for is a decision you would rather not mention in supervision — that reluctance is the whole warning.

**"Let me check with my supervisor" is a complete answer.** It is not weakness and clients do not hear it as one. It is what a professional says when a request sits at the edge of their role.

**Rehearse the refusal before you need it.** Write down, in your own words, how you will decline a request for money, a ride, or your personal number. Under pressure you will say whatever you have already said to yourself.

**Confidentiality fails in ordinary moments.** Not in dramatic disclosures — in a screen left unlocked, a name said in a corridor, a chart photographed "just to remember the number," a story told to someone who knows the neighborhood. Audit your own habits at the end of week one.

**Escalate on the low end of your uncertainty.** If you are debating whether something is serious enough to interrupt your mentor, you have already answered the question. Over-escalating costs a conversation; under-escalating costs something you cannot get back.

**Do the documentation before you leave.** Notes written the next morning are shorter, vaguer, and less accurate, and the gap is visible to anyone reading the record later.
