## Navigation is a skill you perform with the client, not for them

The fastest way to get a client an appointment is to make the call yourself while they sit there. Do that every time and you have built a client who needs you to make every call for the rest of their life. The slower way — the client dials, you sit beside them — takes eleven minutes instead of four and leaves behind something they keep.

This is the "do with, not for" principle, and it is the spine of both navigation and coaching. It does not mean abandoning people. It means choosing, deliberately and out loud, how much of each task the client does today.

A workable ladder, from most support to least:

1. **You do it, the client watches.** Appropriate in a crisis, when the client is unwell, when the system is hostile, or the first time through something complicated. Narrate what you are doing so watching is still learning.
2. **You do it together.** You dial, the client talks. Or the client dials and you sit next to them with the script.
3. **The client does it, you are in the room.** They call from your office while you listen, ready to step in.
4. **The client does it alone, then tells you.** You prepare beforehand and debrief afterward.
5. **The client does it and it does not come up.** They handled it. You may not even hear about it.

Say the rung out loud: "Last time I made the call. This time, how about you talk and I'll sit right here in case they ask something weird?" That sentence is the coaching.

## Making an appointment, taught as a skill

Most clients are not blocked by the concept of calling a clinic. They are blocked by the specifics: the phone tree, the questions they will be asked, not knowing their member ID, and the fear of sounding foolish.

Prepare before dialing. Put in front of the client, on one piece of paper:

- The clinic name, number, and which department to ask for
- Their insurance or program ID, and date of birth
- What to ask for, in one sentence
- Days and times they genuinely cannot come
- A pen, to write down the date, time, and the name of the person they spoke to

Then rehearse the first fifteen seconds — that is where most calls fail.

```text
CHW:    They'll pick up and say "Valley Health, how can I help you." What
        do you say?
Client: Um. That I need a doctor?
CHW:    That works, and they'll ask three things after it. Let's make it
        so you only have to say it once. Try: "Hi, I'm a new patient and I
        need to schedule a first appointment."
Client: Hi, I'm a new patient, I need to schedule a first appointment.
CHW:    That's it. Then they ask your date of birth and your insurance.
        Where's that on your card?
Client: (points) This long number?
CHW:    That one. Circle it. If they put you on hold, that's normal — it's
        not you. If they say something you don't understand, you can say
        "can you say that a different way?" You're allowed to say that.
Client: Okay. I'll call.
CHW:    I'm right here. Write down the day, the time, and the name of
        whoever you talk to.
```

Two details matter. The CHW gave the client a repair phrase — "can you say that a different way?" — which transfers to every other system the client will meet. And the client wrote down the details themselves, which is both a record and a rehearsal.

Afterward, debrief in a sentence or two: what worked, what was harder than expected, what you would both do differently. That is what turns one call into a skill.

## Referral forms and paperwork

Forms are a literacy test that nobody consented to take. They are dense, they use program jargon, and they ask for documents people do not have.

Working method:

- **Read the form yourself first.** Never open a form cold in front of a client. You should know which sections apply, which can be skipped, and which will require a document.
- **Tell the client the shape of it.** "There are four pages. Three of them are easy. Page two asks about income and that's the one that takes time."
- **Let the client hold the pen when they can.** If they ask you to write, write — and read each answer back before it goes in the box.
- **Never guess an answer.** If the client does not know their household income to the dollar, write what they do know and note the gap. A confidently wrong answer causes a denial three weeks later.
- **Make a copy or a photo before it goes anywhere,** and give the client one.
- **Write down what happens next and when.** "They said 10 business days. That's the 24th. If you haven't heard by the 25th, call this number and give them this confirmation number."
- **Name the documents needed** and make a plan for the ones they do not have. Missing documents, not unwillingness, are what kill most applications.

Two things to protect. First, privacy: you are handling income, immigration-adjacent, and health information on a piece of paper in a public building. Do not leave it face up, and do not carry a stack of other clients' forms into a visit. Second, scope: filling in a medical history section is helping; deciding what belongs in it is not. When a form asks something clinical, the answer comes from the client or their provider, not from you.

## Keeping a resource directory that is actually true

A resource directory is only worth what its last verification date says. Sending a client across town to a food pantry that closed in March costs them a bus fare, an afternoon, and some of their willingness to try the next thing you suggest.

What a usable entry contains:

| Field | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Organization, program name, and physical address | The program name is what the client must ask for at the desk |
| Direct phone, and the name of a real person if you have one | Main lines route to voicemail |
| Hours, including which days the service actually runs | "Open 9-5" and "does intake Tuesdays only" are different facts |
| Eligibility in plain words | Income, residence, age, documents, insurance |
| Languages available and interpretation options | Decides whether the referral can work at all |
| Access notes | Bus route, parking, stairs, entrance to use, whether ID is required at the door |
| Cost | Free, sliding scale, or what people actually end up paying |
| What to bring | The single most common reason a client is turned away |
| Last verified date and who verified it | The field that makes the rest trustworthy |

Verification habits that survive a real caseload:

- Set a cadence — a common practice is calling each entry quarterly, and monthly for high-volume ones like food, shelter, and utility assistance.
- Verify by calling as though you were a client, not as a professional. You will learn what the client will actually hear.
- Update the entry the moment a client reports something different, and flag it as unverified until you confirm it.
- Note closures and waitlist status, including the date. "Waitlist closed as of 3/14" is useful; "waitlist closed" is not.
- Keep it somewhere shared. A directory living in one CHW's notebook disappears when they take a week off.
- Delete or clearly mark dead entries. A directory with stale entries in it teaches the whole team not to trust any of it.

Do not put client-identifying information in the directory. It is a resource file, not a case record.

## Recognizing capacity and stepping back

The end state of good navigation is a client who does not need you. That should be discussed openly and early, not sprung on someone as a discharge.

Signs capacity has grown:

- The client is telling you about calls they already made.
- They know the vocabulary — they say "prior authorization" and know what it means for them.
- They are correcting you on details of their own case.
- They ask for the number rather than asking you to call.
- They are troubleshooting: "the line was busy so I went in person."
- They are helping someone else in their household or building do the same thing.

When you see that, name it and renegotiate.

```text
CHW:    Something I noticed — you rescheduled the eye appointment and got
        the transport set up without calling me at all.
Client: I mean, it wasn't a big deal.
CHW:    Four months ago that was the thing you dreaded most. That's a real
        change, and you did it.
Client: (laughs) I guess.
CHW:    So I want to change how we work. Instead of every week, what if we
        go to twice a month, and you call me if something comes up? Same
        number, same me. You've got the housing recertification in
        November — that one's still messy, and I'd want to be on that
        with you.
Client: Twice a month is fine. And yeah, help me with the housing thing.
```

The move is not withdrawal. It is calibration: less support on what the client now owns, full support on what is still hard, and an explicit door back in.

Two failure modes to watch in yourself. **Fading out without saying so** — quietly reducing contact — reads to the client as being dropped, and it is how people fall out of programs. And **keeping people longer than they need you** feels like care and is not; if you find it hard to reduce contact with a client who is clearly ready, that is a supervision conversation, not a scheduling one. Document the change in support level and the reason, so the record shows a plan rather than a gap.

## Practice

1. **Ladder placement.** Given six client scenarios (new diagnosis and overwhelmed; six months in and confident; in crisis; limited English with an interpreter available; recently housed after eviction; a client who has done this twice before), place each on the five-rung support ladder and write the exact sentence you would say to set the level. Compare with a partner and argue the ones you disagree on.

2. **Call rehearsal, both chairs.** Take turns as CHW and client. Prepare a client to call a clinic they have never contacted: assemble the one-pager, rehearse the opening fifteen seconds, and give them a repair phrase. Then swap and be the difficult receptionist — put them on hold, ask for a member ID, transfer them to the wrong department.

3. **Form walk-through.** Using a blank sample application your instructor supplies, run a fifteen-minute session. You must read the form in advance, describe its shape before starting, and end with a written next-step line including a date. The observer checks whether the client held the pen and whether every unknown was recorded as unknown.

4. **Directory audit.** Take five entries from your program's directory (or a set provided in class). For each, list what is missing against the fields table above. Call two of them as a client would and record what you learned that the entry did not say.

5. **Verification call script.** Write and run a two-minute verification call: hours, eligibility, what to bring, current waitlist status, languages. Update the entry with today's date and your initials.

6. **Step-back conversation.** Role-play the renegotiation with a client who has clearly built capacity but reacts badly — "so you're done with me?" Get to a specific new arrangement without either backing down entirely or dismissing the reaction. Then write the two-line case note documenting the change.

7. **Self-check.** Review your last week (or a provided sample caseload) and name one task you did for a client that they could have done with you. Write the sentence you will use next time to hand it back.
