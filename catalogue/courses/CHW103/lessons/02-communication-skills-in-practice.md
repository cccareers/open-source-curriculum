## Plain language is a translation job

Health systems run on a dialect. Clinics, insurers, and public programs each have their own vocabulary, and none of it was built for the person receiving the service. A large part of your daily work is translation: taking what a provider, a form, or a letter said, and re-saying it in words the client can act on today.

Plain language does not mean simple ideas. It means ordinary words, short sentences, and one idea at a time. The test is not "did I say it correctly" — it is "can this person do something with what I just said."

| What the system says | What you say |
| --- | --- |
| "You need to fast for twelve hours prior to the lab draw." | "Don't eat or drink anything but water after 8 tonight. Your blood test is at 8 in the morning." |
| "Your prior authorization was denied." | "Your insurance said no to paying for this, for now. There's an appeal, and I can help you start it." |
| "Take this medication as needed for exacerbations." | "Use this inhaler when your breathing gets hard. Not every day — just when it gets bad." |
| "The clinic offers a sliding-scale fee." | "What you pay depends on what you earn. Bring a pay stub and they'll figure out your price." |

Three habits carry most of the load.

**Say the action first.** People hold the first thing they hear. "Don't eat after 8" lands; "Prior to your appointment, it is important that you observe a fasting period" does not.

**Use the everyday word.** High blood pressure, not hypertension. Water pill, not diuretic. If the client will hear the clinical word from someone else, teach both: "They'll call it hypertension. That means high blood pressure."

**Chunk it.** Three items is a conversation. Seven items is a lecture, and a lecture is forgotten. Decide which three matter before the next visit and put the rest in writing.

Watch your own defaults, too. Program staff pick up jargon from the systems they work in and stop hearing it — "intake," "eligibility," "case closure," "no-show," "care plan." Those are our words, not the client's.

## Active listening you can be observed doing

Active listening is not a feeling you have toward the client. It is a set of behaviors someone sitting in the corner of the room could check off.

**Open-ended questions.** "What's been the hardest part of getting to your appointments?" opens the conversation. "Do you have a ride?" closes it after one word. Closed questions have a place — you need a yes or a phone number sometimes — but if your first three questions are all closed, you have run an interview instead of a conversation.

**Paraphrasing.** Say back what you heard, in your own words, and let the client correct you. "So the bus part is fine, it's the transfer downtown that eats an hour" gives the client a chance to say "no, it's the walk after."

**Silence.** Three seconds feels like a minute when you are nervous. Leave it anyway. Most of the useful information in a first visit comes after a pause the CHW did not fill.

**Attentive body language, matched to the person.** Eye contact, an open posture, and a notebook you are not hiding behind all signal that you are present. Read the client rather than applying one rule: steady eye contact is warm for some people and confrontational for others, and a person in distress may need you to look at the table for a while.

**No premature advice.** The most common listening failure is solving too early. The client mentions a problem, and the CHW is three steps into a referral before the client has finished the sentence. You lose the real problem that way.

Compare these two openings.

```text
CHW:    Are you taking the blood pressure pills?
Client: Most days.
CHW:    You really need to take them every day. Do you have a pill box?
Client: No.
CHW:    I can get you one.
Client: Okay.
```

```text
CHW:    Tell me how the blood pressure pills have been going.
Client: (pause) Some days I take them. Some days I don't.
CHW:    Some days you don't. (waits)
Client: The ones I take at night, I forget. I work doubles Tuesday and
        Wednesday and I'm not home.
CHW:    So it's the night dose on the days you're working late — the morning
        one is fine?
Client: Right. Mornings I never miss.
CHW:    That's useful. You've already got a system that works for mornings.
```

The first exchange produced a pill box. The second produced the actual pattern, and a strength to build on. Same thirty seconds.

## Teach-back: checking your explanation, not the client

Asking "do you understand?" or "any questions?" almost always gets a yes and a head shake. It is a politeness test, not a comprehension check. Clients say yes because they do not want to look slow, because they are tired, because you have been kind and they do not want to disappoint you, and because they do not yet know what they missed.

Teach-back asks the client to say the information back in their own words. Done right, it puts the responsibility on you, not on them.

```text
CHW:    I want to make sure I explained this clearly — I don't always do a
        good job of it. When you get home tonight, what are you going to
        tell your daughter about the appointment on Thursday?
Client: That I go Thursday at 9. And I can't eat.
CHW:    That's it. One more piece — the not eating starts the night before.
        Nothing after 8 Wednesday night, water's fine. What time do you
        usually eat dinner?
Client: Around 6.
CHW:    Then you're already fine. Tell me the whole thing once more?
Client: Nothing after 8 Wednesday. Water's okay. Be there 9 Thursday.
CHW:    Perfect.
```

Notice four things. The CHW framed the check as being about their own explanation. The question was concrete and forward-looking — "what will you tell your daughter" beats "can you repeat that." When a piece was missing, the CHW re-taught it instead of correcting the person. And the loop closed with a second pass, because a teach-back that fails once and is never re-checked has told you nothing.

Chunk and check as you go rather than dumping five minutes of information and testing at the end. One idea, teach-back, next idea.

Teach-back is not a quiz and should never sound like one. "Repeat back what I said" is a test. "Help me make sure I got that across" is a check. Also use it for skills, not just facts: have the client show you the inhaler, walk you through the pill organizer, or dial the number on speaker while you sit there.

## Matching the mode to the person

Verbal explanation is the default because it is the easiest for us, not because it is the best for the client. The mode should follow the person's literacy, language, vision, hearing, attention, and situation.

- **Written** suits someone who reads comfortably and wants a reference later. Half a page, large type, one instruction per line, specifics hand-written — a brochure with your three sentences circled beats a clean brochure nobody reads.
- **Visual** works when words are the barrier: a pill card with sun and moon icons, a calendar with the appointment circled, a photo of the building entrance so the client knows which door.
- **Demonstration** works for anything with steps. Show it, then have the client do it while you watch.
- **Phone or text** suits reminders and confirmations, when the client has a reliable phone and has told you texting is safe. Never assume a shared phone is private.
- **In person** is right for hard news, first meetings, and anything the client may feel ashamed about.

Ask instead of guessing: "Would it help if I wrote this down, or would you rather I text it?" Also read the room — someone who holds a form at arm's length, who says "I forgot my glasses" twice, or who hands every paper to their adult child is telling you something about reading without saying it. Respond by changing the mode, not by naming the problem.

Language access is its own skill set, covered in the cultural humility work; for now, know that a qualified interpreter is part of communication, not an interruption to it.

## Empathy and non-judgmental language on sensitive topics

Sensitive topics — money, food, housing, substance use, mental health, immigration, violence at home, sexual health — are exactly the ones where a single word can end the conversation. The client is scanning you for judgment. They will find it if it is there.

Practical moves:

**Ask permission.** "Is it okay if I ask you a couple of questions about food at home?" A client who says yes has agreed to the conversation. A client who says "not today" has told you something too.

**Normalize before you ask.** "A lot of people I work with are stretching food at the end of the month. How's that for you?" Normalizing tells the client they are not the only one and are not being singled out.

**Replace why with what.** "Why didn't you go to the appointment?" asks the person to defend themselves. "What got in the way of Thursday?" asks about the world. Same information, different conversation.

**Reflect the feeling, then wait.** "That sounds exhausting." Do not follow it immediately with a plan.

**Do not minimize.** "It'll be fine," "at least you have your health," and "everything happens for a reason" all tell the client to stop talking.

**Do not make it about you.** A brief "I've been there" can build a bridge; a story about your cousin takes the floor away.

**Stay in scope.** When a client describes symptoms or asks whether they should stop a medication, you do not diagnose and you do not advise. You reflect, you help them get the question to the right person, and you make sure they can ask it clearly when they get there.

```text
Client: I stopped the water pill. I was up all night going to the bathroom
        and I've got work in the morning. I know that's stupid.
CHW:    It's not stupid — a pill that keeps you up when you're working
        doubles is a real problem. Thanks for telling me straight.
Client: I didn't want to say anything at the clinic.
CHW:    That's the exact thing they need to hear, and there may be
        something they can change about the timing. I'm not the one who can
        answer it — but I can help you say it. If we called Monday, what
        would you want to tell them?
Client: That it works but I can't sleep.
CHW:    Say that. Want me to be on the call with you?
```

The CHW removed shame, kept the medical question with the clinic, and turned the moment into self-advocacy the client can repeat.

## Practice

Work in pairs. Rotate roles: CHW, client, observer. The observer's job is to count behaviors, not to give impressions — how many open questions, how many paraphrases, whether teach-back looped.

1. **Jargon rewrite, timed.** Take five sentences from a real appointment letter, denial notice, or discharge instruction your instructor provides. Rewrite each as one or two spoken sentences beginning with the action. Read them aloud to your partner, who must repeat back what they would do. Anything they cannot repeat, rewrite again.

2. **Open-question ladder.** Run a five-minute intake about transportation. You may not ask a closed question until minute four. The observer tallies every closed question that slips out.

3. **Paraphrase-only round.** Your partner talks for two minutes about a barrier. You may only paraphrase and stay silent — no questions, no advice. Afterward, the client says what you got wrong. Almost everyone gets something wrong; that is the point of saying it out loud.

4. **Teach-back loop.** Explain a three-part instruction (appointment time, what to bring, what to do the night before). Run teach-back framed as checking yourself. The client is instructed to get one part wrong. Re-teach and loop until it is right, without correcting the person.

5. **Mode switch.** Same instruction as drill 4, delivered to a client who cannot read comfortably, then to one who is hard of hearing, then to one on a shared phone. Design what you hand over or send in each case.

6. **Sensitive topic role-play.** Choose a scenario card: food running out, a missed dose, a partner reading the client's mail, a screening result the client does not want to discuss. Open with permission and a normalizing statement. The observer flags every "why" question, every minimizing phrase, and every drift toward advice. Then record one round on your phone with consent, listen once, note the three most jargon-heavy phrases you used without noticing, and delete the recording.
