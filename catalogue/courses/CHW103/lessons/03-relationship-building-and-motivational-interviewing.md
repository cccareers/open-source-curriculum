## Trust is built out of small kept promises

Clients do not trust you because of your badge, your training, or the agency behind you. Many of them have good reasons to distrust all three. Trust gets built the same way in this work as anywhere else: you say you will do something small, and then you do it, and then you do it again.

That means the promises you make have to be sized to what you can actually deliver.

| Promise that damages trust | Promise you can keep |
| --- | --- |
| "I'll get you into housing." | "I'll find out today whether that waitlist is open and call you by Friday either way." |
| "Call me anytime." | "I'm here Monday to Thursday. If you call Friday, I'll get back to you Monday morning." |
| "They'll approve it." | "I'll submit it Tuesday and I'll tell you the day I hear anything." |

Three rules follow.

**Call back even when the answer is no.** The call that builds trust is the one where you have nothing good to report. "I checked, the list is closed until spring, and here's what I'm trying next" tells the client you did the work and that they will not be left wondering.

**Show up when you said, and say when you cannot.** A cancelled visit with two hours' notice costs you very little. A no-show costs you months.

**Be the same person every time.** Consistency in tone matters as much as consistency in scheduling. If a client has to guess which version of you is arriving, they will not tell you anything difficult.

Reliability sounds like a soft skill. Treat it as a clinical one: the client who does not trust you will not tell you they stopped the medication, and nobody can help with a problem they never hear about.

## Motivational interviewing: evoking rather than installing

Motivational interviewing (MI) is a way of talking with someone about change that assumes the reasons for change have to come from them. Your job is not to supply motivation. It is to help the client hear their own.

MI rests on a few observable moves.

**Reflective listening.** A reflection is a statement, not a question, that says back what you understood — including what the client implied but did not say. A simple reflection stays close: "Mornings are fine, nights are the problem." A complex reflection adds the meaning underneath: "You're managing this on your own and you're tired of it." Aim for more reflections than questions. If you are asking three questions in a row, you have slipped into interrogation.

**Affirmations.** An affirmation names a specific strength or effort the client actually demonstrated. "You rescheduled that appointment twice and you still made it" is an affirmation. "Good job!" is praise, and praise from a professional can feel like being graded. Affirmations are how you build on strengths instead of cataloging deficits.

**Open questions.** The same skill as in the previous lesson, aimed at change: "What would be different if the mornings were easier?" "What have you already tried?" "How would you want this to look in six months?"

**Summaries.** Every few minutes, gather what the client has said and hand it back, deliberately collecting the change-oriented pieces at the end: "So the cost is real, the clinic hours don't work with your shift — and you said you're tired of feeling like this and your daughter's been asking. Where does that leave you?"

**Change talk** is any statement from the client pointing toward change: the desire ("I want to sleep better"), the ability ("I could probably do mornings"), the reasons ("my daughter's scared"), the need ("I can't keep going like this"), or a commitment ("I'll call Monday"). Your work is to notice it and ask for more of it — "say more about that" — rather than jumping to a plan the second you hear it.

```text
Client: I know I should quit. Everyone tells me.
CHW:    Everybody's got an opinion about it. (reflection, not a lecture)
Client: Yeah. My sister especially.
CHW:    What do you think, when nobody's telling you?
Client: I think about my kid. He's six. He asked me why I go outside.
CHW:    That question stayed with you.
Client: (pause) Yeah.
CHW:    You've told me two things — that you're tired of being told, and
        that when your son asked, it landed different. What would be
        different for you if you did cut down?
Client: I'd stop hiding it, I guess. That's the tiring part.
```

The CHW never argued, never listed health risks, and never used the word "should." The client produced their own reason.

## Ambivalence, and the righting reflex

Ambivalence is not resistance. It is the normal state of a person considering change: wanting it and not wanting it at the same time, both sincerely. A client who says "I want to, but" is not being difficult. They are telling you exactly where the work is.

The **righting reflex** is your urge to fix that ambivalence by arguing for the good side. It is a caring impulse and it backfires reliably, because in a conversation where one person argues for change, the other person argues against it — and people are persuaded most by what they hear themselves say. Argue for the medication and the client will spend five minutes explaining why they cannot take it, and leave more convinced than when they arrived.

What to do instead:

**Roll with it.** Reflect both sides without picking one: "Part of you is done with the pain, and part of you can't see how to fit another appointment into the week."

**Explore both sides on purpose.** Ask about the good things about the current situation first — "What do you get out of the way things are now?" — then the not-so-good. A client who feels their reasons were taken seriously will look at the other side honestly.

**Ask permission before information.** "Would it be all right if I told you what the clinic said about that?" Then give the information plainly and hand it back: "What do you make of that?"

**Let the client choose.** Emphasize autonomy out loud: "This is completely your call. I'll help either way." That sentence removes the thing they were bracing against and often frees them to move.

```text
Client: I'm not going to that group. Sitting in a circle telling strangers
        my business — no.
CHW:    Not your thing at all.  (rolling with it)
Client: I've done it before. It was a waste of a Tuesday.
CHW:    So you tried it, and it cost you an evening and gave you nothing
        back. That's a fair reason to say no.
Client: I mean, the counselor was fine. It was the group part.
CHW:    The counselor part was okay, the group part wasn't. Would it help
        if I found out whether one-to-one is an option there? Totally up to
        you whether you use it.
Client: ...Yeah, find out.
```

Notice what did not happen: no defending the group, no "give it another try," no sigh.

## Boundaries that protect the relationship

Warmth and boundaries are not opposites. Boundaries are what make the warmth sustainable and safe — for the client as much as for you. A client whose CHW lends them money is now in a relationship with a creditor, and they will stop calling when they cannot repay it.

Where the line usually sits in CHW practice:

- **Money and gifts.** You do not lend money and you do not accept cash. Small handmade or food gifts are common, and refusing can be an insult in some communities; accept graciously, share with the team where possible, and tell your supervisor. Anything of real value gets declined kindly and reported.
- **Contact.** Program phone, program hours. Giving out your personal number turns you into the client's only support and eventually into someone who stops answering. Do not friend or follow clients on social media.
- **Rides, keys, errands.** Follow program policy, and if the policy is silent, ask before improvising.
- **Self-disclosure.** Share only what serves the client, briefly, then return the floor. "I've been through something like that" can be powerful; twenty minutes of your story is not.
- **Doing for versus doing with.** Taking over is a boundary issue as much as a coaching one. If you make every call, the client's world shrinks to you.

The useful skill here is not memorizing rules — it is noticing **drift**. Drift is gradual: one after-hours text, then five; one ride, then a standing arrangement; a client you think about at 11pm. Drift is not a character failure. It is a signal, and the correct response is supervision, early and without drama: "I want to check something with you. I've been texting this client after hours and I think I'm off track." Supervisors handle these conversations constantly. The dangerous version is the one nobody says out loud.

When you need to reset a boundary, be warm, be specific, and give an alternative.

```text
Client: I texted you Saturday and you didn't answer.
CHW:    You did, and I saw it Monday morning — I'm sorry that felt like
        being ignored. I only have the work phone, and it's off on
        weekends. That's the same for everybody I work with.
Client: So what am I supposed to do if something happens?
CHW:    That's a good question and I don't want to leave you without an
        answer. Let's write down right now who you call on a weekend for
        the two or three things most likely to come up. And anything that
        can wait, text me — I'll get it Monday.
```

## Reading emotional cues

Most of what a client tells you about how a conversation is going is nonverbal. Learn to notice, name to yourself, and adjust — without announcing your interpretation.

Common signals and what they might mean:

- **Going flat or short.** Answers shrink to one word, eye contact drops. Often you moved too fast or hit something sensitive.
- **Physical withdrawal.** Leaning back, arms crossing, turning toward the door, hand on the bag. The person is ready to leave, or wants the topic to end.
- **Tears or a shaking voice.** Stop the agenda. Silence, a tissue, and "take your time" do more than any sentence you could add.
- **Rising heat.** Louder, faster, sharper. Slow your own voice, lower your volume, give them room. Anger at the system is not anger at you — and if it becomes anger at you, your safety plan takes priority over the visit.
- **Sudden brightness.** An abrupt "I'm fine, it's fine, all good" after a hard topic is often a door closing politely. Leave it open: "We can stop here. If it comes back up, I'm around."
- **A hesitation before a yes.** The pause matters more than the word.

Do not state your interpretation as fact. "You seem angry" invites an argument; "I might be reading this wrong — did I push too hard just then?" invites an answer. Read cues in context, too: eye contact, silence, and touch carry different meanings across cultures and for people with trauma histories or sensory differences. Check, do not conclude.

Then adjust visibly — slow down, drop the agenda item, switch to a smaller question, offer a break, or end early and come back. Ending a visit twenty minutes early because the client was done is good practice, not a failed visit.

## Practice

Work in threes: CHW, client, observer. The observer counts reflections, questions, and affirmations, and flags every righting-reflex moment.

1. **Reflection-only round.** Your partner describes something they feel two ways about (not a real personal crisis — use a scenario card). For three minutes you may use reflections only. No questions at all. Debrief on what the client heard.

2. **Ratio drill.** Same scenario, four minutes, aiming for at least two reflections per question. The observer tallies. Most people start at zero reflections and four questions; the tally is the lesson.

3. **Affirmation hunt.** Listen to a five-minute intake and write three specific affirmations based on things the client actually did. Deliver one. It must be so specific that it could not apply to anyone else in the room.

4. **Righting-reflex trap.** The client opens with a strongly stated bad plan ("I'm going to skip the specialist and just deal with it"). Get through three minutes without arguing, warning, or persuading. The observer raises a hand the moment you argue.

5. **Change-talk spotting.** In a five-minute role-play, write down every change-talk statement you hear and label it desire, ability, reasons, need, or commitment. Compare lists; you will disagree, and the disagreement is the discussion.

6. **Boundary reset.** Draw a card: cash offered for gas, a request for your personal number, a friend request, a request to co-sign something. Respond warmly and specifically, with a real alternative, in under thirty seconds. Then write the one-sentence version you would bring to supervision.

7. **Cue reading.** Run a visit in which the client has been secretly instructed to shut down at a specific moment. Note when you noticed, what you did next, and how the visit ended. Observers report the exact second the shift happened — most CHWs miss it by a minute at first, and close that gap with practice.
