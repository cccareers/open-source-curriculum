## Confidentiality is a habit across every channel

Almost no confidentiality breach in CHW work happens because someone decided to break a rule. They happen in hallways, in cars, on phones, and in the two seconds it takes to hit send. The skill is not knowing the policy. It is having habits that hold when you are tired.

Run through the channels deliberately.

**Spoken.** No client details in a hallway, elevator, waiting room, break room, or parking lot — including to a colleague who is not on that client's care team, because "just venting" is still disclosure. In the field, a home visit conversation carries through a wall, and a neighbor who greets you by name has just learned something about the person whose door you knocked on.

**Paper.** Forms face down, bags closed, nothing left in a car, nothing carried home. Shred rather than bin, and never bring one client's paperwork into another client's home.

**Digital.** Screens locked and angled away. Client information only in the program's system, never in personal email or a notes app. Text clients only if your program permits it, only what is needed, and never assume a phone belongs to one person — ask, "Is it okay to text this number, and does anyone else use it?"

**Social media.** Never post about clients, even with names removed. "Had the most amazing visit today with a grandma in the east side apartments" is identifiable to anyone who knows the neighborhood. No friend requests, and no photos of clients, homes, or documents without explicit written consent — none at all on a personal device.

**Households — theirs and yours.** A spouse, adult child, or friend does not automatically get information; ask the client who may be told what, and write it down. Interpreters, drivers, students, and volunteers are bound by the same rules, and say so out loud when they join a visit. On your own side, the most common informal breach is describing your day to a partner in a way that identifies someone.

Two things confidentiality is not. It is not secrecy from the client — they can generally see their own record, and you should write as though they will. And it is not absolute: the limits are set by law and program policy, and you state them up front rather than after a disclosure.

## Boundaries and the supervision conversation

Boundary problems are not moral failures; they are occupational hazards of a role built on closeness. The professional skill is noticing drift early and bringing it to supervision, not handling it alone.

Signs of drift worth naming:

- You are contacting a client outside work hours or on your personal phone.
- You are doing things for this client you do not do for others.
- You think about this client at night, or you dread their name on the schedule.
- You have not documented something because it would be hard to explain.
- You are keeping a client's secret from your supervisor.
- You feel personally responsible for an outcome you do not control.
- The client's family has started treating you as a member of it.
- You have loaned money, run a personal errand, or given a ride outside policy.

That last item — undocumented and unmentioned — is the one that ends careers. The same act, brought to supervision the same week, is a conversation and a plan.

Bring it plainly: name the behavior, name the honest reason, and ask for a plan. "Mr. Barnes has been calling my work phone most evenings and I answered twice after hours because I felt bad — he's isolated and I'm the only person who calls him. I think I've let this drift." A supervisor can then do what you cannot do alone: script the reset, and refer the underlying need to something that actually meets it, like a friendly-caller program. Write both in the record.

Supervision is also where you take a request you are unsure about, a client who reminds you of your own family, a case that is affecting your sleep, and any situation where you cannot tell whether something is reportable. Using supervision is a competency, not an admission — and boundary drift and burnout feed each other, so the CHW who carries every case personally eventually either leaves or makes a serious error.

## Mandated reporting: recognize, then follow the process

CHWs are commonly designated mandated reporters. The exact categories, thresholds, timeframes, and agencies are set by state law and by your employer's policy, and they differ — you must be trained on your own jurisdiction's rules and have the reporting number written down before you are in the room. What is generic, and what you practice here, is the recognition and the process.

**What is generally reportable.** Suspected abuse or neglect of a child, an older adult, or a dependent adult — physical, sexual, emotional, neglect, abandonment, and financial exploitation. Threats of serious harm to self or others usually trigger a different, immediate response: crisis or emergency services, plus your supervisor.

**The threshold is suspicion, not proof.** You are not required to be sure, and you are not the investigator. Trying to verify first is a common and serious mistake — it delays the report and can compromise an investigation.

**Do not investigate, and do not promise secrecy.** If someone begins to disclose, do not interview them. Listen, do not ask leading questions, do not press for detail, and write down their words as close to verbatim as you can.

**Report yourself.** In most jurisdictions the duty is personal: telling your supervisor does not discharge it. Tell your supervisor as well — both, not either. Timeframes are short, typically an immediate phone report followed by a written one within a set number of days.

**Document what you observed and what you reported,** including the date, time, agency, and the reference number you are given. Good-faith reports generally carry protection from retaliation; failing to report when required generally carries penalties.

The hard part is rarely recognition. It is that you have spent six months building trust and you believe the report will destroy it. It may. You report anyway, and you handle the relationship honestly:

```text
CHW:    I want to tell you something myself rather than let you find out.
        What you described about the bruises — I'm required by law to
        report that. I've made the call. I'm not doing it because I think
        you're a bad parent. I'm doing it because I don't have a choice
        about this one, and I told you at the start that this was the one
        thing I'd have to do.
Client: So you're done with me.
CHW:    No. I'm still your CHW, I'll still be at Thursday's visit, and if
        someone comes out I'll help you understand what they're asking
        for. Do you want to know what usually happens next?
```

You do not have to be cold to be compliant. Tell the client when it is safe to do so — and when telling them would put a child or adult at further risk, tell your supervisor first and follow their guidance.

## Collecting program data accurately

The numbers your program reports to funders are assembled out of your data entry. Sloppy entry does not just make a chart wrong; it decides whether the program is funded next year and whether the community keeps the service.

Working standards:

- **Enter in the required system, on time.** A note in your own notebook does not exist to the program, and backfilling a week at a time produces guesses.
- **Use the definitions as written.** If "encounter" means a contact of five minutes or more with a consented client, a wave across the parking lot is not an encounter. When a definition is unclear, ask — do not invent a local interpretation only you use.
- **Complete every required field, and record unknown as unknown.** Empty is not the same as zero, and zero is not the same as not asked.
- **Never estimate or round up.** Inflated numbers are a form of fraud when they go to a funder, even when the intention is to protect the program.
- **Do not duplicate,** and double-check identifiers — a misspelled name creates a second client record and breaks every count built on it.
- **Match the record to the consent.** Data collected without consent should not be in the system.

You should also be able to say why the numbers exist. CHWs who know that the encounter count keeps two positions funded, and that outcome measures decide whether the model spreads, enter better data than those who treat entry as bureaucracy.

## Participating in evaluation

Evaluation is not something done to you. CHWs hold information no chart contains — why people miss appointments, which sites work, what a barrier looks like in practice — and evaluation is where that information becomes evidence.

You will be asked to take part in surveys of staff and clients (administered consistently, with client participation voluntary and explicitly separate from service); focus groups, where your job is candor because the sanitized version helps nobody; data review meetings, where you bring the field explanation for the pattern on the screen — a drop in follow-up completion may be a bus route change, not a motivation problem; quality improvement cycles, where the team tries a change and looks at what happened; and reporting findings back, in plain language, to the people whose data produced them.

Two ideas make these meetings legible. **Outputs** are what the program did: encounters, classes held, referrals made, people enrolled. **Outcomes** are what changed for people: blood pressure controlled, housing secured, appointments kept. Programs are good at counting outputs and are judged on outcomes, and the gap between the two is where most evaluation conversations live.

## Flagging data quality problems

When the data is wrong, you flag it. You do not ignore it and you do not quietly fix it yourself.

Flag blank required fields, duplicate client records, impossible values (a date of birth in the future, a visit logged for a day the site was closed), numbers that do not match what you saw in the field, a definition being applied differently by different staff, and a system that will not accept an accurate answer. Name the specific record or field, what you think is wrong, the correct value if you know it, and how you noticed — in writing, to whoever owns the data, so there is a trail.

What not to do: overwrite historical entries, delete records, fix another person's entry without telling them, or work around the problem with a private spreadsheet. Silent fixes destroy the audit trail and hide systemic problems that would otherwise be found and repaired.

```text
CHW:    Two things on last month's export. Twenty-two encounters have a
        blank primary-language field, and they're all from the Thursday
        site — I think the tablet form skips that question in landscape.
        And there are two records for the same client, one with the name
        spelled Nguyen and one Nyugen. I haven't changed anything. Do you
        want me to send the record numbers?
Analyst: Yes, send them, and don't merge those two — I'll do it so the
        history stays intact.
```

## Community-based participatory research, at awareness level

You will hear two terms in evaluation meetings, and you should know what they mean and where you fit.

**Community-based participatory research (CBPR)** is research done with a community rather than on it. Community members and organizations are partners across the whole process — deciding the question, designing the study, collecting and interpreting data, sharing results and credit. It exists because of a long history of research that extracted data from communities, published it elsewhere, and returned nothing. **Participatory action research (PAR)** shares those commitments and puts action at the center: the point of the inquiry is that the community uses the findings to change something, in repeating cycles of study and action.

Principles common to both: the community is a partner, not a subject; the work builds on community strengths; power and resources are shared; findings return in usable form; the research is expected to produce action, not just publication.

Where a CHW realistically fits: helping frame questions that matter locally, advising on the wording and cultural fit of instruments, recruiting and collecting data, sitting on a community advisory board, helping interpret findings ("that number is low because the site moved"), and presenting results back to the community — sometimes as co-author.

Out of scope without training and approval: designing a study, obtaining research consent, and any data collection for research purposes outside your program's approved process. Human-subjects research usually requires ethics review and a consent process separate from clinical or program consent, and participation must never be a condition of receiving services. Route any researcher who approaches you to your supervisor before agreeing to anything.

## Practice

1. **Channel sweep.** Given a written account of one CHW's day, identify every confidentiality exposure — spoken, paper, digital, social, household — and write the corrective habit for each. Then describe a difficult visit twice: once to a partner playing your spouse, once to a partner playing your supervisor. The observer flags any identifying detail in the first version.

2. **Drift inventory and reset.** Privately, against the eight signs above, note which you have felt in any helping role, and draft the three-sentence opener you would use in supervision (you share the sentences, not the case). Then role-play the after-hours-calls reset: warm, specific, with a real alternative that meets the underlying need — and write the case note documenting it.

3. **Reportable or not.** Sort ten scenario cards into report, do not report, and need supervision now, and defend the ones your group split on. Then look up your own jurisdiction's reporting number, timeframe, and written-report deadline, and put them where you will have them in the field.

4. **Disclosure handling.** Practice the first ninety seconds after a client begins to disclose: listen, do not interview, do not promise secrecy, record verbatim words. Then practice telling the client you are reporting — without coldness and without apologizing for the law.

5. **Data audit.** Given a sample export of twenty encounter records, find every quality problem — blanks, duplicates, impossible values, misapplied definitions — and write the flag message you would send, including what you did not change.

6. **Outputs versus outcomes.** Sort fifteen program measures into outputs and outcomes, then name the field data you personally contribute to measuring one of the outcomes.

7. **CBPR role sort.** For a proposed community study on transportation barriers, list which tasks a CHW could take on, which need supervisor approval, and which require research training and ethics review. Say where in the process results return to the community, and in what form.

## Check your understanding

1. A client tells you a neighbor's elderly father "looks like he hasn't eaten in days" and nobody checks on him. You are not sure it is neglect. What is the threshold, and what is your next step? *Reasonable suspicion, not proof. Do not investigate. Follow your jurisdiction's and program's reporting process, which typically means making the report yourself and telling your supervisor the same day.*
2. You notice 22 encounter records with a blank primary-language field, all from one site. You know the correct values for three of them. What do you do? *Flag it in writing to whoever owns the data, naming the records, what you think is wrong, the correct values you know, and how you noticed. Do not silently fix or overwrite entries.*
3. A university researcher asks you to hand out a survey to your clients next week. What do you do first? *Route the request to your supervisor before agreeing. Research data collection requires its own consent and ethics review, and participation can never be a condition of services.*
