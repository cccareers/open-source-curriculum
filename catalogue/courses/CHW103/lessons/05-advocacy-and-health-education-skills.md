## Speaking up for a client without burning the bridge

Advocacy in CHW practice is usually small and specific: a client was told they were ineligible when they are not, an appointment was cancelled without notice, an interpreter was never called, a pharmacy is refusing a refill that was approved. You are not filing a lawsuit. You are getting one person unstuck, in a building you will be back in next week.

Effective advocacy has a shape.

**Get consent and get the facts first.** Advocating without the client's permission — or on facts you have not checked — costs you credibility you cannot rebuild. Ask: "Would you like me to call them about this?" Then confirm dates, names, and what was actually said.

**Start with the person in front of you, calmly.** Most denials are errors, not decisions. A great many are fixed at the counter by someone who wants to help and needs a clear ask.

**Make one specific ask.** Not "this isn't right." Rather: "Can you check whether the referral from Dr. Okafor on the 12th is in the system? She was told it was sent."

**Bring evidence.** The confirmation number, the date, the letter, the name of who said what.

**Escalate on the ladder, not off it.** Front desk, then supervisor, then the program's grievance or member-services line, then your own supervisor. Naming the next rung is often enough: "I understand — who would I talk to about an exception?"

**Stay professional even when you are right.** The staff member across the counter is often underpaid, under-trained, and following a rule they did not write. Anger feels satisfying and reliably closes the door on the client who comes after yours.

```text
CHW:    Hi — I'm with the community health program, here with Ms. Alvarez.
        She got a letter saying her application was denied for missing
        income documents. She brought her pay stubs on the 3rd and got
        this receipt. Could we look at what's in the file?
Staff:  It says incomplete. Once it's denied she has to reapply.
CHW:    I hear you, and I want to make sure we're not restarting something
        that's already there. The receipt is dated the 3rd and the denial
        is dated the 5th — is it possible the documents hadn't been
        scanned in yet when the review ran?
Staff:  (checks) ...There is a batch from the 3rd not attached to a file.
CHW:    That might be them. Is there a way to reopen it rather than
        reapply, or is there someone who can make that call?
Staff:  I can send it to my supervisor with a note.
CHW:    Thank you — that helps a lot. What's a reasonable day for us to
        check back, and who do we ask for?
```

Note the ending: a named person and a date. An advocacy call without a follow-up plan is a hope, not a plan.

## Coaching self-advocacy

Speaking for a client is sometimes necessary and always second-best. The client will be in that building again without you. The higher-value skill is preparing them to speak for themselves — and knowing when not to.

Prepare, don't just reassure. Before an appointment or a benefits interview, do four things:

1. **Name the goal in one sentence.** "By the end of this visit, I want them to change the time I take the water pill." Written on a card, in the client's own words.
2. **Write down three questions.** More than three and none get asked.
3. **Rehearse the opening line.** The first sentence carries the visit; everything else can be improvised.
4. **Give them a repair phrase and a hold phrase.** "Can you explain that a different way?" and "Wait — before we move on, I still have one question." Practice saying them out loud, because they feel rude to people who have been trained not to interrupt professionals.

Then agree who does what in the room. This part is often skipped, and it is why coached clients still end up silent while the CHW does the talking.

```text
CHW:    So in there tomorrow — who talks?
Client: You know it better than me.
CHW:    I do know the words better. But she needs to hear about your
        nights from you; I wasn't there. How about this: you start, and if
        you get stuck, look at me and I'll pick it up.
Client: What if I forget everything?
CHW:    That's what the card is for. Read it off the card if you want —
        people do that all the time. What's your first sentence?
Client: "I need to talk about the pill that keeps me up."
CHW:    That's a strong opener. And afterward we'll go over what happened
        so we can get better at the next one.
```

Afterward, debrief specifically: what did you ask, what did they say, what got missed. Affirm the attempt regardless of the outcome — the skill is the point, and the outcome partly is not in the client's hands.

There are moments to step in and speak: when the client asks you to, when they are in acute distress, when they are being treated in a way that risks harm, or when a power imbalance makes self-advocacy unsafe. Step in, then hand the floor back as soon as you can.

## Documenting and reporting patterns

One client denied is a case. Ten clients denied by the same office for the same wrong reason is a pattern, and a pattern is information your program can act on — but only if it exists on paper.

What to record, each time:

- Date, site or program, and the specific barrier
- What the client was told, quoted where you can
- What you did and what happened
- The outcome and how long it took
- Whether it repeated

Write it factually. "Front desk said no interpreter available; visit proceeded with the client's adult son translating" is a usable record. "Staff were dismissive and rude" is an impression, and it will not survive being repeated to that agency's director.

Watch the confidentiality boundary. A pattern report is about the system, not about the individuals you served. Strip identifying details, or use case numbers, unless there is a signed release and a reason.

Then route it. Your supervisor first — that is nearly always the correct channel, and it is what supervision is for. Bring the pattern in a form a supervisor can carry into a meeting: what is happening, how many times, over what period, what it costs clients, what you tried, and what you would ask for. A one-page summary with five dated examples travels much further than a story told in passing at the end of a check-in.

Do not take a systemic complaint to an outside agency, a funder, a reporter, or social media on your own initiative. That is not caution for its own sake; it is how the program keeps the relationships that make the next client's referral work.

## Facilitating participatory education

A health education session is not a presentation with questions at the end. Participants who talk, decide, and try things remember what happened; participants who watch do not.

Design so that participation is the default:

- **Open with something everyone can answer.** "What's one thing you've heard about this that you're not sure is true?" Every hand can go up for that.
- **Keep any single stretch of you talking under ten minutes.** Then do something.
- **Use pairs.** "Turn to the person next to you and take one minute each." Pairs get everyone talking, including people who will never speak to the whole room.
- **Use the room's own expertise.** "Has anyone here dealt with this?" Someone in the room has managed the thing you are teaching, and their answer will be more persuasive than yours.
- **Use role-play and demonstration for anything with steps.** Reading a nutrition label is a doing skill; hand out labels.
- **Handle dominance and silence.** For the person who takes every question: "That's helpful — let's hear from someone who hasn't gone yet." For a silent room: pairs, or written cards, rather than more questions to the whole group.
- **Set ground rules in the first two minutes,** including confidentiality: what people share here stays here.

Two guardrails. Everything you teach is evidence-based content from your program's approved materials, delivered without your personal opinions layered on — and when a participant asks something clinical or personal ("should I stop taking my metformin?"), the answer is a warm referral, not an answer. "That's a real question for your provider, and I can help you get it asked."

## Adjusting in real time

Watch the room like you watch a client. The signals are the same and the response is faster.

| What you see | Likely meaning | Move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phones out, side conversations | Pace is wrong or relevance is unclear | Stop, ask what would be most useful, cut a section |
| Nodding but no questions at all | Politeness, not comprehension | Switch to a task: "In pairs, tell each other what you'd do Monday" |
| One person answers everything | Others have checked out | Go to pairs or written responses |
| Blank looks after a term | Vocabulary lost them | Define it in ordinary words, use a local example, move on |
| Sharp, repeated pushback | The content conflicts with lived experience | Take it seriously out loud, ask what they have seen, do not argue |
| Watching the clock, coats on | You are past the end | Cut to the one thing that matters and close |

Prepare so you can cut. Know before you start which single point must survive if you lose half your time, and which activity you will drop first. A facilitator with a plan for losing twenty minutes stays calm; one without it speeds up and loses the room entirely.

Say the adjustment out loud when it helps: "I can see this part isn't landing — let's do it differently." That is not weakness. It tells the room their response counts.

## Checking whether the session worked

Attendance is not learning. Ten people in chairs and a good feeling in the room tells you nothing about whether anyone can do something differently tomorrow.

Feasible checks, roughly in order of effort:

- **Three-question pre/post.** Same three questions before and after, on paper or by show of hands. Simple, comparable, and enough for most sessions.
- **Return demonstration.** For a skill, watch them do it. This is the strongest evidence available and it costs no extra time if you build it into the session.
- **One-minute exit card.** "One thing I'll do this week" and "one thing I'm still unsure about." The second half tells you what to fix next time.
- **Structured observation.** A colleague notes how many participants spoke, whether the activity ran as planned, and where attention dropped.
- **Follow-up contact.** A call two weeks later asking whether they did the thing. Expensive, and the only one that measures behavior rather than intent.

Write the objectives first, in observable language — "participants can name two signs that need a same-day call" — and the check almost designs itself. Then actually read what you collect, tell participants what you learned, and change one thing for the next session. Data you gather and never look at teaches participants that you were checking a box.

## Practice

1. **Advocacy call, three rounds.** Same scenario — a wrongly denied application — played by three different counter staff: helpful, rule-bound, and hostile. Each round is three minutes. You must end each with a specific ask, a named person, and a callback date. Observers note the first moment your tone slipped.

2. **Escalation map.** For one real or provided scenario, write the four rungs of escalation with the actual phone numbers and titles for your area, plus the sentence you would use to move up each rung.

3. **Pre-appointment coaching.** Prepare a partner for a benefits interview: goal card, three questions, opening line, repair and hold phrases, and an explicit who-talks agreement. Then run the interview with a third person as the caseworker, and debrief in five minutes.

4. **Fact versus impression.** Rewrite five judgmental incident notes provided by your instructor into factual pattern-log entries. Then assemble three of them into a one-page summary you could hand a supervisor.

5. **Ten-minute participatory teach.** Teach one small topic — reading a prescription label, what to bring to a first appointment, how to use a blood pressure cuff — in ten minutes. Requirements: no stretch of lecture over three minutes, at least one pair activity, and a written objective stated up front.

6. **Derailment drill.** Repeat drill 5 while the group is instructed to derail you: one dominant talker, one silent row, one person who asks a clinical question you must not answer, and one interruption that costs you five minutes. Name your adjustment out loud each time.

7. **Design the check.** For the session you just taught, write the three pre/post questions and the exit card. Run them with the group, score them, and state one specific change you will make next time based on what the answers showed.
