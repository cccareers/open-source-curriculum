## Running a needs assessment that people answer honestly

A needs assessment is a structured conversation, not a form-filling exercise. The tool your program uses — a social needs screener, an intake questionnaire, a guided set of questions — gives you coverage and comparability. Your skill decides whether the answers are true.

**Set it up before the first question.** Tell the client what this is, how long it takes, where the answers go, and that they can skip anything.

```text
CHW:    Before we start — this is a set of questions we ask everybody, and
        it takes about fifteen minutes. It's about things outside the
        clinic that make health harder: food, housing, getting around,
        bills, safety. The answers go in your program record, and I use
        them to figure out what I can actually help with. Anything you'd
        rather not answer, say pass and we move on. Nothing here changes
        whether you get services. Okay to start?
Client: What happens if I say something's bad?
CHW:    Then I tell you what we've got for it, and you decide what you
        want to do. I don't do anything without telling you first. There's
        one exception I have to be straight about, and it's about safety —
        if a child or an older adult is being hurt, I'm required to report
        that. I'd tell you before I did.
```

That last part is not optional and it belongs at the beginning, not after a disclosure. Naming the limits of confidentiality up front is how you keep trust when they matter.

**Read the questions as written, then follow up in your own words.** Validated tools depend on their wording; if you paraphrase the question, you have changed what it measures. But once the answer is given, you are free — "you said sometimes worried about food; tell me what a hard week looks like."

**Watch how you ask sensitive items.** Normalize first, use the plain-language version of the concept, and never editorialize on an answer. A raised eyebrow at an income figure will end the honest answers for the rest of the form.

**Ask about strengths, not only gaps.** "Who helps you out when things get tight?" belongs in every assessment. It surfaces assets you will build a plan on, and it changes the tone of the conversation from inventory-of-failures to problem-solving.

**Informal assessment counts too.** A lot of what you learn arrives sideways — the fridge you can see from the doorway, the eviction notice on the table, the client who declines every afternoon appointment. Informal observation is legitimate assessment data. It needs the same discipline as formal data when you write it down: record what you observed, not what you concluded.

## Triage: what has to happen today

Every assessment produces more needs than anyone can address at once. Triage is deciding what moves first and saying so out loud with the client.

Three buckets:

**Immediate — today or this week.** Safety, medical urgency, and needs where a short delay causes real harm: no food tonight, nowhere to sleep, no medication for a condition that will not wait, utility shutoff notice with a date, a client describing self-harm, violence in the home. These do not wait for a care plan meeting. They get an action and a warm handoff now, and they get escalated to your supervisor or the appropriate clinical or crisis service immediately when they exceed your role.

**Short-term — the next few weeks.** An expiring benefit, a missed specialist referral, a prescription about to run out, a recertification with a deadline. These get a date and a named owner.

**Long-term — the plan.** Housing, employment, chronic condition management, immigration matters, education. Real work, none of it solvable this month, and none of it a reason to lose the deadline-driven items.

Two mistakes are common. Treating everything as urgent burns you out and confuses the client about what actually matters. Treating nothing as urgent — logging a shutoff notice as a long-term utility issue — causes harm. When you are not sure, ask directly: "Of everything we've talked about, what's the thing that if it isn't handled this week, it gets worse?" Clients triage well when asked.

Then say the plan back:

```text
CHW:    Here's what I heard, and tell me if I've got the order wrong.
        Today: the electric bill has a shutoff date of the 12th, so that's
        first, and I'll help you call the assistance program before we
        finish this visit. This month: your daughter's insurance renewal
        is due the 30th. Longer: you want out of this apartment, and
        that's a real conversation but not a fast one. Does that match
        what matters to you?
Client: The apartment is what keeps me up at night.
CHW:    I believe you. We'll start it today too — I'll get you the waitlist
        information before you leave. I just don't want the lights going
        off while we work on it.
```

The client's priority is acknowledged and given a concrete first step, and the deadline-driven item still gets handled.

## Documenting findings clearly, accurately, and confidentially

Your notes are read by people who were not in the room, sometimes years later, sometimes in an audit, occasionally in a legal proceeding. Write accordingly.

**Facts and quotes, not conclusions.** "Client stated she has skipped the evening dose 4 of the last 7 days because of work hours" is a fact. "Client is non-compliant" is a judgment and a useless one. When you must record an impression, mark it — "CHW observed" or "appeared."

**Complete but relevant.** Record what affects the service: the need, what was done, what was agreed, the next step and its date. Leave out details that serve curiosity rather than care. A client's immigration status, sexual history, or family conflict goes in only when it is directly relevant to the plan, and you should be able to say why.

**Timely.** Same day is the standard in most programs. Notes written a week later are shorter, vaguer, and wrong more often.

**Client-readable.** Assume the client will read it, because in most systems they can. That standard alone removes most of the bad writing in case notes.

**Confidential in practice, not just policy.** Screens locked and angled away. No client names in personal texts or email. Paper forms in a closed bag, never in a car overnight or on a passenger seat. Nothing about a client on social media, ever, including "had a great visit today." No hallway or elevator conversations. Shared devices and shared printers deserve specific attention — check the tray.

**Corrections done properly.** Do not delete or overwrite. Add a dated correcting entry, or use your system's amendment function.

A workable note structure, adapted to whatever your program uses:

```text
Date/time, contact type (home visit / phone / text / field), duration
Purpose of contact
What the client reported (facts, quotes)
What the CHW observed
Needs identified, and triage level
Actions taken this contact
Client-agreed next steps, with dates and who owns each
Referrals made, with agency, date, and consent status
Follow-up date
```

## Outreach: describing the program so people can decide

Outreach happens where people already are — a laundromat, a church hall, a bus stop, a swap meet, a shelter dining room, a front porch. You have between twenty seconds and two minutes, and the person did not ask you to come.

What works:

**Say who you are and who you are with, immediately.** People are entitled to know who is approaching them and why. Badge visible, agency named.

**Lead with the concrete benefit, not the program name.** Nobody wants a "care coordination program." People want help getting an appointment, a lower bill, food, or someone to call the insurance company for them.

**Be honest about what it is not.** Overpromising at the door is the fastest way to make a community distrust the program. Say what it costs, what it requires, and what it cannot do.

**Give them a way to say no gracefully,** and accept it the first time. Pressure gets you one signup and loses you a street.

**Leave something behind.** A card with one sentence and a number, in the languages the community speaks.

```text
CHW:    Morning — I'm Rosa, I work with the county health program at the
        clinic on Third. We help people get appointments and deal with
        bills and paperwork. Free, and you don't need insurance.
Person: I don't have papers.
CHW:    You don't need any immigration documents for this, and I don't ask
        about that or write it down. Some of the programs I can connect
        you to do have rules, and I'll tell you straight which ones do
        before you spend time on them.
Person: What's the catch?
CHW:    Fair question. It's grant-funded, so we count how many people we
        see — that's a number, not your name. If you sign up, I ask for a
        name and a phone so I can call you back. You can give me a first
        name only.
Person: Maybe. Not today.
CHW:    No problem. Here's my card — that number reaches me directly,
        Monday to Thursday. If you'd rather not call, I'm at the food
        distribution here every other Thursday.
```

Nobody was pressured, the person's real concern was answered honestly, and the door was left open with a specific place and time.

Ethical lines to hold: no exaggerated eligibility, no implied consequence for declining, no collecting more personal information than the encounter requires, and no discussing one community member's situation with another. In someone's home or workplace you are a guest and the host's rules apply.

## Tracking outreach contacts accurately

Outreach data is the part of the job most often done badly, because it is done at the end of a long day from memory. It matters for two reasons: your program's funding depends on it, and follow-up depends on it — a person who said "call me next week" and never gets called learns that the program is like every other one.

Practices that hold up:

- **Log within the hour, not at the end of the week.** Use whatever your program provides on a phone or tablet; a small paper log transcribed the same day is fine, as long as the paper is kept secure and destroyed after transcription.
- **Distinguish the encounter types your program defines** — an anonymous conversation, a material handed over, a person who consented to follow-up, an enrollment. These count differently and mixing them corrupts every number built on them.
- **Record what you actually did, not what you meant to do.** Twelve conversations is twelve, not "about twenty."
- **Never invent or estimate identifiers.** A missing phone number is missing.
- **Note the location, date, time, and setting type** — that is how a program learns which sites work.
- **Record the follow-up commitment and its date,** because that is the promise you made.
- **Collect only what consent covers.** A person who agreed to a flyer did not agree to a record with their name in it.
- **Flag anything you are unsure of** rather than guessing; a note that says "name spelling unconfirmed" is worth more than a confident wrong spelling.

Keep field notes secure between the street and the system. A notebook with names in it, left in a car, is a confidentiality breach the same as an unlocked chart.

## Practice

1. **Assessment opener.** Deliver the setup — purpose, length, where answers go, right to skip, limits of confidentiality — in under ninety seconds, twice: once to a wary adult, once to someone in a hurry. The observer checks whether the reporting limit was stated before any question was asked.

2. **Full assessment run.** Conduct a fifteen-minute needs assessment using the tool your instructor provides. Read validated items verbatim; follow up in your own words. Include at least two strengths questions. The observer counts editorializing reactions.

3. **Triage sort.** Take a scenario with eight identified needs. Sort into immediate, short-term, and long-term, assign an owner and a date to each of the first two buckets, and defend your sort to a partner who has sorted it differently.

4. **Say-it-back.** From your triage sort, deliver the plan back to the client in under a minute, in plain language, and ask them to correct the order. Then adjust the plan on the spot when they do.

5. **Note rewrite.** Rewrite five judgment-laden case notes into factual, client-readable entries. Then write a complete note for drill 2 using the structure above, in under ten minutes.

6. **Confidentiality hunt.** Given a written scenario of a CHW's ordinary day, list every point at which client information was exposed — screens, papers, conversations, phones, printers, car — and the fix for each.

7. **Doorway pitch.** Deliver a sixty-second outreach introduction, then handle three objections: "what's the catch," "I don't have insurance," and "I'm not interested." You must accept the refusal on the first no and still leave a specific way back in.

8. **Log it.** Immediately after drill 7, and without discussing it, write the encounter log entries from memory: setting, date, time, encounter type, follow-up commitment, and what you are unsure about. Compare with the observer's record of what actually happened.
