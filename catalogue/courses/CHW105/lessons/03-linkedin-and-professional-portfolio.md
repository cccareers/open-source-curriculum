## What you are actually building

A resume claims. A portfolio proves. When you tell a hiring manager or a prospective field mentor that you can conduct a community needs assessment, that is a claim, and every other applicant is making it too. When you hand them a two-page assessment write-up you produced — with every client detail removed — the claim is settled in thirty seconds.

You are building two connected things in this lesson. The first is a LinkedIn profile, which is how people find you and how they check you before a phone call. The second is a portfolio: a small, curated set of work samples that show what you can do. They serve different purposes. The profile gets you in the room; the portfolio is what you bring into it.

Both are worth building now, before you start the internship, for a practical reason. Your field mentor and your placement site will look you up. A thin, half-finished profile makes you look like someone who has not started yet. A complete one makes you look like someone already working in the field.

## Artifacts, not coursework

The most common mistake in a student portfolio is a list of courses completed. Nobody hiring a CHW wants to read a syllabus. They want evidence of behavior: can you assess, teach, document, coordinate, and follow through?

So convert coursework into artifacts. Every substantial assignment you have completed in this pathway is a potential work sample if you clean it up and frame it. Strong candidates include:

- **A community needs assessment or asset map** — a written summary of a community's health needs and existing resources, with your method described.
- **An outreach plan** — the population, the goal, the channels, the message, the timeline, and how you would know it worked.
- **A health education handout or flyer you created** — ideally in plain language, at an appropriate reading level, and in more than one language if you can.
- **A client encounter write-up** — a fully de-identified example of how you document an interaction, including the referral you made and the follow-up plan.
- **A resource guide** for a specific need in your area — food, housing, immigration legal services, behavioral health — with current contact details.
- **A teach-back or motivational interviewing example** — a short script or reflection showing how you confirmed understanding or handled ambivalence.
- **A workshop or presentation** you delivered, with the outline and any materials.
- **Certificates and training records** — CPR, HIPAA, mandated reporter, CHW training completion, language certification.

Six to eight strong artifacts is a full portfolio. Ten weak ones is worse than four good ones.

## The rule that governs everything: no client-identifying information

This is not a style preference. Putting client-identifying information into a portfolio is a confidentiality breach, and it is the fastest way to end a career in this field before it starts. A portfolio circulates — you email it, you post it, you leave a copy on a table. Assume every artifact you include will be read by someone you did not choose.

De-identify before anything goes in the portfolio, and de-identify thoroughly:

- Remove names of clients and their family members. Do not substitute initials — initials plus a neighborhood plus a diagnosis is still identifying. Use a generic label instead.
- Remove dates of birth, exact dates of contact (keep the year at most, and remove even the year if it indicates an age over 89), ages over 89 and any exact age that would stand out, addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, medical record numbers, insurance and member ID numbers, and case or referral numbers.
- Remove or generalize anything that identifies by rarity: an uncommon diagnosis, an unusual occupation, a specific building or program site, a small clinic name paired with a specific date.
- Generalize geography. "A rural county in the Central Valley" is safe; a named neighborhood of 400 people paired with a health condition is not.
- Strip photos of clients entirely unless you have a written release, and even then, think hard about whether you need it.
- Check the file itself. Document properties and file names often carry a client name you forgot about. Screenshots often catch a name in a browser tab or a sidebar.

Then apply the last test: read the artifact as if you were the client's neighbor. If you could work out who this is, it is not de-identified yet.

When an artifact cannot be safely de-identified, rebuild it as a composite or a fictional scenario and label it as such. A clearly labeled sample assessment written on an invented case still demonstrates your documentation skill, and it demonstrates your judgment as well.

## Framing each artifact

An artifact dropped into a folder with no context makes the reader do work. Put a short header on each one — four or five lines is enough.

```text
Title: Diabetes outreach plan, uninsured adults
Context: Course project, CHW pathway, community clinic scenario
My role: Sole author
What I did: Defined the target population, chose three outreach
  channels, wrote the phone script, and set a two-week follow-up cadence
Result / what I would change: Script was too long for a first call;
  I would cut it to four questions
Confidentiality note: Scenario-based; no client information included
```

That last line is worth including on every artifact. It tells the reader you know the rule and you applied it deliberately.

## Your LinkedIn profile, section by section

**Photo.** A clear, friendly headshot against a plain background. A phone photo in good light is fine. You do not need a suit; you need to look like someone a nervous client would talk to.

**Headline.** This is the line that follows your name everywhere on the platform, and it is the highest-value real estate on the profile. Do not leave it as your current job title if that job is unrelated. Name the role and the value.

```text
Community Health Worker | Bilingual Spanish/English |
Care coordination, health education, and resource navigation
```

**About.** Three short paragraphs, written in first person. Who you serve and why, what you can do, and what you are looking for now. Say the concrete things: languages you speak, populations you have worked with, the settings you are targeting. Avoid abstractions like "passionate about helping people" — everyone writes that, and it says nothing.

**Experience.** Include the internship as soon as it starts, and include relevant non-health work honestly. Retail, food service, caregiving, and call center work all build transferable skills — de-escalation, high-volume communication, documentation, reliability. Describe them in those terms rather than in the vocabulary of the old industry.

**Education and licenses and certifications.** List the CHW pathway program and put every certificate in the certifications section, not buried in a description. Recruiters filter on these fields.

**Skills.** Choose skills that match how employers search: health education, care coordination, motivational interviewing, community outreach, case documentation, HIPAA compliance, medical interpretation, and each language you speak. Pin the three that matter most.

**Custom URL.** Edit your profile URL to your name. It looks intentional on a resume and it is easy to say out loud.

**Featured.** Attach two or three portfolio artifacts here — a flyer, a resource guide, a one-page assessment sample. This is where the profile and the portfolio meet.

## Being findable and being active

A complete profile is a starting point, not a finished project. Follow the organizations on your placement shortlist, follow the county health department and the local clinic association, and connect with the CHWs and supervisors you meet in informational conversations — always with a one-sentence note reminding them who you are.

You do not need to post frequently. A short post when you complete a certification, and a thoughtful comment now and then on something a local organization publishes, is enough to keep you visible. Keep everything you write on the platform inside the same confidentiality rule as your portfolio: no client details, no photos of clients, no identifiable stories from your placement, ever.

## Assembling and sharing the portfolio

Keep it simple and portable. A single well-organized PDF, in a fixed order, with a one-page contents list at the front, works everywhere and cannot be broken by a link. A shared cloud folder is acceptable if the sharing permissions are set deliberately — never leave it open to anyone with the link if it contains anything you would not publish. A simple personal site is nice but optional.

Name files so a stranger can read them, keep one master copy and one shareable copy, and update it every time you finish something worth showing. Bring a printed copy to your mentor meeting. Very few students do, and it is remembered.

## Practice

1. Inventory every assignment, project, flyer, script, and certificate you have produced in this pathway. Pick the six that best evidence distinct skills, and write one sentence per item naming the skill it proves.
2. Take one artifact that came from real client contact and de-identify it completely using the checklist in this lesson. Then have a classmate read it and try to identify anything about the person. Fix whatever they find.
3. Write the five-line framing header for three of your artifacts, including the confidentiality note.
4. Write your LinkedIn headline and your About section. Both must name at least one population you serve, one language you work in, and two concrete skills.
5. Assemble the portfolio as a single PDF with a contents page, and confirm no file name, document property, or screenshot contains a client name.
