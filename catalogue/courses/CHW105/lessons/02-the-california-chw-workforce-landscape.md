## One job title, five very different jobs

"Community health worker" is a single title stretched across a lot of different work. Two people can hold the same title in the same county and have almost nothing in common in their daily routine: one spends the day in exam-room hallways with a panel of patients, the other spends it on the phone with health plan members, and a third spends it at a food distribution in a church parking lot.

That variety is good news — it means there is probably a version of this job that fits you — but it makes the job search confusing. If you search postings by title alone you will get a scrambled list with no way to tell which roles you would enjoy or grow in. The fix is to search by *setting* first. Once you know what the work looks like inside each type of organization, a posting stops being a wall of text and becomes something you can quickly place, compare, and rank.

This lesson walks the five main settings that employ CHWs in California, then shows you how to research real organizations near you and evaluate them for an internship placement.

## Community clinics and federally qualified health centers

Federally qualified health centers, usually shortened to FQHCs, and other community clinics are the most common clinical home for CHWs. You sit inside a care team — providers, medical assistants, nurses, care managers, sometimes behavioral health — and you are often assigned a panel of patients with a specific need in common: uncontrolled diabetes, a missed cancer screening, a new pregnancy, frequent no-shows.

Daily work looks like calling patients to close gaps in care, delivering health education one-on-one or in small groups, connecting people to food, housing, transportation, or benefits, occasionally accompanying someone to an appointment, and documenting everything in the electronic health record. The rhythm is appointment-driven, and your supervisor is usually a nurse, care manager, or program coordinator.

Choose this setting if you want to be close to clinical care, learn medical vocabulary and workflows quickly, and see the same people repeatedly over months.

## Medi-Cal health plans

Health plans employ CHWs on care management and outreach teams. Instead of a clinic panel you get a member list, often generated by data: members with recent emergency department visits, members who have not seen a primary care provider, members enrolled in enhanced care management.

Much of this work happens by phone, with some field visits. You are coordinating: scheduling appointments, arranging non-emergency medical transportation, explaining benefits, finding a provider who speaks the member's language, and following up until something actually happens. Documentation lives in a care management platform, and your productivity is usually tracked — outreach attempts made, members reached, plans completed.

Choose this setting if you are strong on the phone, organized, persistent about follow-through, and interested in how systems and benefits work.

## Community-based organizations

Community-based organizations, or CBOs, are where the promotora and promotor tradition is strongest. These are nonprofits rooted in a specific community: an immigrant services agency, a Black maternal health collective, a harm reduction program, a farmworker health project, a refugee resettlement office.

The work is the most relational of the five. You run group education, do home visits, staff community events, distribute food or supplies, build trust with people who avoid institutions, and advocate for individuals inside systems that are hard to navigate. Most CBO positions are grant-funded, which means your scope is tied to what the grant promised, and it also means schedules can include evenings and weekends when the community is available.

Choose this setting if you share language, culture, or lived experience with a specific community and want that to be the center of your work rather than a side benefit.

## County public health departments

County public health departments hire CHWs for outreach, health education, and program support. The work is population-level: immunization and screening campaigns, communicable disease follow-up support, WIC and nutrition programs, home visiting programs for new parents, emergency preparedness outreach, and community engagement for county initiatives.

Public sector employment brings structure. Job classifications are defined, hiring goes through civil service processes and can be slow, and the roles tend to be stable once you are in. You will spend more time on program-level tasks — event logistics, materials, data collection — than a clinic CHW does.

Choose this setting if you are drawn to prevention and population health, and you want to see how programs are designed and funded rather than only delivered.

## Hospitals and health systems

Hospitals employ CHWs where the system is leaking: emergency department navigation, readmission reduction, discharge follow-up, and social needs screening at the bedside. You may meet someone once, at a moment of crisis, and your job is to make sure they leave with a plan and a real connection to something in the community.

The pace is fast and the environment is interdisciplinary — social workers, case managers, discharge planners, nurses. Shifts may be scheduled rather than standard business hours.

Choose this setting if you are steady under pressure, comfortable in high-acuity environments, and interested in the handoff between hospital and community.

## Why the demand is real

You should understand, in general terms, why CHW job postings in California have grown. California recognizes community health worker services as a covered Medi-Cal benefit, which means clinics, plans, and other qualifying employers can be reimbursed for certain preventive services a CHW provides when they are recommended by a licensed provider. You do not need the policy details for an internship search. What matters practically is this: employers now have a funding stream attached to the work, so CHW roles are increasingly budgeted positions rather than temporary grant lines, and employers care about documented training and supervision because reimbursement depends on it.

That is also why your planning documentation and your record of training matter more than they might in another field. Keep both clean.

## Researching real organizations

Turn the five settings into a real, local list.

- **Start geographically.** Decide the radius you can reliably commute for the length of the internship. Be honest — a placement you cannot get to is not a placement.
- **Work each setting type.** For clinics, look up the community clinic and health center association members in your region. For plans, identify the Medi-Cal managed care plans operating in your county. For CBOs, search by the population or issue you care about, and use a local resource directory or 211 listing. For public health, go straight to your county health department site. For hospitals, list the systems within your radius.
- **Read careers pages, not just job boards.** Search the careers page for "community health worker," "promotora," "health navigator," "patient navigator," "outreach worker," and "community health advocate." The title varies; the work does not.
- **Check whether the organization already employs CHWs.** An organization with existing CHWs has supervisors who know what to do with you. An organization hiring its first one may not.
- **Use LinkedIn as a research tool.** Look at the organization's page, then at people who hold CHW-adjacent titles there. What did their path look like? Who supervises them?
- **Ask for informational conversations.** Twenty minutes with someone doing the job tells you more than an hour of reading, and it is how most placements actually get found.

Keep your findings in one simple table with a row per organization and columns for setting type, population served, languages used, location and commute, whether they employ CHWs now, a contact name, and your notes.

## Evaluating a placement for fit

A shortlist is not a ranking. Score each candidate against four things.

**Population and language.** Will you work with people you can communicate with, and ideally people you want to serve long term? An internship where you cannot speak with clients directly limits what you can learn.

**Skills you will build.** Look at the actual tasks. Will you do assessments, education, care coordination, documentation? Or will you be asked to shadow, file, and staff a table? Shadowing is fine for a week and a waste for a semester.

**Supervision.** This is the one people underweight. Is there a named person who will supervise you, who has time, and who has supervised a student before? Good supervision turns an average site into a great placement, and its absence sinks a great site.

**Logistics and capacity.** Commute, schedule, and whether the organization can actually host you — onboarding, background check, TB clearance and immunization requirements, and a timeline that fits your program's deadlines. Ask about these early; they are the most common reason a placement falls through late.

Warning signs worth taking seriously: no one can describe what you would do day to day, the supervisor has never had an intern, the site cannot commit to a start date, or the conversation focuses entirely on unpaid help rather than your learning.

## Practice

1. Build a shortlist of six organizations within your commute radius, with at least one from each of the five settings described in this lesson. Record them in a table with columns for setting type, population served, languages used, commute time, whether they currently employ CHWs, and a contact name or page.
2. Pick your top two and write a short profile of each — roughly 150 words — describing what you believe a CHW does there on a typical day and what evidence you used to conclude that.
3. Draft an informational-interview request of no more than 120 words that you could send to a CHW or supervisor at one of those organizations. Say who you are, what you are studying, what you want to ask about, and how little of their time you need.
4. Write a one-paragraph fit statement for your leading choice that names the population, the skills you expect to build, and one logistical risk you will need to resolve.
