## An unevidenced control fails exactly like a missing one

Here is the sentence that makes this lesson worth three hours. In a period examination, a control that is perfectly implemented and cannot be evidenced produces the same outcome as a control that was never built: a finding.

That is not auditors being pedantic. The auditor's job is to form an opinion based on evidence, and "the engineer told me it was configured correctly all year" is not evidence — not because you are untrustworthy, but because an opinion built on assertions is worth nothing to the customer relying on it.

So the documentation you write is not administrative overhead attached to the real work. For a large part of the year it *is* the real work's only visible output. Three kinds of document do three different jobs, and mixing them is the most common documentation failure in the field.

## The document hierarchy

```text
POLICY      What we commit to, and why.      Audience: everyone, auditors,
            Stable. Changes rarely.          customers. Approved by leadership.

STANDARD    The specific parameters.         Audience: engineers, architects.
            Changes occasionally.            Owned by the technical function.

RUNBOOK /   How to actually do it.           Audience: whoever is on shift,
PROCEDURE   Changes whenever the system      possibly at 3am, possibly new.
            changes.

RECORD /    Proof it happened.               Audience: auditors, incident
EVIDENCE    Generated by doing the work.     reviewers, future you.
```

Each layer answers a different question, and each has a different lifetime. A policy that contains a command line is wrong, because the command will change and a leadership-approved document should not need reapproval when a flag changes. A runbook that contains a paragraph of principle is wrong for the opposite reason — nobody at 3am reads principle.

The relationship auditors look for is *traceability*: a policy statement points to a standard, the standard points to a procedure, and the procedure produces a record. Break any link and the chain fails. The very common broken chain is a policy that commits to quarterly access reviews with no procedure describing how one is done, so each review is improvised differently and the records are inconsistent.

## Writing a policy page

A policy is a commitment. It gets audited *against*, which means every sentence you write is a sentence you will be measured on. The discipline is therefore restraint: write what you can sustain, not what sounds impressive.

Required parts:

- **Identifier, owner, version, approval date, review cadence.** A policy with no owner and no date is not a policy.
- **Purpose.** One or two sentences.
- **Scope.** Which systems, which environments, which people, which data. Exclusions stated explicitly.
- **Policy statements.** The commitments, numbered so they can be cited. Present tense, specific, testable.
- **Roles and responsibilities.** Who does what.
- **Exceptions.** How one is requested, who may approve it, that it is time-bound and recorded.
- **Related documents.** The standards and procedures beneath it, and the requirements above it.

A worked example, deliberately short:

```text
POL-07  Access Control Policy
Owner: Head of Platform        Version: 3.1
Approved: 2026-02-10 by the Security Steering Group
Review cadence: annually, or on material change      Next review: 2027-02-10

1. PURPOSE
   To ensure access to systems processing customer data is limited to
   individuals with a current business need, and that access can be
   demonstrated as appropriate.

2. SCOPE
   All production cloud accounts and the systems within them, all human
   and machine identities holding access to them. Excludes the sandbox
   account, which holds no customer data (enforced by tag policy).

3. POLICY STATEMENTS
   3.1 Access is granted on the principle of least privilege: only the
       permissions required by the individual's current role.
   3.2 Human access is granted through named individual identities
       federated from the corporate directory. Shared credentials are
       not permitted.
   3.3 Permissions are assigned through role groups, not attached to
       individual identities.
   3.4 Multi-factor authentication is required for all human access to
       production.
   3.5 Access is reviewed at least quarterly by the system owner. The
       review covers human and machine identities and is recorded.
   3.6 Access is revoked within one business day of departure and within
       five business days of a role change.
   3.7 Privileged access is granted for a bounded period with a recorded
       reason. Standing privileged access requires a documented exception.
   3.8 Machine identities use platform-native workload identity where
       supported. Static credentials require a named owner, a documented
       rotation schedule, and storage in the approved secrets manager.

4. ROLES
   System owner — performs and signs the quarterly review.
   Platform team — implements and maintains access mechanisms.
   People team — notifies of joiners, movers, and leavers within one
   business day.

5. EXCEPTIONS
   Requested in writing to the Head of Platform, approved for a maximum
   of 90 days, recorded in the exceptions register with a compensating
   control and a review date.

6. RELATED
   STD-07a Cloud IAM Standard; RUN-12 Access Review Procedure;
   RUN-13 Joiner/Mover/Leaver Procedure; control mapping ACC-01..09.
```

Read statement 3.6 and notice what it commits you to. One business day for departures. If your actual process depends on a weekly HR export, you have just written a finding into your own policy. **The single most common self-inflicted audit failure is a policy that describes an aspiration.** Write five business days if that is what you can do, and improve the process before you improve the sentence.

Notice also that every statement is checkable, and that each maps to a control from lesson 04 and a procedure below it. That is the traceability chain being built deliberately.

## Writing a runbook an auditor can follow

A runbook has two audiences and both matter. The operator needs to execute it under pressure without judgement calls. The auditor needs to read it and believe the control operates consistently regardless of who is on shift.

That shared requirement produces one rule: **a runbook is written for a competent stranger.** Not for you. Not for the person who built the system. For a capable engineer who has never done this before and cannot ask anyone.

What that means concretely:

- **Preconditions first.** What access is needed, what must be true before starting, what to check.
- **Numbered, literal steps.** Exact commands or exact navigation. "Verify the configuration" is not a step; "run this command and confirm the output contains X" is.
- **Expected output for each step**, so the operator knows whether it worked. This is the most-skipped element and the most valuable.
- **Decision points made explicit**, with the criteria for each branch and who to escalate to if the criteria are unclear.
- **Failure branches.** What to do when a step does not produce the expected output.
- **What to record.** The runbook should say which artifact to capture at which step, with a naming convention. A runbook that produces its own evidence as a byproduct is worth far more than one that requires someone to remember afterwards.
- **Version, owner, last-tested date.** The last-tested date is what an auditor looks at first.

An excerpt:

```text
RUN-12  Quarterly Access Review Procedure
Owner: Head of Platform   Version 2.4   Last executed: 2026-07-03
Expected duration: 90 minutes   Required access: prod-read, directory read

PRECONDITIONS
  P1. The quarter has closed.
  P2. You hold prod-read in both production accounts. If not, request via
      RUN-13 before starting; do not use another person's session.
  P3. The evidence folder for the quarter exists:
      evidence/access-review/<YYYY>-Q<n>/

STEPS
  1. Export the identity inventory for each production account.

       aws iam generate-credential-report
       aws iam get-credential-report --query Content --output text \
         | base64 --decode > evidence/access-review/2026-Q2/prod-app-identities.csv
       aws iam get-credential-report --query GeneratedTime --output text \
         > evidence/access-review/2026-Q2/prod-app-identities.generated-time.txt

     Expected: a CSV with one row per IAM user, including mfa_active and
     access_key_1_last_rotated. The CSV itself carries no generation
     timestamp, so the second command records it. The service regenerates
     the report at most once every 4 hours; if the recorded generated time
     predates the quarter close, wait and re-run both commands.
     NOTE: federated humans (POL-07 3.2) are not IAM users and will not
     appear here. Their population comes from step 2 plus an export of the
     roles they assume; do not treat a short credential report as a
     complete inventory.
     RECORD: the CSV and its generated-time file, filenames as above.

  2. Export role group membership from the corporate directory.
     [navigation steps]
     Expected: one row per (identity, group) pair.
     RECORD: evidence/access-review/2026-Q2/group-membership.csv

  3. Export permission usage for the period.
     Expected: last-used timestamp per identity per service.
     RECORD: evidence/access-review/2026-Q2/permission-usage.csv

  4. Produce the review sheet by joining steps 1-3 on identity.
     Expected: one row per identity with role groups, last used, and an
     empty decision column.

  5. Send the review sheet to the system owner with a 5-business-day
     deadline. Do not decide on their behalf.
     DECISION POINT: if the system owner is unavailable for more than
     5 business days, escalate to the Head of Platform. Do not allow the
     review to lapse past quarter-end + 15 business days — POL-07 3.5.

  6. On return, verify every row has a decision. Rows with no decision
     are not complete; return the sheet.
     Expected: zero blank decisions.

  7. Raise a change ticket per revocation. Execute revocations.
     RECORD: ticket numbers in the review record.

  8. Re-export the identity inventory and confirm each revoked identity
     no longer holds the removed access.
     Expected: revoked identities absent from the relevant groups.
     RECORD: evidence/access-review/2026-Q2/post-revocation-identities.csv

  9. Complete the review record (template TPL-04), attach all exports,
     obtain the system owner's signature, and file in the evidence folder.

FAILURE BRANCHES
  F1. Step 1 fails with access denied → your prod-read has lapsed; see P2.
  F2. An identity appears in the cloud account with no directory match →
      stop and raise a security incident; an unfederated identity in
      production is out of policy (POL-07 3.2).
```

Failure branch F2 is the kind of thing that separates a real runbook from a checklist. It anticipates a genuinely possible outcome and tells a stranger what to do about it, rather than leaving them to improvise a security decision.

## Evidence as a distinct artifact

Evidence has its own rules, and they are not the rules for documentation generally. Four properties determine whether a piece of evidence will be accepted.

**Traceability.** Evidence must connect to a specific control statement. A folder of screenshots nobody can map to a control is not evidence, it is clutter. The connection is made by an evidence index — the artifact that turns a pile of files into a body of proof.

**Continuity over the period.** This is the property most often missing. For a point-in-time control, a dated export is fine. For an operational control, the auditor needs to believe it operated *throughout*. Configuration history, monitoring records that span the period, ticket trails with dates, and posture-tool compliance timelines all provide continuity. A single screenshot from last Tuesday does not.

**Tamper resistance and integrity.** Evidence generated by the system beats evidence generated by a person. A configuration export from an API is stronger than a screenshot of a console, which is stronger than a spreadsheet someone typed. Where you must use weaker forms, strengthen them with metadata: visible timestamps, visible account identifiers, unedited images. And never crop out the part that shows which environment you were in — an auditor cannot distinguish a production screenshot from a staging one if the identifying detail is gone.

**Completeness and sampling honesty.** If a control ran twelve times and you kept three records, say that, and say how the three were chosen. Silently presenting three as if it were all is the kind of thing that turns a routine finding into a question about the integrity of your whole submission.

Practical hygiene rules that prevent most rejected evidence:

- **Date at collection, in the filename**, in a sortable format: `2026-07-06-prod-security-groups.json`.
- **Prefer exports to screenshots.** Where a screenshot is unavoidable, capture the whole window including the clock and the account or subscription identifier.
- **Never edit an artifact after collection.** If redaction is genuinely required, keep the original in a controlled location, redact a copy, and note that you did.
- **Store evidence outside the system it describes.** Evidence for the production account should not live only in the production account.
- **Restrict who can modify the evidence store**, and prefer append-only or versioned storage.
- **Keep evidence for the retention your own policy states** — and check that this is compatible with any deletion obligations for personal data inside it. Evidence containing personal data is subject to the same rules as any other copy, which is a good reason to prefer evidence that does not contain any.

The index that ties it together:

```text
EVIDENCE INDEX — Q2 2026 — Access Control (POL-07 / controls ACC-01..09)

CONTROL  EVIDENCE                                    TYPE      PERIOD     COLLECTED   BY
ACC-04   2026-Q2-access-review-record.pdf            record    Q2         2026-07-04  S.Begum
ACC-04   2026-07-01-prod-identity-inventory.csv      export    point      2026-07-01  S.Begum
ACC-04   2026-07-04-post-revocation-identities.csv   export    point      2026-07-04  S.Begum
ACC-04   OPS-4471/4472/4473 (revocation tickets)     system    Q2         continuous  system
ACC-02   config-rule-mfa-enabled-history-Q2.json     history   Q2 cont.   2026-07-06  M.Osei
ACC-06   joiner-mover-leaver-tickets-Q2.csv          export    Q2         2026-07-06  M.Osei
ACC-07   privileged-elevation-log-Q2.json            system    Q2 cont.   2026-07-06  M.Osei
ACC-07   break-glass-alert-2026-05-22.png            screenshot point     2026-05-22  A.Reyes

SAMPLING NOTES
  ACC-06: 63 joiner/mover/leaver events in Q2; all exported, no sampling.
  ACC-07: elevation log is complete for the period; the single break-glass
          use is evidenced individually.
GAPS
  ACC-05: no evidence of the semi-annual firewall rule review. The review
          was not performed in the period. Raised as INT-2026-Q3-011.
```

That index does three things a folder cannot. It maps every artifact to a control. It distinguishes continuous evidence from point-in-time evidence, so the auditor can see at a glance which controls have period coverage. And it declares a gap rather than hoping nobody notices — which is both more honest and, practically, better, because a gap you found and logged reads very differently from one the auditor found.

## Documentation as a reusable asset

One more audience, easy to forget: your colleagues. The same discipline that makes a runbook auditable makes it usable, and the artifacts you build for compliance are the highest-value knowledge-base content most teams have. The access review procedure is also the answer to "how do I get production access". The DR design record is also the onboarding document for how the system fails.

Two habits make documentation reusable rather than write-only: **one canonical location** so there is never a question of which copy is current, and **a review date on every document**, because a document nobody has confirmed in two years is a document a new engineer cannot trust. Stale documentation is worse than none — it is followed.

## Practice

Use the environment and findings from lessons 04 through 09.

**Exercise 1 — Write a policy page (the main artifact, part one).**

Write a complete policy page for **one** of: data protection, backup and recovery, or change management. Use the structure in this lesson, with all seven required parts. Between six and ten numbered policy statements. Every statement must be one your organization could actually sustain — after writing them, go back through and for each one write, in a margin note, the evidence that would prove it operated for a quarter. Any statement whose evidence you cannot name is a statement to rewrite or delete.

**Exercise 2 — Write a runbook (the main artifact, part two).**

Write the procedure that implements one statement from your policy. It must have preconditions, numbered literal steps with expected output for each, at least one explicit decision point with escalation criteria, at least two failure branches, and a `RECORD:` line naming the evidence artifact and its filename convention at every step that produces one. Include the version, owner, and last-executed fields.

**Exercise 3 — Build the evidence index.**

For the control your policy statement and runbook cover, build an evidence index in the format above for one quarter. It must include at least one continuous artifact and one point-in-time artifact, a sampling note, and — this is required — at least one honestly declared gap with a finding reference.

**Exercise 4 — Fix bad evidence.**

For each of the following, say why it would be rejected and what you would submit instead:

1. A screenshot of the IAM console showing MFA enabled, cropped to the table, submitted for a twelve-month period control.
2. A spreadsheet titled `access review final FINAL v3.xlsx` with no dates and no reviewer name.
3. A configuration export with a filename of `export.json`, collected at an unknown time.
4. Twelve monthly patch reports, of which four are missing, submitted without comment.
5. A screenshot of a green compliance dashboard, submitted as evidence that firewall rules were reviewed.

**Exercise 5 — The stranger test.**

Hand your runbook to a classmate who has never seen your environment. They must attempt to follow it literally, doing exactly what is written and nothing else, and stop at the first step they cannot complete without asking a question. Record where they stopped. Then fix that step and repeat. A runbook that survives two different strangers without a question is one an auditor will accept.
