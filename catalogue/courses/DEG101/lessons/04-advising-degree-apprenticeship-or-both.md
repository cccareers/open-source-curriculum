## The advising job

By now you can explain how credit works and what California's models look like. This lesson is about the conversation itself: helping a participant compare three paths side by side so they can make their own decision.

| Path | In one sentence |
| --- | --- |
| Traditional degree | The participant enrolls as a student, pays or finances tuition, and works around school or not at all |
| Apprenticeship | The participant is hired, earns wages that rise with skill, completes related instruction, and finishes with a journey-level credential |
| Apprenticeship degree | The participant is hired into an apprenticeship that is built to produce college credit or a degree along the way |

Your role is to make the trade-offs visible. It is not to steer the participant toward the option that is easiest to fund or best for your numbers.

## The three comparison factors

### Cost

Look at the full cost, not only tuition.

- **Direct costs:** tuition, fees, books, tools, and required exams or licenses.
- **Who pays:** the participant, the employer or sponsor, financial aid, or WIOA. Lesson 5 covers WIOA.
- **Lost earnings:** if the participant reduces work hours to attend school, the wages they give up are a real cost. This is often the largest cost and the one people forget.
- **Debt:** any loans the participant would take on.

### Time

- Time to the **first job** in the target occupation.
- Time to the **degree**.
- Time to the **credential or license**, if one is required.
- **Pace control:** a traditional student can often speed up or slow down. An apprentice's pace is tied to the job and the program schedule.

### Earnings

- **Earnings during the program:** none or part-time for many students; apprentice wages for apprentices.
- **Wage progression:** apprenticeships usually include scheduled wage increases as skills are gained. Get the actual schedule from the program standards.
- **Earnings after completion:** use current local labor market information for the target occupation, not national averages or anecdotes.

## Build a comparison sheet

A comparison sheet turns the conversation into something the participant can take home. Use a template like this and fill every cell from a named source.

| Factor | Traditional degree | Apprenticeship | Apprenticeship degree |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Direct cost to participant | [from college cost estimate] | [from sponsor] | [from sponsor and college] |
| Wages during program | [participant's realistic plan] | [from wage schedule] | [from wage schedule] |
| Time to first target job | [estimate] | [hired at start] | [hired at start] |
| Time to degree | [college program map] | [no degree unless credit route exists] | [program map] |
| Credential or license | [requirements] | [completion credential] | [both, if built in] |
| Expected wage after completion | [local LMI] | [local LMI or journey wage] | [local LMI] |
| Key risks | [debt, lost wages] | [no degree; credit may not count] | [new program; details may change] |

Mark anything you could not confirm as "estimate" or "unverified." A sheet that looks precise but rests on guesses does more harm than a rough sheet that is honest.

## Worked example: Danielle's three paths

Here is a summary note from your second meeting with Danielle. The details are fictional.

```text
Reviewed comparison sheet with participant.
Traditional degree: would need to cut work to part-time to attend full-time;
participant says she cannot afford the lost wages. Time to credential longest.
Apprenticeship only: no teacher apprenticeship without a degree path; not a fit
for her goal.
Apprenticeship degree: keeps her employed as a paraeducator; associate portion
confirmed at Riverbend CC; bachelor's partner not yet finalized; wage schedule
requested from sponsor.
Participant's choice: apply to the teacher apprenticeship; keep a traditional
transfer plan as backup if the bachelor's portion is not finalized.
Next steps: participant to request CPL review; staff to confirm wage schedule
and cost to participant by [date].
```

Notice three things. The participant made the choice. The note records the risk (an unfinished partnership) and a backup plan. And the staff next steps are about verifying facts, not about selling the option.

## Running the conversation

A simple flow that works in about 30 minutes:

1. **Goal first.** "Where do you want to be in three to five years?" A degree is a means; find out what the end is.
2. **Constraints.** "How many hours can you work, and how much can you go without?" This often decides the question.
3. **Walk the sheet.** One column at a time, in the participant's words.
4. **Name the unknowns.** Say plainly what is not yet confirmed.
5. **Let them choose.** Then record the choice and the reasons in their own words.

Watch for these red flags in your own advising:

- Presenting the apprenticeship degree as the "best" option before looking at the participant's constraints.
- Quoting wages or tuition from memory.
- Glossing over the risk of a new or unfinished program.
- Forgetting that an apprenticeship depends on keeping the job.

## Practice

**Role play (20 minutes, groups of three: advisor, participant, observer):**

Case: Marcus, 34, is a fictional warehouse lead with two children. He wants to move into IT and has heard about an IT apprenticeship that includes credit at a local college, and about a full-time degree program.

1. The advisor runs the five-step conversation and fills in the comparison sheet template with [placeholder] values, marking each as confirmed or unverified.
2. The observer notes any moment the advisor steered, quoted a number without a source, or skipped a risk.
3. Rotate roles and repeat with Danielle's case.
4. Each advisor writes a five-line case note recording the participant's choice and the reasons.

**Discussion prompt:** When is a traditional degree clearly the better choice for a participant, even when an apprenticeship degree exists?
