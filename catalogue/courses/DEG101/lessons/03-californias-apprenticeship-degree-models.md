## Start with a caution

California's apprenticeship-degree landscape is early. Programs are being designed, piloted, and renamed as you read this. That makes this lesson different from most compliance training: the most important skill is not memorizing a list of programs, it is knowing what is confirmed, what is still emerging, and how to check before you refer someone.

This course treats two things as the current anchors, as named in CCC's course catalog:

- **AB 1904**, which addresses apprenticeships for credentialed educators
- **The California Community Colleges Chancellor's Office Apprenticeship Pathway Demonstration Project**

The catalog also notes that a California State University or Apprenticeship Degree Network footprint in California was **not verified**. Do not tell participants that a CSU apprenticeship degree exists in your region unless you have confirmed a specific program yourself.

## The model types you will see

Whatever the name on the brochure, most California apprenticeship-degree arrangements fall into one of these patterns. They map to the three credit routes from Lesson 2.

| Model pattern | What it looks like | Credit route |
| --- | --- | --- |
| College as related instruction provider | A registered program uses community college courses as its related instruction | Credit for related instruction |
| CPL-linked apprenticeship | A college awards credit for a completed apprenticeship or journey-level credential, ideally through a crosswalk | Credit for prior learning |
| Degree-embedded apprenticeship | Apprenticeship and degree are designed together; finishing one means finishing, or nearly finishing, the other | Degree-embedded |

California community colleges and adult schools have long served as related instruction providers for registered programs, so the first pattern is the most familiar. The newer work, and the reason "apprenticeship degree" has become a phrase, is moving programs toward the third pattern.

## Educator apprenticeships under AB 1904

Teacher and educator apprenticeships are California's most visible current example, and the catalog identifies AB 1904 as the anchor. At the level you need for advising:

- The apprentice is employed by an education employer, often in a role like paraeducator, while working toward a degree and a credential.
- The apprenticeship is a pathway, not a shortcut. A teaching credential is still issued by the state's credentialing authority and still has its own requirements, which the apprenticeship must help the apprentice meet.
- Programs involve several partners, such as a school district or county office, a college or university, and a sponsor.

What you should **not** do is describe the bill's specific provisions, funding amounts, or eligibility rules from memory. Before you advise on an educator apprenticeship, read the current text and any implementing guidance, and get the program's own requirements in writing from the sponsor or education partner.

## The Chancellor's Office Apprenticeship Pathway Demonstration Project

The catalog names this project as the second anchor. Treat it as what the name says: a demonstration, meaning the Chancellor's Office is testing ways community colleges can build apprenticeship pathways. For advising purposes:

- Ask your local college whether it participates and, if so, which occupations and which degrees are involved.
- Expect details to change as a demonstration matures. Re-check before each new referral cycle.
- Do not assume every community college offers this; participation is specific to the colleges involved.

## Requirements: what to confirm for any program

Because the field is new, you confirm requirements program by program. Use this checklist every time.

| Requirement to confirm | Why it matters | Where to look |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is the apprenticeship registered, and with whom? | An unregistered "apprenticeship" may not lead to a recognized completion credential | DAS or the sponsor's registration documents |
| Who is the employer, and must the participant be hired first? | Apprentices are employees; many programs only accept people the employer has hired | Sponsor and employer |
| Which college awards credit, for which degree? | Credit belongs to a specific program at a specific institution | College program map or articulation agreement |
| Which credit route applies? | Determines whether credit is guaranteed or case by case | College CPL policy, course catalog |
| What academic entry requirements apply? | Some degree programs have prerequisites or placement steps | College admissions and counseling |
| Is there an occupational license or credential at the end? | For teaching, the credential has its own requirements | The licensing or credentialing authority |
| What does the participant pay? | Tuition, fees, books, and who covers them vary | Sponsor, college financial aid |

Write down the source and the date for every answer. "Program contact said so in a phone call" is a weak source; a program map or agreement is a strong one.

## Worked example: Danielle's program sheet

You build a one-page sheet for Danielle's fictional teacher apprenticeship. Every line has a source.

```text
Program: [District name] Teacher Apprenticeship (fictional)
Registered with: DAS (confirmed on sponsor's registration letter, [date])
Employer: [District name]; participant must be employed as a paraeducator
Associate degree: Riverbend CC, via credit-bearing related instruction
Bachelor's and credential: university partner not yet finalized (sponsor, [date])
CPL for prior paraeducator experience: case by case, Riverbend CPL policy
Still to confirm: credential requirements, cost to participant, start date
```

The "still to confirm" line is the most important line on the sheet. It keeps you and Danielle honest about what is known.

## Practice

**Activity (15 to 20 minutes):**

1. Pick one apprenticeship-linked program you have heard of in your region. Using the requirements checklist, fill in as many rows as you can, with a source and date for each.
2. Mark every row you could not confirm as "unverified."
3. Write two sentences you could say to a participant about this program that are fully supported by what you confirmed, and nothing more.

**Discussion prompt:** A colleague tells a participant, "The CSU has an apprenticeship degree for that." What do you ask your colleague, and what would you need to see before repeating the claim?
