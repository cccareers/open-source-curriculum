## Meet Danielle

Danielle Ortiz is 27. For four years she has worked as a paraeducator at a fictional elementary school in the Valley Regional Workforce Board's area. She has some community college units from years ago and no degree. She tells you: "I want to be a teacher, but I can't stop working to go back to school." She has heard the phrase "apprenticeship degree" and wants to know if her work hours could count toward college.

The honest answer is "sometimes, and it depends on how the program is built." This lesson gives you the vocabulary and the logic to explain why.

## Two kinds of learning in every apprenticeship

You know from APP 101 that a Registered Apprenticeship combines two parts:

| Part | What it is | Who usually provides it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| On-the-job learning (OJL) | Supervised work, organized around the program's work processes | The employer, under a mentor or journey-level worker |
| Related instruction | Classroom or online learning tied to the occupation (California's Division of Apprenticeship Standards uses the term related and supplemental instruction) | A college, adult school, sponsor, or other instruction provider |

College credit can come from either part, but it gets there by different routes.

## The key idea: credit follows learning, not hours

The most common misunderstanding is that apprenticeship hours convert to credit at a fixed rate, like "every 1,000 hours equals a semester." They do not. A college awards credit when it is satisfied that a student has met the learning outcomes of a specific course. Hours are evidence that learning probably happened. The college decides whether the evidence is enough and which course it matches.

That means two things for your advising:

- **The college is the decision-maker.** Not the sponsor, not the employer, and not you. Each college follows its own academic policies.
- **Credit is matched to courses in a specific program.** Credit that counts toward one degree may count only as an elective, or not at all, in another.

## Three routes from apprenticeship to credit

The course catalog names three models. Here is how each one works in plain terms.

### 1. Credit for related instruction

The related instruction is itself delivered as college courses. The apprentice enrolls at the college, takes credit-bearing courses that the program uses as its related instruction, and earns credit on a transcript the same way any student would. This is the most direct route because no conversion is needed; the credit exists from day one.

What to ask: Are the related instruction courses credit-bearing or noncredit? If noncredit, does the college have a way to award credit for them later?

### 2. Credit for prior learning (CPL)

The apprentice has already learned something on the job or in instruction that was not college credit. The college evaluates that learning and, if it meets a course's outcomes, awards credit. Common methods colleges use include:

- Reviewing an industry credential or journey-level certificate against a course
- A credit-by-exam or skills demonstration
- A portfolio of work samples
- A crosswalk the college has already built for a specific apprenticeship program

California Community Colleges have a credit for prior learning framework, and each college sets its own CPL policy and procedures. Check the specific college's CPL policy and ask whether a crosswalk already exists for the apprenticeship your participant is in. A pre-built crosswalk is far more reliable than an individual review.

### 3. Degree-embedded apprenticeship

The program is designed from the start so that completing the apprenticeship and completing a degree happen together. The work processes and the course outcomes are aligned in advance, the related instruction is the degree coursework, and the apprentice finishes with both a completion certificate and a degree, or close to it. This is the strongest model for the participant, and the rarest, because it takes deliberate partnership work between a sponsor and a college.

## Worked example: sorting Danielle's options

Suppose Danielle's district is exploring a teacher apprenticeship with a fictional partner, Riverbend Community College. You ask the program contact three questions and record the answers:

| Question | Answer from program contact | Which route? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Will her related instruction be credit courses at Riverbend? | Yes, the general education and education courses count | Credit for related instruction |
| Can her four years as a paraeducator earn credit? | Possibly, through Riverbend's CPL process; no crosswalk exists yet | Credit for prior learning, case by case |
| Is the program built so she finishes with a degree? | The first phase leads to an associate degree; the bachelor's portion is with a university partner still being finalized | Partly degree-embedded |

Your case note might read:

```text
Discussed apprenticeship-degree options with participant. Per program contact at
Riverbend CC: related instruction is credit-bearing; CPL for prior paraeducator
experience is case by case under the college's CPL policy (no crosswalk yet);
bachelor's partner not finalized. Advised participant to request a CPL review and
to get the degree map in writing before relying on it.
```

Notice the note does not promise credit. It records what the college said and what still needs confirming.

## Common pitfalls

- Telling a participant "your hours will count" before the college confirms it.
- Assuming credit earned at one college transfers the same way to another college or a university.
- Confusing a completion certificate from the apprenticeship with an academic credential. They are different documents from different authorities.
- Forgetting that CPL credit may appear on a transcript differently from regular coursework. Ask how the college records it.
