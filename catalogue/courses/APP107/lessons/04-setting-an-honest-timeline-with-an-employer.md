## The promise that breaks partnerships

An employer who hears "a few weeks" and waits half a year does not come back. The catalog for this course notes that intermediary studies cite registration timelines of roughly six months for new programs. Treat that as a planning anchor, not a guarantee: some programs move faster, and some take longer.

Your job is to give an estimate the employer can plan hiring around, and to explain what makes it longer or shorter.

## What drives the timeline

Break the estimate into phases. Each phase has its own drivers.

| Phase | What drives the duration | Who controls it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Preparation | How ready the employer is: job descriptions, wage plan, RSI partner, a decision-maker with time | Employer |
| Drafting standards | Complexity of the occupation; whether a template or similar program exists | Employer, DAS consultant, intermediary |
| DAS review | Review periods set in regulation (see 8 CCR 206), plus revisions requested | DAS, sponsor |
| Advisory body path | Which body has jurisdiction and how review proceeds | DAS |
| First apprentice registered | Hiring, DAS 1 completion and submission | Employer, sponsor |

The fastest phase to shorten is usually the first one, and it is the one the employer controls. An employer who arrives with a wage progression and an RSI partner already lined up saves weeks.

Do not quote exact regulatory review periods unless you have just checked the current text of 8 CCR 206 or confirmed with DAS.

## Ask before you estimate

Before giving any number, ask:

1. Is there an existing program the employer could join? (If yes, the timeline changes completely; see Lesson 5.)
2. Has the employer drafted job descriptions and a wage progression?
3. Do they have an RSI provider in mind?
4. Who at the company can approve the standards, and how fast do they respond?
5. When do they need apprentices working?

## How to say it

Give a range, name the drivers, and say what the employer can do to move faster. A sample script:

> "For a brand-new program, plan on several months. Studies of intermediaries have seen around six months as typical. The biggest variable is how quickly we can get your standards drafted, so having your wage plan and training partner ready shortens it. If an existing program fits your occupation, your first apprentices could be registered much sooner, because the program is already approved. I'll confirm the current review periods with DAS and follow up in writing."

What makes this work:

- A range, not a date.
- The source of the number is named, not presented as a promise.
- The employer hears what they control.
- There is a written follow-up.

## Bridging the gap

A realistic timeline does not mean the employer waits with nothing. While registration proceeds, you might explore pre-apprenticeship recruitment, plan an OJT contract for once apprentices are registered, or schedule early RSI enrollment. Confirm against your board's OJT and apprenticeship policies before offering any of these.

## Worked example

Priya at Valley Regional AJCC meets Coastal Fabrication, which wants machinist apprentices "by next month." No existing program fits their specialized occupation. Priya's follow-up email:

> "Thank you for meeting today. Based on what we discussed, registering a new program will likely take several months; recent studies cite about six months as typical. To move faster, we will need your wage progression and an RSI partner. I've introduced you to the DAS consultant. In the meantime, we can begin recruiting through our pre-apprenticeship partner so candidates are ready. I'll check in every two weeks."

## Discussion prompt

Think of a time an employer had an unrealistic expectation about a workforce program. What would you say differently using the script above?

## Practice

Write a three-to-five sentence timeline message for each employer. Include a range, at least one driver, one action the employer controls, and a follow-up commitment.

1. A hospital wants surgical technician apprentices, has a community college partner ready, and no existing program fits.
2. A general contractor wants carpentry apprentices in an area with an established joint program.
3. A small IT firm wants help desk apprentices but has no job descriptions or wage plan yet.

Trade messages with a partner and check: does any message sound like a promise?
