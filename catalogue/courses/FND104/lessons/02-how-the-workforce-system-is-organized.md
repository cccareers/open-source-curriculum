## Why the structure matters on day one

On your first morning, a participant asks you, "Who actually runs this place?" It is a fair question, and the honest answer is "several organizations at once." The building you work in, the funding that pays your salary, the rules you follow, and the computer system you log into may all come from different places.

You do not need to memorize the whole system this week. You do need a mental model of the layers, because almost every question you will face later ("Can I approve this?", "Whose policy is this?", "Who do I call?") depends on knowing which layer owns the answer. This lesson gives you that model. The next lesson zooms in on the specific organizations and what each one does day to day.

## Three layers: federal, state, local

The Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA) is the federal law behind most of the work in an AJCC. It sets up the system in three layers.

| Layer | Who | What they do for you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Federal | U.S. Department of Labor (and other federal agencies for partner programs) | Writes the regulations and federal guidance; sets performance measures; funds the states |
| State | California's state workforce board and the Employment Development Department (EDD) | Sets state policy, issues directives, passes funds to local areas, runs CalJOBS, monitors local areas |
| Local | Your Local Workforce Development Board and its local area | Sets local policy, selects the AJCC Operator and service providers, oversees the AJCCs in its area |

A simple way to remember it: **the law and regulations come down from the federal level, California interprets them in state directives, and your local board turns those into local policy that you follow.** When the three disagree, the higher layer wins, and the local policy can add detail or stricter requirements but cannot loosen a federal or state rule.

## The programs under one roof

WIOA covers several programs, organized by title. You will hear staff refer to them by number.

- **Title I** — the Adult, Dislocated Worker, and Youth programs. These are the programs most career services staff work in directly, and the ones this pathway focuses on.
- **Title II** — adult education and literacy.
- **Title III** — Wagner-Peyser employment services (labor exchange), administered in California through EDD.
- **Title IV** — vocational rehabilitation for people with disabilities.

Beyond these core programs, other partner programs (for example, programs serving veterans, older workers, or people receiving public assistance) are also expected to be part of the AJCC system. Which ones are physically present in your center is a local fact; you will look it up in the next lesson.

## What an AJCC is

"America's Job Center of California" is the state's name for the one-stop centers WIOA requires. The idea is that a job seeker or employer can walk into one place and reach many programs, instead of having to visit each agency separately.

Local areas usually have two kinds of sites:

- **Comprehensive AJCC** — a full-service center where the core programs are available, either in person or through a direct connection.
- **Affiliate or specialized sites** — smaller locations offering some services, often connected to a comprehensive center.

Ask your supervisor which kind of site you work in. It changes what you can offer a walk-in participant on the spot and what you need to refer.

## How the money and the rules flow

Follow one dollar and you will understand most of the system:

1. Congress funds WIOA through the Department of Labor.
2. The Department of Labor allocates Title I funds to California.
3. California passes most of those funds to local workforce areas, along with directives describing how they must be used.
4. Your local board decides how to spend its share, writes local policies, and contracts with organizations to run the AJCC and deliver services.
5. Those organizations hire staff like you, who serve participants and record that work in CalJOBS.
6. The data in CalJOBS flows back up to the state and federal levels as performance reporting.

That last step is why accurate records matter so much. Your case notes and data entry are not just paperwork; they are how the whole system shows it did what it was funded to do.

## A worked example

Jordan Reyes started this week as a career services specialist at the Riverbend AJCC, part of the fictional Valley Regional Workforce Board's local area. A participant, Maria, a laid-off medical billing clerk, asks whether she can get help paying for a coding certificate.

Jordan traces the question through the layers:

- **Federal:** WIOA allows training services for eligible Dislocated Workers.
- **State:** California directives describe how training is provided and documented.
- **Local:** Valley Regional's local policy sets the details Jordan must follow, such as approval steps and any local limits.
- **Center:** Jordan's employer, the career services provider, has its own procedures for how staff process the request.

Jordan does not need to answer Maria's question today. But Jordan now knows the answer lives in a local policy built on a state directive, and that a supervisor will be part of the approval.

1. Draw three boxes labeled Federal, State, and Local. Under each, write the name of the organization at that layer that affects your work. Leave a blank if you do not know yet and mark it "ask."
2. Write the name of your local workforce area and your local board.
3. Note whether your site is a comprehensive AJCC or an affiliate site, and how you found out.
4. List which WIOA titles have staff or services in your building. Mark any you are unsure of so you can confirm them in the next lesson.
