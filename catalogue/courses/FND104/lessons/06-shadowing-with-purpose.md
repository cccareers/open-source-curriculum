## Shadowing is not sitting nearby

Most new AJCC staff learn by shadowing, and most shadowing is unstructured: you sit beside someone experienced and hope you absorb what matters. Sometimes you do. Often you pick up habits without the reasons behind them, including shortcuts that are not actually allowed.

Structured shadowing fixes that. You go in knowing what to watch for, you write down what you see, and you ask about it afterward. The goal is not to copy one person's style. It is to see the process clearly enough that you could explain it, and to notice where a decision got handed to someone else.

## Before the session

Spend five minutes preparing:

- Ask your supervisor which kind of appointment you will observe (orientation, eligibility intake, assessment, plan development, follow-up).
- Re-read the local policy you traced in Lesson 4 if it applies to this appointment.
- Ask the staff member you are shadowing whether the participant has agreed to an observer. Participants can say no, and you should leave without fuss if they do.
- Bring your checklist, not a laptop full of participant records.

## The shadowing checklist

Use this during and right after each session. Write observations, not personal information about the participant.

| What to watch | What to write down |
| --- | --- |
| Opening | How the staff member explained who they are, what the program is, and how information will be used |
| Information gathered | What questions were asked, in what order, and why |
| Documents | Which documents were requested or reviewed, and how they were handled |
| CalJOBS | What was entered during the session versus afterward |
| Policy in action | Any moment a local policy or directive shaped what happened |
| Handoffs | Any decision the staff member said they would check with a supervisor or another partner |
| Closing | What the participant was told about next steps and timelines |
| Questions for later | Anything you did not understand |

The "Handoffs" row matters most. Each time an experienced colleague says "I'll need to check on that" or "my supervisor approves that," write it down. Those moments are the map of what you are not yet allowed to decide alone. The next lesson builds on that list.

## After the session

Ask for ten minutes with the staff member you shadowed. Good debrief questions:

- "Why did you ask about that before the other thing?"
- "Is what you did there a board policy, a provider procedure, or your own habit?"
- "What would have changed if the participant had said something different?"
- "Which part of this would you not let a new person do yet?"

That second question is the most useful one you can ask. It separates rules from personal style.

## Worked example

Jordan shadows Dana, a senior specialist, during an eligibility intake with a fictional participant, Luis, a former warehouse lead. Jordan's handoffs row reads:

```text
Handoffs observed:
- Dana said a missing document would need a supervisor decision on next steps
- Dana referred a supportive services question to the approval process in VRWB Policy 25-03
- Dana flagged a possible veteran status question for the on-site veterans representative
Debrief: Dana said the order of questions is provider procedure; the documents are board policy
```

Jordan now has three concrete examples of decisions that leave the specialist's hands.

## Practice

Complete one structured shadowing session and add it to your onboarding record.

1. Copy the checklist table and use it during one real appointment.
2. Fill in at least three rows with specific observations.
3. Record every handoff you notice, even small ones.
4. Hold a debrief using at least two of the questions above, and write down the answers.
5. Label each thing you learned as board policy, provider procedure, or personal habit.
