## The question behind every procedure

Sooner or later you will be told "that's just how we do it." Sometimes that is true. Often there is a written local policy behind it, and behind that policy, a state directive. Being able to find both is one of the most useful skills you can build in your first month, because it lets you answer questions from the source instead of from memory or hearsay.

## The document chain

Most rules you follow come from a chain of documents. Each link adds detail.

| Link | What it is | Who issues it | Where it usually lives |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Federal law and regulations | WIOA and its regulations in Title 20 of the Code of Federal Regulations | Congress; U.S. Department of Labor | Federal websites |
| Federal guidance | Training and Employment Guidance Letters (TEGLs) and similar notices | U.S. Department of Labor | Department of Labor website |
| State directives | Workforce Services Directives, numbered like WSD24-12 | EDD Workforce Services Division | EDD's directives page |
| State information notices | Workforce Services Information Notices (WSINs), which share information rather than set requirements | EDD | EDD's website |
| Local policies | Your board's written policies | Your Local Board | Board website or policy manual |
| Provider procedures | Desk guides, forms, and checklists | Your employer | Shared drive or intranet |

You will work mostly with the bottom three links. Your job is not to interpret federal regulations yourself; it is to know which local policy applies and to be able to see which directive it rests on.

## Reading a directive number

California directive numbers follow a pattern: **WSD** plus a two-digit year plus a sequence number. So WSD24-12 is a directive issued in the 2024 cycle. Two things to check every time you open one:

- **Is it current?** Directives are regularly replaced. A newer directive will say which older one it supersedes. If you are reading a directive someone printed for you years ago, confirm it has not been replaced.
- **What does it require locally?** Directives often say what local areas *must* do and what they *may* decide for themselves. The "may" parts are where your board's local policy comes in.

## Finding your local policies

Every local board organizes its policies differently. Try these places, in this order:

1. **Your board's website.** Look for a page called "Policies," "Local Policies," "Policy Bulletins," or "Directives." Some boards post them for the public; others post them only for providers.
2. **Your provider's shared drive or intranet.** Many providers keep a copy of current board policies alongside their own procedures.
3. **Your supervisor.** Ask which policies new staff are expected to read first, and how you will be told when a policy changes.

When you find a local policy, look near the top or in a "References" section. Most local policies name the state directive (and sometimes the federal regulation) they implement.

## Worked example: tracing one policy

Jordan, at the fictional Riverbend AJCC, wants to understand how supportive services are approved. Jordan:

1. Opens the Valley Regional Workforce Board policy page and finds a supportive services policy (fictional: "VRWB Policy 25-03, Supportive Services").
2. Reads the References section, which cites a state directive on supportive services and the relevant federal regulation.
3. Searches EDD's directives page for that directive number and confirms it is the current version, not superseded.
4. Compares the two. The state directive allows local boards to set their own limits; the local policy sets Valley Regional's limits and approval steps.
5. Writes a short note for the onboarding record.

Here is Jordan's note:

```text
Policy traced: VRWB Policy 25-03, Supportive Services (local)
Implements: [state directive number, from References section]
Directive status: current as of [date checked], per EDD directives page
What the state sets: allowable uses; local boards set their own limits
What our board sets: local limits, documentation, approval levels
Where I found it: board website > Policies page
Questions for supervisor: who approves requests above the staff level?
```

Notice that Jordan writes "per the directive" and "per the local policy" instead of copying numbers from memory. When you cite a rule, cite where it came from.

## Common traps

- **Using an old printout.** Always check the current version.
- **Treating a provider form as policy.** A form implements a policy; it is not the policy itself.
- **Assuming another area's policy applies.** A colleague from a neighboring local area may follow different local rules. Use your own board's policy.
- **Confusing information notices with directives.** An information notice shares news or resources; a directive sets requirements.

## Discussion prompt

Ask an experienced colleague: "Which local policy do you look up most often, and where do you keep it?" Compare their answer with what you found.

## Practice

Add a traced policy to your onboarding record.

1. Find your board's local policies. Write down exactly where they live and whether you needed special access.
2. Pick one local policy you will use in your first month (eligibility documentation, supportive services, or training are good choices).
3. Identify the state directive it cites. Find that directive on EDD's website and confirm whether it is current.
4. Using Jordan's note as a template, write what the state sets and what your board sets.
5. Write one question about the policy to bring to your supervisor.
