## Knowing your limits is a skill

New staff often worry that asking a supervisor makes them look unsure. In an AJCC, the opposite is true. Many decisions carry financial, legal, or safety consequences, and your local area has deliberately assigned them to someone with more authority. Escalating at the right moment is what a careful professional does.

In the last lesson you started a list of handoffs from shadowing. This lesson turns that list into a clear set of escalation rules you can use on your own.

## Decisions that commonly need a supervisor

Who approves what is set by your board's local policy and your provider's procedures. The categories below are common across local areas; your job is to find the local rule for each one.

| Category | Examples | Why it goes up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Eligibility judgment calls | Missing or unclear documents; a situation the documentation list does not clearly cover | Eligibility errors can make costs disallowed in an audit |
| Spending decisions | Supportive services, training enrollment, anything with an approval limit | Funds are limited and spending must follow local policy |
| Exceptions to policy | Any request to do something the written policy does not allow | Only designated staff can authorize exceptions, if at all |
| Complaints and grievances | A participant says they were treated unfairly or wants to file a complaint | Formal procedures and timelines apply |
| Confidentiality incidents | Information sent to the wrong person, lost documents, suspected misuse | Incident procedures must start right away |
| Safety and well-being | Threats, a participant in crisis, concerns about a child's or adult's safety | People's safety comes first; follow your center's emergency procedure |
| Media, legal, or official inquiries | A reporter, attorney, or outside agency asks about a participant | Information release has strict rules |

If something fits none of these categories but you feel unsure, that is reason enough to ask.

## A simple test

Before you act, ask yourself three questions:

1. **Is this written down as mine to decide?** If the local policy or your desk guide does not clearly give you the authority, escalate.
2. **Does it spend money or change eligibility?** If yes, check the approval level.
3. **Could it harm someone or expose information?** If yes, escalate now, not at the end of the day.

## How to escalate well

A good escalation saves your supervisor time. Bring:

- What happened, in two or three sentences.
- What the participant is asking for.
- What the relevant policy says, if you found it.
- What you recommend, if you have a view.

Then record the decision and who made it in your case notes, following your provider's procedure.

## Worked example

Maria, the laid-off medical billing clerk from Lesson 2, asks Jordan to pay for her certificate exam fee today because the testing window closes Friday. Jordan runs the test: the fee is a spending decision, and Jordan's desk guide does not give specialists approval authority. Jordan tells Maria, "I can't approve that myself, but I'll take it to my supervisor today and call you by tomorrow." Jordan brings the supervisor a short summary, the relevant local policy, and the deadline. The supervisor makes the call, and Jordan records the decision in CalJOBS.

## Practice

Finish your onboarding record with an escalation guide.

1. Copy the category table. For each row, find and write your local rule: who decides, and where that is written.
2. Add every handoff from your shadowing session to the correct row.
3. Write the name and contact method of who you escalate to when your supervisor is out.
4. Write a sample escalation summary for one of Jordan's or your own situations, using the four-part format above.
5. Review your finished onboarding record (roles map, traced policy, CalJOBS access steps, shadowing checklist, escalation guide) with your supervisor.
