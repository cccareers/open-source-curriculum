## What a first touch is actually for

Your first message is not asking for a job. It is asking for permission to have a short conversation. That is the whole job of a connection request, and almost every failed message fails because it tried to do more.

Read your first touch from the other side. A hiring manager at a regional employer opens LinkedIn between meetings and sees a request from someone they do not know. They give it about four seconds. In those four seconds they answer two questions: *does this person know who I am,* and *what are they going to cost me?* A message that proves the first and keeps the second small gets accepted. Everything else in this lesson is machinery for those two facts.

## The anatomy of a personalized request

Four parts, in this order, inside the character limit your channel gives you.

**1. The hook — proof you did the work.** One specific, checkable detail about *this* contact: a post they wrote, a talk they gave, their company's recent expansion, the fact that they moved from support into engineering. "I saw your company is growing" is not a hook, because it is true of everyone. The test: if the sentence would survive being pasted into a message to a different person, it is not a hook.

**2. The bridge — why that detail connects to you.** One sentence linking their context to your situation. This is where you earn relevance, and it is where most people either grovel or oversell. Do neither.

**3. The ask — small, specific, easy to say yes to.** Fifteen minutes. One question. A pointer to a resource. Never "any opportunities?" — that asks a stranger to do your research, and it is instantly declinable.

**4. The signature — who you are in one clause.** Your name and your program: a pre-apprentice in the CCC tech pathway. That clause does real work; it tells them you are training, not job-begging, and it is why employer-partner contacts recognize you.

Here is the shape, written for a connection request:

```text
Hi Dana — I read your post about moving your support team onto the
new ticketing stack; the part about training non-technical staff
stuck with me. I'm a pre-apprentice in the CCC tech pathway working
toward a support-engineering role, and I'd value 15 minutes on what
you look for in someone making that jump. Either way, glad to
connect. — Marcus
```

One practical catch: LinkedIn caps the note on a connection request at roughly 300 characters (the limit and the number of personalized notes free accounts can send have both changed over time, so check what your account shows). The example above is about 350, so it would be cut off. Trim words inside each part rather than dropping a part:

```text
Hi Dana — your post on moving support onto the new ticketing stack,
especially training non-technical staff, stuck with me. I'm a CCC tech
pre-apprentice aiming at support engineering; I'd value 15 min on what
you look for in someone making that jump. Glad to connect either way.
— Marcus
```

That version is under 300 characters and still has all four parts. Email and InMail give you more room, but the short version usually reads better there too.

Notice what is absent: no resume, no attachment, no paragraph about passion, no apology for reaching out. The message is short because short messages get read, and because a long message signals that the meeting will be long too.

Two failure modes to name out loud. **The template blast** — same message, hundred contacts, hook slot left generic. It produces a connect rate near zero and burns names you cannot get back. **The essay** — three paragraphs of your story before the ask. It reads as a request for a favor, not a conversation. If you find yourself explaining your career change in the first message, cut it; that story belongs in the conversation you are trying to earn.

## Follow-up sequences

Most replies you get this week will come from a follow-up, not a first touch. Non-reply is the normal case: people are busy, the message arrived at a bad moment, the notification got buried. Silence is not a no, and treating it as one is the single biggest source of wasted pipeline.

A workable sequence for a contact who does not respond:

- **Touch 1 — day 0.** The connection request or first email above.
- **Touch 2 — day 3.** Short, adds something new. Not "just following up" — reference a second detail, or send the one concrete thing you promised. Two to three sentences.
- **Touch 3 — day 8.** Change the ask. If touch 1 asked for fifteen minutes, touch 3 asks a single question they can answer in one line from their phone.
- **Touch 4 — day 15.** The close-out. Acknowledge they are busy, restate the door is open, ask nothing. This is the message that most often gets a reply, precisely because it releases the pressure.

After touch 4, move the contact to **Nurture** with a dated task four to six weeks out, or to **Closed – no fit** if the channel bounced. Do not run a fifth touch this week.

Three rules govern the sequence. **Every touch adds something** — new information, a new angle, or a smaller ask; a follow-up that only repeats the first message trains them to ignore you. **Any reply cancels the sequence** — you are in a conversation now, and you answer as a human, same day if you can. **Same channel unless it went dark** — switching from LinkedIn to email is a reasonable touch 3 move when there was no acceptance at all, and it is noise when there was.

## The daily routine

**Quota** — the minimum number you are expected to hit in a set period — is fifty contacts initiated by the end of Week 1. Spread across lab days that is manageable; done in one panicked block on Friday it is not, and it produces exactly the template blast that ruins your list. The routine below is what makes the number arrive as a byproduct.

Run three blocks, in this order, every lab day:

**Block A — replies and follow-ups (first, always, 30 minutes).** Open the saved view you built in Lesson 2. Answer every reply. Then send every follow-up due today. This block is first because these touches are worth many times a cold send, and because they are the ones that vanish if you run out of time.

**Block B — sends (60–90 minutes).** Work Tier 1 first, then Tier 2 and 3, until you hit the day's send number. Write each message fresh in the hook slot. Log immediately: activity recorded, stage moved to Initiated, next action dated for touch 2.

**Block C — research restock (30 minutes).** Refill the Researched stage so tomorrow's Block B is never blocked on finding people. Research is the *last* block of the day on purpose — it feels productive and it is the easiest place to hide from sending.

Two habits protect the routine. **Log inside the block, never later.** A batch of unlogged sends becomes an unreliable pipeline by tomorrow morning, and unreliable pipelines get abandoned. **Do not stop to research during Block B.** If a contact needs digging, drop them back to Researched and move on; context-switching between writing and researching is what turns a 60-minute block into three hours.

If a day goes sideways, protect Block A and cut Block C. Missing follow-ups costs you conversations you already earned; missing research costs you an hour tomorrow.

## Practice

Do these against your live pipeline. They are the sending half of the Week 1 milestone.

1. **Write three first touches by hand** to three real Tier 1 employer-partner contacts, using the four-part structure. For each one, underline the hook and confirm it would be false if pasted to anyone else. Send all three.
2. **Build your sequence in the CRM.** For every contact you initiate today, set the touch-2 task dated three days out. Confirm zero initiated contacts have no open task.
3. **Draft the four touches for one contact** end to end before you send touch 1, and check that each later touch adds something new and shrinks the ask. Save it as your reusable skeleton — hooks stay hand-written.
4. **Run a full day of the routine.** Blocks A, B, and C, in order, timed. Record how many you initiated and how long Block B actually took, then set tomorrow's send number from that real rate, not from optimism.
5. **Hit the running total.** Track cumulative contacts initiated against the 50-contact milestone daily; if you are behind after two lab days, raise Block B's number rather than adding a catch-up day.
6. **Rewrite one dud.** Take a first touch that got no response after touch 2, rewrite the hook and shrink the ask, and use it on the next similar contact. Bring both versions to lab.
