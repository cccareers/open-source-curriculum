## Your profile is the second half of every message

You have been sending since day one. Here is what happens on the other end: your message lands, it earns four seconds of attention, and if it works, the contact clicks your name. Your LinkedIn profile is what they see next. It is not a separate marketing project — it is the second half of a message already in flight.

That framing decides everything in this lesson. Your profile does not have to impress a stranger cold. It has to **confirm** what your outreach just claimed. If your message says you are a pre-apprentice training toward support engineering and your profile headline still says "Server at Riverside Grill," the contact resolves the mismatch against you. Nothing else about the profile matters as much as that alignment.

This is also why the profile lesson comes third and not first. You now know what you are actually saying to people, so you know what the profile has to back up.

## Skills-first, not history-first

A traditional resume is history-first: here is where I have been, in reverse order, decide what it means. A skills-first profile is the opposite: here is what I can do and the evidence, and here is the history that explains how I got here.

Career changers need skills-first because the history-first read produces the wrong conclusion. Nine years in restaurant management reads as "restaurant manager" unless you tell the reader what to take from it. Skills-first does not hide your background — it puts it to work.

Three sections carry the weight.

**Headline.** This is what appears next to your name in every connection request, every comment, every search result. It is seen far more often than the rest of your profile combined. Write it as *direction plus evidence*, not aspiration alone:

```text
Aspiring tech professional | Open to opportunities
```

```text
Support Engineering Pre-Apprentice @ CCC | Ticket triage,
SQL, customer troubleshooting | 9 yrs frontline ops
```

The second one tells a hiring manager what you are training for, that you are in a structured program, what you can already do, and where your judgment comes from. The first tells them nothing and is indistinguishable from thousands of others.

**About.** Four to six sentences, first person, in this order: what you are training toward and in what program; two or three specific capabilities with evidence; what your prior work gave you that transfers, stated concretely; what you are looking for. Skip the origin story about always loving computers. Concrete beats emotional every time — "I handled 60+ customer escalations a week and built the shift's triage checklist" outperforms "I'm passionate about helping people."

**Skills and Featured.** Fill the skills list with terms that match how employers name the work, not how you experienced it, and reorder so your target-role skills sit at the top. Use Featured for artifacts — a project, a repo, a write-up, a certificate. Evidence a contact can click beats any adjective you could write.

Two more items that quietly matter: a clear, current headshot (a face, well lit, no sunglasses, no group crop), and your custom profile URL cleaned up so it is safe to paste into an email signature. Neither is cosmetic. A missing photo measurably suppresses connection acceptances, and a URL full of random digits reads as an account somebody made yesterday.

One caution on the Experience section: skills-first does not mean deleting your history. Keep every role, and rewrite the bullets so each one names a transferable capability and the scale you did it at — systems you operated, volume you handled, problems you diagnosed, people you trained. A hiring manager reading "trained 14 new hires on the POS system and wrote the reference sheet still in use" sees documentation and onboarding skill. The same job described as "responsible for daily restaurant operations" gives them nothing to transfer.

## Alignment: profile and outreach say the same thing

Before you publish anything else, run the alignment check. Open one of the messages you sent this week beside your profile and confirm:

- The **role direction** in your message matches the headline. One target role, named the same way in both.
- The **capabilities** you claim in a message appear somewhere on the profile with evidence behind them.
- The **program affiliation** appears in both. For employer-partner contacts especially, "CCC pre-apprentice" is the credential doing the work — it says you are in a structured, supported program.
- The **tone** matches. Direct and specific in a message, direct and specific on the profile.

A contact who clicks and finds the same person described the same way moves on to the actual question — should I take this meeting. A contact who finds a mismatch stops there.

## Your first post

Publishing changes what a profile visit feels like. A profile with no activity is a static document. A profile with a recent post shows someone in motion, and momentum is exactly what a prospect is trying to judge when they check whether you are worth fifteen minutes.

Post topics that work in week one, in rough order of safety:

- **What you are building.** "Day 3 of the CCC pre-apprenticeship: today I set up a CRM pipeline and sent my first 20 outreach messages. Here's what surprised me." Specific, honest, and it demonstrates exactly the discipline an employer is scanning for.
- **Something you learned that others would find useful.** A small technical thing, explained plainly. Teaching is the fastest credibility you can build with zero work history in the field.
- **A public thank-you with substance.** Naming someone who gave you fifteen minutes and stating what you took from the conversation. It is genuine, it is visible to their network, and it makes the next informational interview easier to get.

What to avoid in your first post: asking for a job outright, a long career-change confession, anything vague and inspirational, and anything negative about a previous employer.

Shape matters as much as topic. Lead with a concrete first line — the feed truncates, and a generic opener never gets expanded. Keep it short, five to eight lines with white space between them. Close with a light question so people have a reason to comment. Two or three relevant tags, not ten. Post during business hours on a weekday, when the people you are targeting are actually online.

Then work the post for twenty minutes: reply to every comment, and comment substantively on three other people's posts. Engagement is not vanity here — every comment you leave puts your headline in front of a new network, which is the same headline your outreach is relying on.

## Practice

Do this live, on your real profile. Publishing is part of the Week 1 milestone.

1. **Rewrite your headline** in the direction-plus-evidence form, under the character limit. Write three versions, read all three aloud, publish the most specific one.
2. **Rewrite About** to four to six sentences in the order given, with at least two concrete, numeric details from your prior work. Publish it.
3. **Reorder skills and add one Featured item.** Move target-role skills to the top and pin one clickable artifact, even a small one.
4. **Run the alignment check.** Put one sent message beside your profile and check role direction, capabilities, program affiliation, and tone. Fix whichever side is wrong — sometimes it is the message.
5. **Publish your first post.** Pick one of the three topic patterns, write it, publish it during business hours on a lab day, and log the link in your CRM notes so your instructor can find it.
6. **Work the post.** Reply to every comment within the day and leave substantive comments on three other posts. Note in your lab log whether profile views or connection acceptances moved in the following 48 hours.
