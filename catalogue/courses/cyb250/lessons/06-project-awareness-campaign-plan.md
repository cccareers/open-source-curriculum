## The goal

Produce a complete, approval-ready plan for a three-month security awareness campaign at a described organization: who it targets, what it teaches, how it is delivered, how the simulation is authorized and constrained, what you will measure, and what you will tell the executive who has to sign it.

This is the artifact the role is asked for. Nobody hands an apprentice a budget and says "improve the security culture." They hand you a recent incident, a nervous director, and a request for a plan by Friday. The document you produce here is that plan, and it is assessed on whether an executive could approve it, a colleague could run it, and a sceptical auditor could tell afterwards whether it worked.

**You are planning a campaign. You are not running one.** Nothing is sent to anyone. No lure text is written. No message goes to any real person. The deliverable is a document.

Budget three hours. If you are ninety minutes in and still writing curriculum, you have spent your time on the easiest part — the parts that distinguish a professional plan are the authorization, the ethics constraints, and the measurement design.

## The client

**Ardleigh Housing Trust** is a not-for-profit social landlord with about 260 staff and 4,100 tenanted properties.

Who works there:

- **Head office, 85 people.** Finance and procurement (11), HR and payroll (6), IT (4, including a two-person service desk), a tenancy and income team (24), communications and governance (8), and management. Everyone has a corporate mailbox and a laptop.
- **Repairs and maintenance, 95 people.** Plumbers, electricians, joiners, and their supervisors, working out of two depots and vans. No corporate laptops. Job dispatch runs through an app on a company-issued phone; most of them use personal phones for everything else. They start the day at a 15-minute depot briefing.
- **Housing officers, 40 people.** Visit tenants in their homes, work from tablets and personal phones, come into the office roughly once a week.
- **Contact centre, 30 people.** Take tenant calls all day, on shift patterns, high call volume, high turnover — around a third of the team has been there under six months.
- **Around 10 contractors and agency staff** at any time, mostly in repairs and IT.

What has happened recently:

- Eight months ago, a supplier's mailbox was compromised and a bank-detail change went through inside a real email thread. £41,000 left the Trust and about half was recovered. Finance now "double-checks" changes, but there is no written procedure and no callback standard.
- The service desk resets passwords by phone. Verification is "do you sound like you work here." Two staff have separately reported calls from someone claiming to be a new starter in repairs.
- Three months ago a housing officer received a text on a personal phone claiming to be from the Chief Executive, asking whether they were free. They replied. Nothing further happened, and they did not tell anyone for six weeks.
- The contact centre receives tenant calls and has no way to verify that a caller is the tenant they claim to be.

What exists today: an annual 40-minute e-learning module assigned every January, 91% completion, one slide about phishing. No reporting button. Suspicious mail is "sent to IT" at an address two people monitor. There has never been a phishing simulation.

Constraints you have been given:

- One part-time programme owner — you — at roughly two days a week, plus the two-person service desk for triage.
- A small budget: the mail platform's built-in report button and simulation feature are already licensed. There is no money for a content vendor.
- A recognized trade union with a consultative agreement covering staff monitoring.
- Multilingual workforce: around 15% of repairs and contact centre staff speak English as a second language.
- The Director of Finance and Resources is your sponsor. She has asked for "something that shows we've fixed this" in time for the audit committee in four months.

## Requirements

Numbered so a reviewer can mark them one at a time.

**R1 — Audience and risk analysis.** A table with one row per audience covering: how they receive communication, their primary exposure, the two or three behaviors you most need from them, and their reachability constraints. Every group named above appears, including contractors. Add three sentences identifying which audience carries the most loss potential and which carries the most likelihood, and say what you do differently because those are not the same group.

**R2 — Campaign objective and target behaviors.** State the campaign's objective in one sentence, and name **no more than three** target behaviors for the three months. For each, state the audience, the observable evidence that it happened, and the baseline you would need to capture before starting. A campaign that targets six behaviors is a campaign that changes none.

**R3 — Content plan.** Month by month: topic, audience, format, length, and the single behavior each item targets. Mark each item as new or reused, and total the preparation hours against two days a week. At least one item must reach the repairs depots and one must reach contact centre staff on shift; neither may assume a corporate mailbox. Include one just-in-time control — a banner, a workflow prompt, a printed card at a point of decision — and say exactly where it appears.

**R4 — One delivered session, planned in full.** Pick one audience and produce the session plan at the level of detail from lesson 05: local hook, the lever you name, the behavior, the practice activity with two described interactions written out, the ask, the safety net, and timings summing to fifteen minutes or less. Include the handout, at most one side, written at a reading level appropriate to the audience.

**R5 — Reporting procedure.** A usable procedure document, under two pages: what to report, how to report it from a laptop, a company phone, and a personal phone, what happens next with published response commitments, the separate and unmissable path for someone who has already interacted, escalation with named roles, and the non-punitive statement. Add the card-sized version for the depot notice board. Then list the five policy statements you would add to the Trust's policy set to make this stick, each one testable, with the evidence an auditor would gather for each.

**R6 — Simulation design and authorization.** The section that decides whether this plan is professional. Cover:

- Objectives, and an explicit statement of what results will and will not be used for.
- Who authorizes it, in what form, and what the authorization document must contain.
- Which other parties are consulted during design, including the union, and what you are asking each of them for.
- Audiences included, and exclusions with the mechanism by which a manager requests one.
- Cadence and difficulty mix across the three months.
- **Three lure themes you would use and three you have ruled out**, each with one line of reasoning. Themes only — a theme is "a parcel delivery notice," not a message.
- What the debrief landing page says, in the tone you would actually use, in under 100 words.
- What data you collect, who can see individual-level data, how long it is retained, and what is reported in aggregate.
- How the existence of the simulation programme is communicated to staff before it starts.

**R7 — Measurement plan.** A scorecard of six to eight metrics. For each: definition with its exact denominator, data source, collection frequency, leading or lagging, baseline value or how you will obtain one before the campaign starts, and the decision it supports. Click rate may appear, but it may not be the headline, and your plan must say how you will stop difficulty differences from being read as progress. Add a short paragraph on the confounders that could move your numbers during these three months at this organization specifically.

**R8 — Two pieces of stakeholder communication.**

- *An approval memo to the Director of Finance and Resources*, under 400 words: what you are asking her to authorize, what it costs in staff time, what the risks are, what she will be able to tell the audit committee, and what you need from her personally. Lead with the answer.
- *The staff announcement*, under 250 words, that goes out before the programme begins: what is changing, why, that simulations will occur, that results are not used against anyone, and where to report. It must be readable by every audience in R1.

**R9 — Limits and control recommendations.** One page. State plainly what this campaign cannot fix, and recommend at least three technical or process controls that would reduce the Trust's exposure independently of whether anyone's behavior changes. At least one must address the supplier payment incident and at least one must address the service desk reset process. For each, name the owner you would take it to and the evidence you would use to argue for it.

**R10 — One-page summary.** Plain language, leading with the answer, readable on its own without the rest of the document: what the problem is, what you propose, what it costs, what will be measurable in four months, and what you need approved. No jargon that is not defined in the same sentence.

## Constraints

- **Nothing is sent.** No simulated message, no lure content, no test of any real person. Themes and design parameters only. A submission containing a ready-to-send lure fails, regardless of how good the rest is.
- **The ethics constraints from lesson 04 are hard requirements, not preferences.** Written authorization before anything runs; no punitive use of results; no lures exploiting personal distress — no fake redundancies, bonuses, payroll errors, medical or benefits emergencies, disciplinary notices, or impersonation of a named real person; planned exclusions; immediate non-humiliating debrief; data minimization. Your R6 must show all of them, and your three rejected themes must show that you can apply the test rather than recite it.
- **No real organizations or real people.** Ardleigh Housing Trust is fictional and everything in your plan stays inside it. Do not research or characterize a real landlord, and do not use a real colleague's name in any example.
- **Design for the workforce you were given.** A plan that only works for the 85 people with mailboxes has ignored two thirds of the organization and will be marked as such.
- **Resource honesty.** Two days a week and no content budget. If your plan needs more, say so explicitly in R8 and price it; do not quietly assume it.
- **Three hours.** If you must cut, cut breadth in R3. Do not cut R6 or R7 — they are where the assessment weight sits.

## Definition of done

Your submission is complete when all of the following are true.

1. All ten requirements are present and labelled with their requirement number.
2. R2 names three or fewer target behaviors, each with observable evidence and a stated baseline.
3. Every audience in R1 has a delivery route in R3 that does not assume a corporate mailbox unless they have one.
4. The session plan in R4 contains a practice activity in which the learners do something, with both described interactions written out, and the timings sum to fifteen minutes or less.
5. R5's reporting procedure gives a working route from a personal phone and contains an explicit, separate path for someone who has already clicked.
6. All five policy statements are testable and each has its evidence named.
7. R6 contains a named authorizer, a documented authorization, planned exclusions, a non-punitive statement, three used and three rejected lure themes with reasoning, a debrief text, and a data retention answer.
8. No lure content appears anywhere in the document.
9. Every metric in R7 has a denominator, a source, and a baseline plan, and click rate is not the headline.
10. The approval memo states a cost in staff time and a specific ask; the staff announcement is readable by the depot audience.
11. R9 recommends at least three controls that work independently of behavior change, each with an owner.
12. R10 is understandable to someone who has read nothing else and states what is being asked for.

## Hints

**The two incidents in the brief are your curriculum, and your evidence.** A five-figure supplier fraud and an unverified password reset process are not background colour — they are the reason your sponsor will sign, and they should appear in the approval memo, the campaign objective, and at least two content items. A plan that opens with generic phishing statistics when there is a real local loss on the table has wasted its strongest argument.

**The most-loss and the most-likely audiences are different, and that is the design problem.** Eleven people in finance and procurement hold nearly all the financial loss potential. Two hundred people in repairs, housing, and the contact centre hold nearly all the volume of attempts. Resourcing both with two days a week means one gets depth and the other gets reach. Decide which, and defend it.

**Reachability is the hard constraint, not content.** Ninety-five tradespeople who never open a laptop, thirty contact centre staff on rotating shifts, a third of them new, and forty housing officers who are in the office once a week. The depot briefing and the contact centre huddle are the two slots that actually exist. Design around the slots you have, not the ones you wish for.

**The contact centre has a problem the brief only hints at.** They take calls from tenants and cannot verify who is calling. That is social engineering exposure pointing *inward* — the Trust's own data at risk from callers impersonating tenants — and it is a legitimate thing to include or to explicitly scope out. Either is defensible; ignoring it is not.

**Baselines have to be captured before the campaign starts, which means they belong in your month-one plan.** If you propose to measure report rate improvement and have no pre-campaign number, you have designed a scorecard that can never demonstrate anything. Say how and when you take the baseline.

**The union consultation is not an obstacle to route around.** Simulated testing of staff is monitoring, there is a consultative agreement, and involving representatives in the design is both the legal-risk answer and the trust answer. Name what you would take to them and what you would ask for.

**Test your three rejected lure themes properly.** The check is whether you would be comfortable reading the theme aloud to the affected staff in the debrief. At a housing association there are obvious tempting themes involving pay, jobs, and tenant welfare, and they are exactly the ones that damage trust for a year to gain one quarter's statistic.

**R9 is where you show maturity, not where you undermine yourself.** Recommending mandatory callback verification for payment changes, a formal service desk identity verification standard, and phishing-resistant authentication makes the case that you understand awareness as one control among several. A plan that implies training alone will prevent the next £41,000 is a plan that will be quoted back at you when it does not.

**Write R10 last and check it against the document.** If the summary promises the audit committee something your plan does not measure, one of the two is wrong. Finding that yourself is worth more than any other twenty minutes in this project.
