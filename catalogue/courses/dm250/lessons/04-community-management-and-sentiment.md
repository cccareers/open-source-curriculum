## Community Management Is an Operation, Not a Personality

The comments, replies, direct messages, reviews, and stray mentions your content produces are not a side effect of posting. For most brands they are the majority of the actual work, and they are where the brand is either built or damaged in public, one exchange at a time.

Treating this as a personality trait — "Tomás is good with people" — is what makes it fragile. Tomás goes on holiday, or gets three complaints in one hour on a bad Tuesday, and the whole thing degrades. What makes it durable is running it as an operation with four written components: a **queue** you can see, an **SLA** you have promised, a **triage rule** that decides the response type before anyone starts typing, and an **escalation path** that was written down before it was needed.

Start with the size of it. Fernwood's typical week:

```txt
Instagram comments                            85
Instagram DMs                                 40
Facebook comments                             60
Facebook DMs                                  25
Facebook and Google reviews                    9
LinkedIn comments                             12
Tagged mentions (stories, posts)              30
Untagged mentions (name typed, not tagged)    15
                                             ---
Total inbound items per week                 276
```

Not all 276 need a reply. Applying the triage tree below, roughly 40% do — call it 110 items. At a realistic 90 seconds average per reply (some are ten seconds, some are ten minutes):

```txt
110 replies x 90 seconds = 9,900 s = 165 min  = 2.75 h
Weekly review-site and untagged-mention sweep  = 0.50 h
Logging themes for the monthly report          = 0.25 h
                                                -------
                                                 3.50 h per week
```

That is the 3.5 hours lesson 02 subtracted from Fernwood's capacity *before* any content was planned. It is the correct order of operations. An unanswered complaint costs more than a missed post, so the response budget is protected first and content is planned with what is left.

**The SLA.** Fernwood's promise, published internally and posted above Tomás's desk:

```txt
Coverage        Mon-Fri 08:00-18:00, Sat 09:00-13:00. Two passes daily
                minimum: 10:00 and 16:00.
First response  Within 4 working hours for anything actionable.
                Within 1 hour for safety, illness, or legal content.
Outside hours   Auto-response naming the next coverage window. No
                individual replies after 18:00 - a tired reply is the
                single most common source of a bad one.
Owner           Tomas primary. Alex covers cafe-specific questions and
                Saturdays. June covers LinkedIn. Priya covers escalations.
```

Two details do disproportionate work. **Naming a backup** is what makes the SLA survive a holiday. And **refusing to reply after hours** is a policy, not laziness: almost every reply a brand regrets was typed at 10pm by someone who felt attacked.

## The Triage Tree

Sort before you type. The single largest source of self-inflicted damage in this discipline is applying the wrong category's response — arguing with a legitimate complaint, apologizing to a troll, or replying at all to something that should have gone straight to the owner.

```txt
INCOMING ITEM (comment, DM, mention, review, tag)
|
+-1- Is it abuse, a slur, or spam?
|      YES -> Do not reply. Hide or report, block if repeated, log it. No exceptions,
|             including when the reply would be satisfying.
|             Test: if it contains a specific grievance, it is NOT abuse - it is an
|             angry complaint, and it goes down branch 5. When in doubt, not abuse.
|      NO  -> continue
|
+-2- Does it allege illness, injury, contamination, discrimination, a minor's
|    data, or make a legal or regulatory threat?
|      YES -> STOP. Post the holding line verbatim and nothing else. Escalate to
|             Priya within 1 hour. Do not investigate in public, do not apologize
|             in a way that concedes fact, do not delete the original.
|             Holding line: "Thank you for telling us - we take this seriously and
|             I'm sending you a message now so we can get the details." Nothing more.
|      NO  -> continue
|
+-3- Is it a question?
|      Can it be answered from published information (hours, prices, shipping,
|      origin, allergens)?
|        YES -> Public reply within SLA. Use the template, then add one specific
|               detail so it does not read as a bot.
|        NO, it needs order or account data
|             -> Public acknowledgement + move to DM. Never ask for an order
|                number, address, or email in a public thread.
|        NO, it needs expertise we do not have on hand
|             -> Reply within SLA with a real ETA and a name. Route to June
|                (wholesale/equipment) or Alex (cafe/menu). "I don't know, June
|                does, she'll reply today" beats a guess.
|
+-4- Is it praise, a tag, or user-generated content?
|      -> Reply specifically. Never a bare "thanks!" - name the thing they named.
|      -> To repost: ask permission in writing, wait for the yes, credit them.
|      -> Log it. Praise is sentiment data, and the monthly report needs it.
|
+-5- Is it a complaint?
|      First: is the underlying fact correct?
|        YES, we got it wrong
|             -> ACKNOWLEDGE -> OWN -> REMEDY -> PRIVATE -> CLOSE THE LOOP.
|        UNKNOWN, nobody has checked yet
|             -> Acknowledge the EXPERIENCE without conceding the FACT.
|                "That's not the experience we want you to have" is safe.
|                "We're sorry we shipped it late" before checking is not.
|        NO, we have evidence it did not happen
|             -> Public reply says only that you are looking into it. Correct
|                gently, in private, with the evidence. Never win an argument
|                publicly against a customer - the audience is not scoring it
|                on accuracy.
|      Then check the escalation triggers:
|        third contact from the same person, or remedy over $250, or a named
|        employee, or a wholesale account -> escalate before replying.
|
+-6- Is it a journalist, competitor, influencer pitch, or partnership ask?
|      -> No public reply. Route: press to Priya, wholesale to June, pitches to
|         the pitch folder. A public "DM us!" to a journalist is a quote.
|
+-7- None of the above - chatter, memes, a tag with no ask
       -> React or like. No reply needed. If it names a recurring theme, log it.
```

Print this. The value of a tree is that it is consulted when someone is upset, and nobody constructs good policy while upset.

## Responding Without Making It Worse

The objective says *without escalating it*, so it is worth being precise about what escalation means. Escalation is any response that increases the number of people who care, the intensity with which they care, or the number of things being argued about. It is almost always caused by the reply, not by the original complaint.

**The escalation ladder — what makes a bad situation worse, roughly in order of damage:**

1. **Deleting the complaint.** Converts one unhappy customer into a screenshot, a second post, and a story about censorship. Never delete a legitimate complaint. Hide is for abuse; delete is for almost nothing.
2. **Arguing the facts in public.** Even when you are right. The audience reads a brand contradicting a customer, not a factual correction.
3. **"We're sorry you feel that way."** Universally recognized as a non-apology. It apologizes for the customer's emotions rather than the event.
4. **Defending credentials nobody questioned.** "Our roasters have twelve years of experience" answers a question that was not asked and reads as dismissal.
5. **Citing policy first.** Policy may be the answer, but it cannot be the opening. It signals that the rules matter more than the person.
6. **Silence past the SLA.** A public complaint sitting unanswered for two days is itself a message, and it is a louder one than anything you would have written.
7. **Over-apologizing.** Four paragraphs of contrition reads as either insincere or as an admission of something much larger.
8. **Copy-paste replies to a thread.** Five identical apologies in one comment section is a screenshot people make on purpose.

**The pattern that works** is four moves, in this order, and the order matters more than the wording:

```txt
1 ACKNOWLEDGE   Fast, in the first clause. Speed beats polish.
2 OWN           The part that is actually yours, in one sentence. No blame
                on the customer, the courier, the supplier, or the weather.
3 REMEDY        One concrete offer, plus one alternative. Two options is
                respect; five is a negotiation.
4 PRIVATE       Move the details off the public thread. Then close the loop
                publicly, briefly, once it is resolved.
```

Sign with a human name. A reply from "the Fernwood team" is from nobody.

### Three rewrites

**Case 1 — Instagram comment on the July shipping backlog.**

> Third week in a row my subscription is late. I'm paying $19 a bag for coffee that shows up stale. Cancelling.

*Escalating reply:*

> Hi! We're sorry you feel that way. Our shipping partner has been experiencing industry-wide delays which are outside of our control. Per our terms, delivery estimates are not guaranteed. Please DM us your order number and we'll look into it!

Four rungs of the ladder in one paragraph: the non-apology, the blame shift, the policy citation, and a public request for order data. It also disputes nothing the customer said while somehow sounding like it did.

*De-escalating reply:*

> Three weeks late is on us, and I'm not going to blame the courier for it — we changed carriers on the 6th and we did not manage the transition well. I've messaged you: I'd like to refund July and ship this week's bag overnight, or cancel and refund the full month, whichever you'd rather. Either way you shouldn't have to ask twice. — Tomás

Acknowledges in the first clause, owns the specific decision, offers two concrete remedies including the one the customer asked for, moves to private, signed. It concedes only what Fernwood actually knows — the carrier change — and claims nothing about the coffee being stale, because nobody has checked that.

**Case 2 — one-star Google review naming a barista.**

> The tall guy with the beard at River Arts was openly rude to me. Never going back.

*Escalating reply:*

> We're sorry to hear this. That doesn't sound like Marcus at all — he's one of our longest-serving baristas and has never had a complaint. Are you sure you weren't at another location?

It names the employee publicly, defends him against a customer in public, and ends by suggesting the reviewer is mistaken about where they were. Every one of those is a separate injury, and one of them is to the employee.

*De-escalating reply:*

> I'm sorry — being spoken to rudely is not what you came in for, and I'd want to know exactly what happened. I've sent you a message so you can tell me directly, and I'll talk to the team either way. If you're open to it I'd like to buy you a coffee and hear about it in person. — Priya, owner

Never name or discuss an employee publicly. The conversation about what actually happened is a management conversation, and it happens with the employee privately, with the benefit of the doubt intact.

**Case 3 — a wholesale customer complaining on LinkedIn.**

> Fourth missed Tuesday delivery this quarter from @Fernwood Coffee Roasters. We have 40 staff and no coffee. Genuinely considering our options.

*Escalating reply:*

> Hi Dana, thanks for the feedback! Our records show three of those were rescheduled at your front desk's request. Let's take this offline.

Correcting a B2B customer in public, in front of the exact audience Fernwood sells to, and then dismissing them with "let's take this offline."

*De-escalating reply:*

> Forty people without coffee on a Tuesday is a real problem and I'm sorry. I'm calling you this morning, and we're moving your account to a named driver with a standing 7am slot so there is one person accountable for it. I'll come back here once it's fixed. — June, wholesale

Note the last line. **Closing the loop publicly** is what converts a visible complaint into visible proof that problems get fixed — and on LinkedIn, where prospective wholesale buyers are watching, that thread is now a sales asset. If June's records really do show three reschedules, that gets raised on the phone, framed as "let's fix how these get communicated," not as "actually, you're wrong."

### Hide, delete, block, report — write the rules down

```txt
HIDE    Slurs, harassment of staff or other customers, sexual content,
        spam links. Hiding removes it from public view without notifying
        the poster. This is the default tool.
DELETE  Almost never. Only content that must be removed for legal or
        safety reasons, or a duplicate posted by accident. Log every
        deletion with a screenshot and a reason before deleting.
BLOCK   Repeat offenders after two hidden comments. Not for one bad day.
REPORT  Impersonation, threats, coordinated attacks, doxxing.
NEVER   Never hide or delete a complaint because it is negative, embarrassing,
        or well-argued. If you are tempted, that is the signal it must stay up
        and be answered.
```

### The escalation matrix

| Situation | Goes to | Within | Public response allowed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Routine question, praise, small complaint | Tomás | 4 working hours | Yes |
| Café-specific complaint, staff named | Alex, then Priya if the staff member is named | 4 hours | Acknowledge only, no employee discussion |
| Remedy over $250, or third contact from one person | Priya | Same day | Acknowledge only |
| Wholesale account complaint | June | 2 hours | Acknowledge + a named next step |
| Illness, injury, contamination, allergen | Priya | 1 hour | Holding line only |
| Legal threat, regulator, media | Priya, then external counsel | 1 hour | Nothing at all |
| Anything involving a minor's data | Priya | 1 hour | Nothing at all |

Decide these while calm. A front-line responder working out at 9pm whether a legal threat needs the owner will decide badly.

## The Half of the Job That Is Not Replying

Community management that is purely reactive produces a support desk, not a community. Budget some of the response time for going outward:

- **Reply to people who did not mention you.** Local business posts, customers' own coffee posts, questions in neighbourhood groups. Ten thoughtful non-promotional replies a week does more for a small brand's reach than an extra post.
- **Run a ritual.** A recurring thing people can participate in — Fernwood's Friday "what are you brewing this weekend" thread — creates returning commenters, which is the only durable form of organic reach a small account gets.
- **Answer in public what you were asked in private.** A DM asking about decaf options is next week's post. Log the questions; they are free content research.
- **Amplify user content, with permission.** Reposting a customer's photo, credited, is the cheapest content that exists and it makes the next customer more likely to post.
- **Reply first, within the first hour of posting.** Early comments shape whether anyone else joins the thread, and every platform's ranking reads early engagement as a quality signal.

## Brand Sentiment: Measuring the Mood Honestly

Sentiment is the overall emotional tilt of what is being said about you. Monitoring it well at a small-business scale is not a tooling problem; it is a **discipline problem**, and the discipline is coding consistently and comparing to your own baseline instead of to a feeling.

### Code the mentions the same way every month

Three buckets, and a written rule for the ambiguous cases so that Tomás in July codes the same way Tomás in October does:

```txt
POSITIVE  Explicit praise, recommendation, enthusiastic UGC, a defence of
          the brand in someone else's thread.
NEUTRAL   Factual mention, a question with no valence, a tag with no
          comment, news or listing mentions, a mixed comment where praise
          and criticism roughly balance.
NEGATIVE  Any stated dissatisfaction, complaint, warning to others, or
          sarcasm at the brand's expense.

RULES FOR THE HARD ONES
- Mixed with a clear tilt goes to the tilt. "Great coffee, terrible wait"
  from someone who says they'll be back is NEUTRAL; from someone who says
  they won't is NEGATIVE.
- Code the mention, not the person. A loyal customer's complaint is negative.
- Sarcasm counts. "Cool, another week without my order" is NEGATIVE.
- Off-topic use of the brand name (someone named Fernwood) is EXCLUDED, not
  neutral. Exclusions are logged so the volume line stays comparable.
- When you cannot decide in ten seconds, it is NEUTRAL.
```

At Fernwood's volume — a few hundred mentions a month — code every one. Above roughly a thousand a month, code a random sample of 200 and report the percentage with the sample size stated next to it, because a percentage without an n is not a number. Once a quarter, have a second person code the same 40 mentions and compare; if you disagree on more than about a fifth of them, the rules need tightening, not the data.

### The tracking table

One row a month, kept forever. The derived column that matters is the **net sentiment score**:

```txt
NSS = (positive - negative) / total coded mentions x 100
```

```txt
Month  Mentions  Pos   Neu   Neg   Neg %   NSS    Top negative theme
Feb       312    148   141    23    7.4%  +40.1   Wait times, Downtown
Mar       298    139   137    22    7.4%  +39.3   Wait times, Downtown
Apr       341    160   156    25    7.3%  +39.6   Price of the Nyeri lot
May       326    150   152    24    7.4%  +38.7   Parking, River Arts
Jun       355    163   165    27    7.6%  +38.3   Parking, River Arts
Jul       690    214   313   163   23.6%   +7.4   Subscription shipped late
```

Check one row of the arithmetic so you can reproduce it. February: (148 − 23) = 125; 125 ÷ 312 = 0.4006; × 100 = **+40.1**. July: (214 − 163) = 51; 51 ÷ 690 = 0.0739; × 100 = **+7.4**.

### Set a baseline and a threshold before you need them

A single month's sentiment number means nothing on its own. What means something is the comparison to your own recent history.

```txt
Baseline window: Feb-Jun (5 months)
Mean negative share = (23+22+25+24+27) / (312+298+341+326+355)
                    = 121 / 1,632 = 7.41%
Mean NSS            = (40.1+39.3+39.6+38.7+38.3) / 5 = 39.2
Observed NSS spread = 40.1 - 38.3 = 1.8 points

THRESHOLDS (set in advance, in writing)
  WATCH   NSS falls more than 3 points below the baseline mean, OR
          negative share exceeds 1.5x baseline (11.1%), OR
          mention volume exceeds 1.5x baseline (490)
  ACT     NSS falls more than 6 points below the mean, OR
          negative share exceeds 2x baseline (14.8%), OR
          any single theme accounts for more than 25% of the month's negatives
```

The thresholds are deliberately crude, and they are set at multiples of a spread you observed rather than pulled from the air. Their whole purpose is to be decided while nothing is wrong, so that in the bad month nobody has to argue about whether it is a bad month.

### Reading July

July breaches every threshold. Volume is 690 against a 326 baseline (2.1×). Negative share is 23.6% against 7.41% (3.2×). NSS is +7.4 against a mean of +39.2 — a fall of **31.8 points**, when the entire five-month spread was 1.8.

But the number does not tell you what to do. **Always read the raw text before acting on a percentage.** Coding July's 163 negatives by theme:

```txt
Theme                                    Count   Share of negatives
Subscription box arrived late             104          63.8%
No reply to support email                  31          19.0%
Price increase on the Nyeri lot            12           7.4%
Cafe wait times                             9           5.5%
Other / unrelated                           7           4.3%
                                          ---
                                          163
```

Now it is legible. This is not "sentiment declined." It is **one operational failure plus one self-inflicted amplifier.** Fernwood changed shipping carriers on 6 July; the late boxes are the carrier transition. The 31 people complaining about support silence are complaining about Fernwood's own response failure — the queue tripled and the SLA was never re-staffed, so a fixable logistics problem grew a second, entirely avoidable problem on top of it.

One more inference worth drawing, carefully. **Complaints undercount incidents.** Only a minority of affected customers say anything in public — a common working assumption is between one in five and one in ten. If 104 people complained publicly about a late box, the number of late boxes is plausibly 500 to 1,000, against roughly 1,410 subscription shipments in the month. Label that as an estimate and go get the actual carrier data before repeating it; the point of the estimate is to size the urgency, not to be the number in the report.

The response follows from the diagnosis, in this order:

1. **Fix the operation first.** No public statement outruns a box that is still late.
2. **Re-staff the queue.** Clearing 31 unanswered support emails and restoring the 4-hour SLA removes a fifth of the negatives without touching the root cause.
3. **Acknowledge specifically and once.** A short public post that names the carrier change, states what is being done, gives a date, and offers a remedy to affected subscribers. Specific and boring beats warm and vague.
4. **Add a leading indicator.** The monthly table caught this on 31 July. The daily reply-queue volume would have caught it on 9 July. Add "inbound items per day" and "oldest unanswered item" to the weekly numbers so the next one is visible in week one.

### Share of voice, and what it is for

Sentiment tells you how people feel; **share of voice** tells you whether anyone is talking about you at all.

```txt
Category mentions, Asheville coffee, July: 4,850
  Fernwood                690    14.2%
  Competitor A          1,320    27.2%
  Competitor B            505    10.4%
  All others            2,335    48.2%
```

Read it alongside sentiment, never alone. Fernwood's share of voice went *up* in July, because a shipping failure generates conversation. A rise in share of voice during a sentiment collapse is not good news, and reporting it as a win is the kind of thing that destroys a marketer's credibility with an owner permanently.

### The routine that survives a busy quarter

```txt
Twice daily   Triage the queue on every live account. Reply within SLA.
Daily         Log inbound volume and the age of the oldest unanswered item.
Weekly        Sweep review sites, untagged mentions, and common misspellings.
              Log any new theme in one shared document.
Monthly       Complete the tracking table row. Code themes for the negatives.
              Compare against thresholds. Write the brief.
Quarterly     Read 30 mentions end to end, unfiltered, and re-check coding
              agreement with a second person. Numbers drift from reality if
              nobody ever reads the raw text.
```
