## What "Content Creation" Means Here

This lesson is about the part of content work that decides whether the content is worth making: choosing what to say, deciding which format each platform will reward it in, writing the copy, and scheduling it against a calendar your team can sustain. It is not about production craft. How to cut a video, colour-grade a photo, or build a template in a design tool is the multimedia course's job, and nothing here requires you to be good at any of them.

That division is not a dodge. On a small team the bottleneck is almost never editing skill — it is that nobody decided what this week's posts are about, so Thursday arrives and someone posts a photo of a latte with the caption "Happy Thursday!" A content plan is the thing that stops that from happening fifty times a year.

By the end you will be able to take one idea and turn it into five platform-native pieces with real copy, and lay a month of those pieces into a calendar that reconciles with the twelve hours a week Fernwood actually has.

## Pillars Come From Objectives, Not From Vibes

A **content pillar** is a recurring theme the account returns to. Three to five of them give the account an editorial identity and stop every week from starting at a blank page.

The mistake is choosing pillars by what feels on-brand. Derive them from the objectives instead, and give each one a target share of the calendar proportional to how much you need that objective. Fernwood's objectives from lesson 02 were: 120 new DTC subscribers, a 10% lift in midweek morning café transactions, and 6 new wholesale accounts.

| Pillar | Serves | Share of calendar | What it looks like |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Brew Better** | DTC subscribers | 40% | Grind, ratio, water, storage, gear — the fixable reasons home coffee disappoints |
| **Origin & Roast** | DTC subscribers | 20% | Where the beans came from, what the roaster decided and why, price transparency |
| **Café Life** | Café transactions | 25% | Staff, seasonal menu, the room at 9am, events, hours, the Tuesday reason to come in |
| **Wholesale & Work** | Wholesale accounts | 15% | Service reliability, equipment support, account stories, the operational side |

Two things this table buys you. First, **an argument you can win with the owner.** When Priya asks why there is only one post about the new mug, the answer is "mugs are 2% of revenue and this pillar set is proportional to the objectives we agreed in Q3 planning." Second, **a filter.** Any idea that fits no pillar either does not get made or forces a deliberate revision of the pillar set. Both outcomes are better than a calendar that drifts.

The shares also translate directly into slot counts. Fernwood runs 3 Instagram posts a week plus 2 LinkedIn posts a week, so a four-week month is 12 Instagram slots and 8 LinkedIn slots. Wholesale & Work at 15% of a 20-slot month is 3 slots, and all three sensibly live on LinkedIn — which is why the pillar share and the platform allocation have to be reconciled rather than set independently.

## Formats, Described by the Job They Do

You do not need to know how to build every format. You need to know **what each one is for**, so that when you have an idea you can tell which container it belongs in. Formats sort by the job they do:

| Format | The job it does | Relative cost | Where it lives |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Short vertical video | Reach strangers; demonstrate something physical | High | Reels, TikTok, Shorts, LinkedIn video |
| Carousel / multi-card | Teach a sequence; earn saves | Medium | Instagram, LinkedIn document post, Facebook |
| Single photo + caption | Show a moment; low-cost presence | Low | All |
| Text-only post | Make an argument; invite discussion | Low | LinkedIn, Facebook |
| Story / ephemeral | Ask, poll, remind, link out; talk to existing followers | Very low | Instagram, Facebook |
| Link post | Send traffic to something you own | Low | Facebook, LinkedIn |
| Live / event | Real-time presence; local turnout | Medium | Facebook Events, Instagram |

Two rules of thumb worth internalizing. **Reach comes from video and shares; retention comes from saves and comments.** If your objective needs strangers, you need formats the interest graph distributes. If your objective needs an existing audience to act, ephemeral and text formats are cheap and effective.

And **the format is a claim about effort**. A three-minute talking-head video that says what a two-line text post says is not more valuable; it is more expensive. Match the format to the density of the idea, not to what looks impressive.

## One Idea, Five Formats: The Adaptation Model

The competency here is adapting a content piece across platforms, and the reliable method is to separate the **idea** from its **containers**.

An idea worth adapting has three parts, which together form its **spine**:

```txt
CLAIM     A single sentence a reader could agree or disagree with.
EVIDENCE  The reason to believe it — a demonstration, a number, a story.
PAYOFF    What the reader can now do differently.
```

If you cannot write those three lines, you do not have an idea yet, you have a topic. "Grind size" is a topic. Here is the spine:

```txt
CLAIM     Your home coffee tastes sour because it is under-extracted, and
          the grind is almost always the cause — not the beans.
EVIDENCE  Two cups from the same beans, same water, same brewer, ground
          coarse and fine. Coarse tastes sharp and thin; fine tastes sweet.
PAYOFF    Before you buy different beans, grind two clicks finer and brew
          the same batch again.
```

Now build containers. The spine stays constant; what changes is the hook, the pacing, the length, and the call to action — because each surface has different discovery, different link tolerance, and a different reason the person is there.

**Container 1 — Instagram carousel (six cards).** Carousels earn saves, and saves are the signal Instagram treats most favourably for teaching content.

```txt
CARD 1  SOUR COFFEE IS NOT BAD COFFEE.        (large type, one line)
        It is usually under-extracted.
CARD 2  Sour / thin / sharp finish = water left before it pulled the sugars.
CARD 3  The cause is almost never the beans. It is the grind.
CARD 4  Photo: two grinds side by side, coarse left, fine right.
CARD 5  Fix: grind two clicks finer. Same beans, same water, same brewer.
CARD 6  Still sour? Then check water temp (200F) and ratio (1:16).

CAPTION
We get this email about twice a week: "the last bag tasted sour, can you
send a different roast?" Nine times out of ten the roast is fine and the
grind is too coarse.

Under-extraction means the water ran through before it dissolved the sugars
that balance the acids. You taste the acids alone, and you read that as
sour. Grinding finer slows the water down and pulls the rest through.

Two clicks finer, same beans, brew it again. Tell us in the comments what
changed - we read all of them.

Save this for the next bag that disappoints you.

ALT TEXT (card 4): Two piles of ground coffee side by side on white paper.
The left pile is coarse, like raw sugar. The right pile is fine, like table
salt.
```

**Container 2 — Reel / TikTok (28 seconds).** Interest-graph surfaces judge the first two seconds. Lead with the contradiction, not the setup.

```txt
0:00  ON SCREEN: "Stop buying new beans."
      SPOKEN: Your coffee isn't sour because the beans are bad.
0:03  Two cups on the counter, both poured.
      SPOKEN: Same beans, same water, same brewer. One tastes sharp and
      thin. One tastes sweet.
0:09  Close on the two grinds.
      ON SCREEN: "coarse" / "fine"
      SPOKEN: The only difference is how fine I ground it.
0:15  SPOKEN: Coarse means the water gets through too fast. You get the
      acids and none of the sugar. That's the sour.
0:21  Hand turns the grinder two clicks.
      SPOKEN: Two clicks finer. Same everything else.
0:25  SPOKEN: Try it before you blame the bag.
      ON SCREEN: "grind first, then judge"

CAPTION: two clicks. that's the whole fix. #coffeetok #pourover #homebarista
```

**Container 3 — LinkedIn text post.** Different audience, different reason to care. Fernwood's LinkedIn readers are office managers and restaurant owners, so the same spine has to be re-aimed at *their* situation: staff complaints about the office coffee.

```txt
Every office coffee complaint we get investigated in the last year fell
into one of three buckets. Only one of them was about the coffee.

1. The grind was wrong for the brewer. Someone set it once, for a
   different machine, in 2022.
2. The water was never filtered, or the brewer was never descaled.
3. The beans genuinely were stale - open bag, four weeks, warm shelf.

We now check all three on the first service visit, because replacing a
supplier is expensive and usually unnecessary. If your team says the
coffee "went bad," it is worth ten minutes with the grinder before it is
worth a procurement conversation.

Happy to walk any Asheville office through the three checks, customer or
not. It's not a sales call and it takes ten minutes.
```

**Container 4 — Facebook post.** Local, graph-based, link-tolerant. Push toward the room.

```txt
"This bag tastes sour." We hear it constantly, and it's almost never the
bag - it's the grind.

Alex is running a free 20-minute grind clinic at the Downtown cafe on
Tuesday the 15th at 10am. Bring your beans, bring your grinder if it's
portable, and we'll dial it in on our scale with you. Free, no signup,
show up whenever.

Full explanation of what's happening in the cup here:
fernwoodcoffee.com/blog/sour-coffee-grind
```

**Container 5 — Instagram Story sequence (three frames).** Ephemeral surfaces are for asking, not teaching.

```txt
FRAME 1  Poll: "Does your home coffee taste sour?"  [Often] [Never]
FRAME 2  "It's the grind, not the beans. Here's why ->" + sticker linking
         to the carousel
FRAME 3  Question box: "What grinder are you using?" - answers feed next
         week's Brew Better slot
```

Five surfaces, one spine, and none of them is a copy-paste of another. Look at what actually changed across the containers:

- **The hook length and position.** The carousel can spend a card on setup; the Reel cannot spend two seconds.
- **The audience's situation.** LinkedIn readers are not brewing at home, they are fielding complaints. Same claim, different pain.
- **The call to action, matched to link tolerance.** The carousel asks for a comment and a save because Instagram will not surface a link in the caption. Facebook gets the URL because Facebook will actually distribute it. Stories get the link sticker.
- **The commitment asked for.** Story asks for a tap. Carousel asks for a save. Facebook asks you to leave the house.

The failure mode this replaces is **cross-posting**: writing one post and pushing it to four platforms unchanged. It reliably produces a Facebook post that says "link in bio," a LinkedIn post full of hashtags, and a vertical video with text burned in where the platform puts its own interface.

## Writing the Copy

A few mechanics that matter more on social than anywhere else:

**Front-load ruthlessly.** Most surfaces truncate the caption after roughly the first line or two and hide the rest behind "more." Your first line has to work as a standalone. "We get this email about twice a week" earns the tap; "At Fernwood, we believe great coffee starts at home" does not, because it makes no claim anyone could disagree with.

**One idea per post.** If a draft contains two claims, it is two posts and you have just saved yourself a slot.

**Write the call to action for the surface, not from habit.** "Link in bio" on Facebook is a self-inflicted wound. "Comment below" on LinkedIn works; "double tap" does not.

**Hashtags and keywords are search, not decoration.** Treat them as the terms someone would use to find this content. Three to five specific ones beat thirty generic ones, and platforms increasingly read the caption text itself as searchable — so write the words your reader would type.

**Alt text is not optional.** Every image gets a plain description of what is in it. It serves people using screen readers, and it is also read by the platform. Write it as a sentence, not a keyword list.

**Read the draft aloud.** Social copy that sounds like a press release was written by someone imagining a brand rather than talking to a person.

## The Calendar

A calendar is not a list of post ideas. It is a production schedule, and every row has to answer: what is going out, where, what it needs before it can go out, and who owes it.

A usable slot record has eight fields:

```txt
DATE | PLATFORM | PILLAR | FORMAT | WORKING HOOK | OWNER | ASSET NEEDED | STATUS
```

The two fields people leave off are the ones that make the calendar work. **Asset needed** is what turns "we should post about the new roast" into a task with a dependency — if the photo does not exist, the post cannot ship, and you want to discover that on the 3rd rather than the 14th. **Status** moves through a fixed set of states so anyone can see where a post is stuck:

```txt
idea -> drafted -> approved -> scheduled -> posted
```

Here is a real month for Fernwood. Instagram Monday/Wednesday/Friday, LinkedIn Tuesday/Thursday, Facebook mirroring two of the Instagram posts, one reactive slot held open every week, and Stories running daily off whatever is in the cafés.

Notice what the pillar check does. That conversation happening in planning is worth more than the calendar itself.

### Ratios, lead times, and the reserve slot

Three planning conventions make a calendar survive contact with a real month.

**Evergreen to timely to reactive, roughly 60/30/10.** Evergreen content (the grind explainer) can be made in advance and reused. Timely content (the clinic, the seasonal menu) is dated and must ship on the day. Reactive content is what you post because something happened. If your calendar is 90% timely you have no buffer; if it is 100% evergreen the account feels like a brochure.

**Lead times, stated per format.** Video needs filming scheduled before the slot; at Fernwood, Alex films on Tuesdays, so a Friday Reel must be requested by the previous Tuesday or it does not exist. Write the film day into the calendar as its own row if you keep missing it.

**One reserve slot a week, held empty on purpose.** Something will happen — a review, a local news mention, a heat wave that makes cold brew relevant, an equipment failure worth being honest about. If every slot is committed, reactive content displaces planned content and the pillar balance quietly collapses.

### Reconciling with capacity

Lesson 02 left Fernwood 7.5 hours a week for planning and creation. Price the calendar:

```txt
Batch planning + drafting, one 2-hour block Monday      2.0 h
Carousel build (1/wk, ~60 min incl. copy)               1.0 h
Reel: filming with Alex + script (1/wk)                 2.0 h
Photo posts + captions (2/wk)                           0.5 h
LinkedIn drafting with June (2/wk)                      1.0 h
Stories, daily, in-the-moment                           0.5 h
Facebook mirroring and reformatting (2/wk)              0.5 h
                                                        ------
                                                        7.5 h
```

It fits exactly, which means it does not fit — there is no room for the week the espresso machine dies. The honest revision is to drop to one carousel every other week and bank the saved 0.5 hours, or to batch two Reels in a single filming session every fortnight, which costs less than two separate sessions because setup dominates. Say which you chose, in writing, so that when the plan slips a reviewer can see whether the plan or the execution failed.

## Approvals, Reuse, and Permission

Three governance rules that prevent most of the ugly incidents:

**One named approver, one working day.** Anything making a factual claim about sourcing, pricing, health, or hiring goes to Priya before it ships. Everything else ships on Tomás's judgment. An approval process with no time limit is a process that stops the calendar.

**Get permission in writing before you post a person.** A customer's photo, a customer's name, an employee's face, a wholesale account's logo or story — ask, get a yes you can point to later, and keep it. June's "Blue Ridge Dental" case study post in week 3 has "customer approval" in the asset column for exactly this reason. This is a courtesy that becomes a legal requirement fast when personal data is involved.

**Plan reuse from the start.** The best-performing evergreen post of a quarter should be re-cut and re-run the next quarter, not retired. Keep a short list of what earned saves and shares, with the date it last ran, and treat the list as a supply of slots you do not have to invent.

## Practice

**Part 1 — Pillars from objectives.** For Fernwood, or a business of your choice, write three to five content pillars. For each: the objective it serves, its target share of the calendar, and two example post titles. Then show how the shares convert into slot counts for one month at your chosen cadence, and name any conflict between the pillar shares and the platform allocation.

**Part 2 — Adapt one idea across four formats.** Pick one idea and write its spine (claim, evidence, payoff) in three lines. Then produce **actual copy, not descriptions**, for four containers on at least three platforms. Include at minimum:

- a carousel or multi-card outline with the text of every card, plus the full caption and alt text for one image
- a short vertical video script with timestamps, spoken lines, and on-screen text
- a text-only post aimed at a *different* audience segment than the other pieces
- one ephemeral or link-bearing piece with a call to action appropriate to that surface's link tolerance

Then write a short paragraph naming what you changed between containers and why — hook, audience situation, call to action, and commitment level.

**Part 3 — Build the calendar.** Produce a four-week calendar with all eight fields per slot. It must include: a stated cadence per platform, a reserve slot each week, at least one row that exists only to schedule an asset (a film day, a photo shoot), and a pillar check at the bottom showing planned share against target share with the arithmetic.

**Part 4 — Reconcile it.** Price your calendar in hours by activity, total it, and compare it to a stated weekly capacity. If it does not fit with at least 10% slack, change the calendar — not the capacity number — and say what you cut.
