## Where This Lesson Starts and Stops

Paid social sits in this course because the competency is *organic and paid* social campaigns — a coordinated effort, not two disconnected disciplines. What you learn here is how to **plan** paid support outward from an organic strategy you already have: which content earns money behind it, who to aim it at, what objective to select, how much of the budget to commit, and what to hand to the person who builds it.

What you will not learn here is how the auction works, how to choose and tune a bid strategy, how to structure an ad account, or how to run and read a statistically valid test. Those belong to the paid advertising course, and duplicating them badly here would make you worse at both. When this lesson needs a number like a cost per thousand impressions, it will be labelled as a **planning assumption** and used to size a plan, not to forecast an outcome.

The line is roughly this: **you decide what deserves spend and who should see it; the media buyer decides how to buy it.** Both jobs are real, and on a small team the same person often does both — but the thinking is different and they fail in different ways.

## Three Legitimate Jobs, and One Illegitimate One

Paid social does three useful things for a brand that already posts:

**1. It extends the reach of content that has already proven itself.** Organic distribution is capped — most platforms show an unpromoted post to a fraction of your followers and almost nobody else. When a post has demonstrably worked with the people who saw it, money buys more of those people. This is amplification, and it is the highest-confidence use of a small budget because the creative risk has already been retired.

**2. It reaches people the graph cannot reach.** Organic on a graph-based platform mostly talks to people who already know you. Fernwood's objective of 120 new DTC subscribers from outside a 50-mile radius is not solvable organically at Fernwood's scale, on the platforms Fernwood can staff. Paid targeting is the mechanism that makes the audience reachable at all.

**3. It re-reaches the audience organic built.** Someone who watched three quarters of a Fernwood Reel, or visited the site from a Story link, is a warmer audience than any interest targeting can construct — and they are addressable with spend. This is the part of paid social most dependent on organic, because organic is what generated the audience in the first place.

The illegitimate one: **boosting content that has not earned organic engagement.** This is the classic beginner error and it deserves a paragraph of its own. A post that underperformed with the people most likely to like it — your own followers — will not perform better with strangers. Spending on it does not fix it; it buys a larger sample of the same disappointment, and it wastes the one signal a small brand gets for free. Organic performance is your creative testing lab, and it is free. Use it.

### The organic evidence gate

Make the rule explicit, and calibrate it against your own baseline rather than an industry number. Fernwood's Instagram baseline across the last 90 days:

```txt
Median reach per post                              2,840
Median saves per post                                 31
Median shares per post                                18
Median saves per 1,000 reached  (31 / 2,840 x 1,000)  10.9
Median shares per 1,000 reached (18 / 2,840 x 1,000)   6.3
```

The gate:

```txt
A post is eligible for promotion only if, after 7 days of organic life, it
clears BOTH:
  saves per 1,000 reached  >= 16.4   (1.5x the median of 10.9)
  shares per 1,000 reached >=  9.5   (1.5x the median of 6.3)
and it names a specific next step a stranger could take.

A post is eligible for LOCAL promotion (cafe objectives) at 1.2x the medians,
because the audience is smaller and the action is cheaper.

Nothing is promoted before day 7. Nothing is promoted because someone in the
business liked it.
```

Why saves and shares rather than likes: a like is a reaction to content, while a save is a statement that the content will be useful later and a share is a willingness to spend social credit on you. Both correlate far better with the behaviour you want from a stranger. Lesson 06 goes into why.

Apply the gate to the July calendar from lesson 03:

```txt
Post                                Reach   Saves  Shares  Saves/1k  Shares/1k  Eligible?

Jul 10  Reel "Two clicks finer"    11,650    204     249      17.5      21.4     YES

```

Two national candidates out of seven, one local. That ratio is normal and it is the point: the gate exists to stop you spending on the other four.

## Audience Planning

With no search query to tell you what someone wants, the audience definition *is* the targeting. Plan it in three layers, ordered by how much you already know about the people in them.

**Layer 1 — audiences built from your own data.** Customer lists you are permitted to upload, site visitors, subscribers who lapsed, people who engaged with your content, people who watched a video past a threshold, people who opened a lead form. These are the warmest and the cheapest to convert, and they exist *because* of organic work. They are also small: Fernwood's 30-day site visitors number about 4,100, which is enough for retargeting and nothing else.

**Layer 2 — audiences modelled from your data.** "People similar to this source list." The quality of the output is entirely determined by the quality of the source. A model built from *all site visitors* describes people who visit websites. A model built from **subscribers who have renewed at least three times** describes people who buy coffee subscriptions repeatedly, which is the population Fernwood actually wants more of. Choose the source list deliberately and write down why.

**Layer 3 — interest and broad audiences.** Defined by stated interests, behaviours, demographics, geography, or job attributes. This is where genuinely new people come from, and it is the layer where creative does most of the persuading because you know least about who you reached.

### The retargeting ladder, built from organic

Every rung below is an asset organic created. This is the concrete sense in which paid depends on organic:

```txt
Rung  Audience                                  Approx size  Built by
6     Existing subscribers                          1,180    Purchases
5     Site visitors, last 30 days                    4,100    Story links, FB links, bio link
4     Video viewers past 50%, last 60 days           9,300    The Reels in lesson 03
3     Profile visitors + post engagers, 90 days      6,750    Organic posting and replies
2     Similar-to-audience from 3x renewers          modelled  Subscription data
1     Interest and broad, national                   large    Nothing - purely paid
```

Exclusions matter as much as inclusions. Existing subscribers should not see an acquisition offer for the thing they already pay for; a first-purchase discount shown to a loyal customer is an insult you paid for. State every exclusion in the plan.

### Match the message to the rung

| Audience | What they already know | Message | Asset |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rung 1 — interest, national | Nothing | Teach one thing; earn the follow or the click | Reel "Two clicks finer" |
| Rung 2 — similar to renewers | Nothing, but they resemble buyers | Teach + name the subscription plainly | Carousel "Sour coffee" + offer card |
| Rung 3 — engagers | Recognize the brand | Why Fernwood specifically: origin, roast dates | Origin content + first-bag offer |
| Rung 4 — video viewers 50% | Watched us explain something | Continue the same idea, then ask | Sequel Reel + offer |
| Rung 5 — site visitors 30d | Considered buying | Remove the friction they stalled on: shipping, cancel-anytime | Direct offer, short copy |
| Rung 6 — subscribers | Everything | Excluded from acquisition. Referral or upsell only | Referral card |
| Local, 5-mile radius | Some know the cafés | Occasion + location + time | "Cardamom cold brew" photo |

The reason to write this table before anything is built is that it forces the plan to answer the only question that matters on a cold surface: *why would this specific person stop scrolling for this specific message?* "Because we boosted it" is not an answer.

## Choosing the Objective

Every ad platform makes you pick an objective, and that choice tells the system what to optimize for. Pick it from the funnel stage the content already occupies, not from the outcome you wish for.

| Objective family | Optimizes toward | Use when | Will not do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Awareness / reach | Cheapest impressions or reach | Launching, local occasion, brand introduction | Produce measurable purchases |
| Video views | People who watch | Proving a creative concept cheaply | Produce site traffic |
| Engagement | Reactions, comments, follows | Building a retargetable audience early | Produce revenue |
| Traffic | Clicks or landing page views | Sending readers to something you own | Produce conversions on its own |
| Leads | Form completions | Wholesale enquiries, waitlists | Qualify the lead for you |
| Sales / conversions | A specific tracked action | You have tracking installed and enough volume | Work without conversion tracking |

Two rules that prevent the most common mismatches. **Do not pick a conversion objective for a brand with no conversion tracking installed** — the system cannot optimize toward an event it never receives. And **do not pick an awareness objective and then judge it on sales**, which is the marketing equivalent of buying a bus ticket and complaining it did not fly. The objective and the success metric are the same decision made twice; if they disagree, one of them is wrong.

Fernwood's mapping is straightforward once the audience ladder exists: rungs 1 and 2 get video views and traffic, because the job is to introduce and to build a retargetable pool; rungs 3 through 5 get traffic or conversions, because the pool now exists; the local café push gets reach with a tight radius, because the outcome is a person walking through a door and nothing on the platform can see that anyway.

## Budget as a Planning Decision

Bidding is dm230's subject. Budget *allocation* is yours, and it is answered with four questions: what share of the marketing budget, split how across the three jobs, at what floor per test, for how long.

Fernwood's Q3 paid social budget is **$3,600** — $1,200 a month, about $40 a day.

```txt
Job                                    Share   Quarter    Per month
Amplify proven organic (rungs 1-2)      40%    $1,440       $480
Prospecting to new audiences (rung 1)   35%    $1,260       $420
Retargeting (rungs 3-5)                 15%      $540       $180
Local cafe and events (geo-fenced)      10%      $360       $120
                                       -----   -------
                                       100%    $3,600
```

### Does the plan produce enough data to learn anything?

This is the check almost everyone skips, and it is the difference between a plan and a wish. Before committing, count the **cells** — every unique combination of creative concept and audience that will run — and divide.

The tempting version of the prospecting plan is four creative concepts against three audiences:

```txt
4 concepts x 3 audiences = 12 cells
$1,260 / 12 cells = $105 per cell per quarter = about $8 per week per cell
```

Eight dollars a week per cell produces, at any plausible cost per result, a small handful of results per cell across the entire quarter. Nothing distinguishable from noise will come out of it, and you will have spent $1,260 to learn nothing. A workable rule of thumb at the planning stage: **a cell needs to be sized to produce on the order of 50 results over the test window, or do not run it as a separate cell.** (What counts as enough results to *declare* a winner is a statistics question, and dm230 answers it properly. This is a coarse sizing check, deliberately.)

Rebuild it:

```txt
2 concepts x 2 audiences = 4 cells
$1,260 / 4 cells = $315 per cell per quarter = about $24 per week per cell
```

That is still modest, but it is now a plan that can produce a signal. The cost of the check is that you dropped two creative concepts and one audience — and dropping them on purpose, before spending, is strictly better than discovering in October that all twelve cells were unreadable.

### Sanity-check the cost per result against the unit economics

You need to know, before launch, what a result is allowed to cost.

```txt
DTC subscription: $19 per bag, one bag per month, average life 7 months
  Lifetime revenue        $19 x 7           = $133.00
  Gross margin                                    48%
  Lifetime gross profit   $133.00 x 0.48    =  $63.84
  Break-even cost per new subscriber        =  $63.84
  Fernwood's rule: leave 60% of the profit  =  $63.84 x 0.40 = $25.54 target

One-off order (no subscription)
  Average order           $46.00
  Gross profit            $46.00 x 0.48     =  $22.08
  Break-even on FIRST purchase alone        =  $22.08
```

Now the planning arithmetic can be honest. Suppose the plan assumes — as a placeholder — a $14 cost per thousand impressions on national interest audiences and a 1.0% click-through rate:

```txt
$1,260 at a $14 CPM   = 1,260 / 14 x 1,000 = 90,000 impressions
Clicks at 1.0% CTR    = 900 clicks
Effective cost/click  = $1,260 / 900       = $1.40
Subscription starts needed to hit the $25.54 target
                      = $1,260 / $25.54    = 49.3 -> about 49 starts
Site conversion rate that requires
                      = 49 / 900           = 5.4%
```

That last line is the one to argue about. A 5.4% conversion rate from cold social traffic to a paid subscription is optimistic — it is roughly double what cold interruption traffic typically does. So the plan, on its own assumptions, does not clear its own target, and you know that **before** spending $1,260. The options are to accept a higher cost per subscriber on first purchase and defend it on lifetime value, to point the prospecting budget at a cheaper conversion (a free grind guide that produces an email address) and convert later, or to move budget from prospecting into retargeting where the conversion rate is several times higher. Any of those is a defensible plan. Launching and hoping is not.

Every number in that block except the two prices and the margin is a **stated assumption**. Label them as such, in the plan, so that when the real cost per thousand comes back at $21 the plan can be corrected instead of defended.

## What Changes When a Post Becomes an Ad

An organic post and an ad built from it are not the same artifact, and handing the raw post to the buyer produces predictable problems.

- **The hook has to work for someone with no context.** Organic reaches people who follow you; an ad reaches strangers mid-scroll. Anything that assumes prior knowledge — "as we mentioned last week" — has to go.
- **The call to action becomes explicit and singular.** "Save this" is an organic action. An ad asks for one thing: click, subscribe, sign up, visit.
- **Captions and on-screen text become mandatory**, because a large share of feed video is watched with sound off, and because it is an accessibility baseline.
- **Claims become regulated speech.** Anything about health, weight, energy, sleep, or medical effect is a policy minefield on every major platform. "Our cold brew has more caffeine than drip" needs to be substantiable. Comparative claims about competitors, guarantees, prices, and urgency all need to be true and supportable.
- **Disclosure obligations attach.** If the creative came from a customer or a partner, the commercial relationship has to be disclosed, and using a customer's face or words in a paid ad usually requires a permission distinct from the one they gave for an organic repost. Go and get it.
- **Personal attributes cannot be implied.** Copy that addresses the viewer by an inferred characteristic is prohibited on the major platforms. Write about the product, not about who you think the reader is.
- **Frequency becomes your problem.** Organic rarely shows one person the same post nine times. Paid does, and it is the most common cause of the pattern where results look great for eight days and then collapse.

## Measuring the Overlay Honestly

Lesson 06 covers social measurement in full. Three cautions specific to paid:

**Platform-reported results are the platform's own account of its work.** They are useful and they are not neutral. Compare them against your own web analytics, expect them to disagree, and report both.

**Paid reach overlaps organic reach.** Some of the people your ad reached would have seen the post anyway, so the incremental effect is smaller than the reported one. At Fernwood's budget you cannot measure this precisely; you can at least stop adding paid and organic reach together and calling the sum an audience.

**Judge each line item on the signal you named before launch**, not on the metric that happened to look best. If asset 1 was bought on video views to build a retargeting pool, it is judged on pool growth and cost per view — not on the two subscriptions it incidentally produced.

## Failure Modes

- **Boosting whatever the owner liked.** The gate exists to make this a conversation about a rule rather than about taste.
- **One campaign, one audience, forever.** No cells, no learning, and frequency quietly destroys performance around week three.
- **Prospecting with no retargeting.** Paying to introduce yourself to people and then never following up is the most expensive way to run social.
- **Paid replacing organic.** When the budget ends, so does the brand's presence, and the retargeting pool that made paid efficient stops being refilled.
- **Targeting so narrow the system cannot work.** Stacked interests, a 3-mile radius, and an age band produce an audience too small to deliver, at a premium price.
- **Judging awareness spend on last-click revenue.** You will kill the top of your own funnel and then wonder why retargeting dried up two months later.
- **Reporting platform numbers and web analytics numbers as if they were the same number.** They are not, and the day someone notices, your credibility goes with it.

## Practice

**Part 1 — Build the gate.** Using the 90-day medians below for a brand of your choice (or Fernwood's), write a promotion eligibility gate: the metrics, the multiples, the waiting period, and any qualitative requirement. Defend each multiple in one sentence. Then apply it to a set of at least six posts and produce the eligibility table, showing the per-thousand arithmetic for every row.

**Part 2 — The audience ladder.** Build the six-rung ladder for your brand. For each rung state: what organic activity created it, its approximate size, what that audience already knows, the message you would send, and the asset you would send. Then state every exclusion and why.

**Part 3 — Objective mapping.** For each of your top three rungs, name the campaign objective you would select, what it optimizes toward, the one success signal you will judge it on, and — importantly — one outcome you are explicitly *not* expecting it to produce.

**Part 4 — Budget and readability.** Given a quarterly budget you choose (or Fernwood's $3,600):

1. Split it across amplification, prospecting, retargeting, and any local job, with a defended share for each.
2. Count your cells and divide. If a cell cannot be sized to produce roughly 50 results over the window, restructure the plan and say what you dropped.
3. Derive the allowable cost per result from unit economics, showing every line of the arithmetic, including the difference between break-even on first purchase and break-even on lifetime value.
4. Using stated planning assumptions for cost per thousand and click-through rate, work forward to the conversion rate your plan implicitly requires. Say whether you believe it, and if not, name which of the three fixes you are taking.

**Part 5 — The handoff.** Write the one-page overlay plan a media buyer could build from: every asset with its organic evidence, audience, exclusions, objective, budget, run length, and pre-declared success signal, plus a labelled list of every assumption you made. Finish with one paragraph naming what you are handing over to the buyer and what you are keeping.
