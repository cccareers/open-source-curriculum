## The First Decision Is Not "What Should We Post"

Most social media work goes wrong before the first post is written, because the platform was never actually chosen. It was inherited, or it was picked because the owner's daughter uses it, or it was opened during a burst of enthusiasm in 2021 and nobody has closed it since.

Platform choice is a resource-allocation decision, and it behaves like one. Every platform you run costs a fixed weekly amount of attention whether or not it produces anything: someone drafts, someone schedules, someone reads the replies, someone answers the direct messages that arrive at 9pm. Adding a fifth account does not add a fifth of your results, it subtracts from the four you already had. The single most common cause of a bad social presence is not bad content. It is a good team spread across too many surfaces.

So this lesson builds a decision, not an opinion. You will end with a scoring method that takes three inputs — an objective, an audience, and your real capacity — and produces a ranked platform list plus a written statement of what you deliberately declined and what would make you revisit it.

### The brand we will use all course

**Fernwood Coffee Roasters** is a regional roaster in Asheville, North Carolina, founded in 2016. It has three parts:

- **Cafés.** Three locations — Downtown, West End, River Arts — that together produce about 54% of revenue. Local customers, heavily weekday-morning.
- **Direct-to-consumer.** Bags and subscriptions shipped nationally. About 27% of revenue. A bag is $19, the average order is $46, and gross margin is 48%.
- **Wholesale.** Twenty-two accounts: offices, restaurants, and two grocery co-ops. About 19% of revenue. The average account bills $340 a month at a 31% margin and lasts about 19 months.

The people: **Priya Raman** owns the business. **Tomás Barrera** is the marketing coordinator and the person whose job you are learning. **June Whitfield** runs wholesale. **Alex Moreno** manages the Downtown café and is the one staff member who will reliably film something.

The constraint that will decide everything: **Fernwood has 12 hours a week of human attention for social media, total.** Not 12 hours of posting — 12 hours including planning, drafting, scheduling, replying, and reporting.

## Write the Objective Before You Look at a Platform

"Grow our social media" is not an objective; it is a wish with a budget attached. A usable objective names three things: the **outcome** in business terms, the **audience** who has to act, and the **horizon** over which you will judge it.

Compare:

```txt
Not usable:  Grow our Instagram following.
Usable:      Add 120 net new DTC subscribers in Q3, from people outside a
             50-mile radius who have never bought from us.

Not usable:  Get more people into the cafés.
Usable:      Lift Tuesday-through-Thursday 9am-11am café transactions 10%
             by the end of Q3, among people who live or work within 3 miles
             of a location.

Not usable:  Be more visible to businesses.
Usable:      Open 6 new wholesale accounts in Q3, from office and restaurant
             decision-makers in the Asheville metro.
```

The usable versions do work for you immediately. Each one names a population, and a population is a thing you can go look for on a platform. Each one names an outcome you can measure, which means the measurement plan in lesson 06 has something to attach to. And each one has a deadline, which is what turns "we tried TikTok" into "we ran TikTok for one quarter against a stated target and here is what happened."

One more discipline before you score anything: ask whether social is even the right channel for the objective. Social media is strong at **being discovered by people who were not looking for you**, at **showing personality and proof**, and at **being where people talk to you and about you**. It is weak at **capturing existing demand** — someone who has already decided to buy fresh-roasted coffee types that into a search engine, and that is paid search and SEO territory. If the objective is "capture people already searching for us," a social platform is not the answer no matter how it scores.

## A Comparison Framework That Survives Redesigns

Platform interfaces change every few months, audience statistics change every year, and entire platforms appear and lose relevance inside the lifetime of one course revision. Memorizing where a button lives is therefore a waste of your time. What does not change is the small set of structural properties that determine what a platform is good for. Learn to read those, and you can evaluate a platform that did not exist when you learned the method.

There are seven axes worth scoring.

**1. Discovery mechanism.** Who sees a post, and why? Three broad answers: the **social graph** (people who chose to follow you, plus their connections), the **interest graph** (an algorithm shows content to strangers it predicts will like it, regardless of following), and **search or professional context** (people find you while looking for something in a defined category). An interest-graph platform can put a brand-new account in front of 40,000 strangers; a graph-based platform generally cannot. This axis determines whether the platform can serve an acquisition objective at all.

**2. Dominant format and production cost.** What format does the platform reward with distribution, and what does one unit of it cost your team in hours? A platform that rewards vertical video with talking-head editing costs a small team three to five times what a text-and-photo platform costs.

**3. Audience composition.** Not "how many users" but "how many of *your* people, in a state where they might act." A platform with 200 million users and none of your buyers is worth less than a platform with 4 million users where your entire wholesale market lives.

**4. Content half-life.** How long a post keeps earning distribution. Some platforms surface content for hours; some keep resurfacing a post for weeks. Short half-life means volume is mandatory. Long half-life means a single strong post can carry a month, and quality beats cadence.

**5. Link tolerance.** Does the platform surface off-platform links, bury them, or route them through a single profile link? This axis decides whether a platform can drive traffic directly or only build demand that gets captured elsewhere. Get it wrong and you will build a beautiful presence that sends nobody anywhere.

**6. Conversation surface.** Is this where your customers expect to reach you — questions, complaints, direct messages? This is a *cost* axis as much as a benefit one. Opening an account on a platform where people complain commits you to a response operation, which lesson 04 will price out.

**7. Paid overlay.** Can you amplify what works organically, and to whom? Some platforms have mature self-serve advertising with rich audience tooling; some have almost none. Lesson 05 depends on this axis.

Applied to the four platforms this course uses for worked examples, the framework reads roughly like this. Treat the cells as a snapshot to be re-derived, not as facts to memorize:

| Axis | Instagram | TikTok | LinkedIn | Facebook |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Discovery | Mixed graph + interest | Interest-dominant | Professional graph + search | Graph, with local surfaces (Groups, Events, Marketplace) |
| Dominant format | Vertical video, carousel, photo | Short vertical video | Text post, document, short video | Photo, text, link, Events |
| Production cost per unit | Medium | High | Low to medium | Low |
| Composition | Broad consumer, strong 20-45 | Skews younger, widening | Working professionals, buying roles | Broad, skews older; strongest local penetration |
| Content half-life | Hours to days | Days to weeks | Days | Hours |
| Link tolerance | Poor in feed; profile link and Stories | Poor; profile link | Good, though native-first is favored | Good |
| Conversation surface | High — comments and DMs | Medium — comments | Low volume, high stakes | High, especially reviews and Groups |
| Paid overlay | Mature, deep audience tooling | Mature, creative-led | Mature, expensive, precise job targeting | Mature, deep, strong local |

**How to re-derive a row in thirty minutes for a platform not listed here.** Open the platform. Look at what appears in the default feed of a brand-new account with no follows — if it is full of strangers' content, discovery is interest-driven. Post a link and see whether it gets distribution. Look at the top ten posts in your category and count how many are video versus text. Check whether the platform publishes an ad manager with audience tools. Look at whether brands in your category are answering complaints there. That is the whole method, and it will outlast any table.

## Segment Before You Score

An audience is not a demographic. It is a group of people who share a **situation** that your product resolves, and who share **where they are when the situation occurs**. Fernwood's three revenue lines map to three genuinely different populations:

| Segment | Situation | Where they already are | What convinces them |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Local café regulars | Want a good morning, want a place to sit | Local Facebook groups, Instagram, in-person | Atmosphere, staff, convenience, a reason to come Tuesday |
| National subscribers | Bored with grocery coffee; brewing at home | Instagram and TikTok, coffee creators | Taste education, transparency about origin, "the bag arrives on time" |
| Wholesale buyers | Office manager or restaurant owner who needs a supplier who does not fail | LinkedIn, referrals, industry email | Reliability, service, equipment support, references |

Notice the framework already tells you something the demographics never would. Wholesale buyers are the smallest population and the highest value per unit — the average account is worth $340 × 19 = $6,460 in revenue over its life, and at 31% margin that is $2,002.60 of gross profit per account. One wholesale account is worth roughly as much as **91 direct-to-consumer orders** ($2,002.60 ÷ $22.08 of gross profit per $46 order at 48% margin = 90.7). A platform that reaches forty office managers can beat a platform that reaches four thousand strangers, and the framework surfaces that before you spend a quarter finding out.

## The Scoring Matrix

Score **per objective**, never per brand. This is the step almost everybody skips, and skipping it produces the classic error of ranking platforms once and then wondering why the winner never delivers on half your goals.

Use four weighted criteria. These weights are Fernwood's; a different business would defend different ones, and defending them is part of the work.

```txt
Audience presence   35%   Are the people in this objective's population here,
                          in a state where they might act?
Objective fit       25%   Does the platform's discovery mechanism and link
                          tolerance support this specific outcome?
Capacity fit        25%   Can our team produce the format this platform
                          rewards, at the cadence it demands, every week?
Conversation load   15%   What ongoing response obligation does opening this
                          account create, and can we staff it?
```

Score each criterion 1-5, multiply by the weight, sum. Here is **Objective A — add 120 net new DTC subscribers in Q3 from outside a 50-mile radius**:

```txt
Platform    Audience  Objective  Capacity  Conversation   Weighted total
            (x0.35)   (x0.25)    (x0.25)   (x0.15)

Instagram      4         4          4          4      1.40+1.00+1.00+0.60 = 4.00
TikTok         5         4          2          3      1.75+1.00+0.50+0.45 = 3.70
Facebook       3         2          4          3      1.05+0.50+1.00+0.45 = 3.00
LinkedIn       1         1          3          4      0.35+0.25+0.75+0.60 = 1.95
```

Read the reasoning, not just the ranking. TikTok scores highest on audience presence because its interest-graph discovery is the only mechanism on the list that can put Fernwood in front of tens of thousands of home-brewing strangers with no existing following — which is precisely what "outside a 50-mile radius who have never bought from us" requires. It loses the objective anyway, on capacity: a 2 on production cost, because Fernwood's entire video capability is Alex filming between café rushes.

That is not a permanent verdict on TikTok. It is a verdict on TikTok *given Fernwood's current capacity*, and capacity is the one input a business can change on purpose.

Now the same four platforms against **Objective C — open 6 new wholesale accounts in Q3**:

```txt
Platform    Audience  Objective  Capacity  Conversation   Weighted total

LinkedIn       5         5          4          3      1.75+1.25+1.00+0.45 = 4.45
Facebook       3         3          4          3      1.05+0.75+1.00+0.45 = 3.25
Instagram      2         2          4          3      0.70+0.50+1.00+0.45 = 2.65
TikTok         1         1          2          3      0.35+0.25+0.50+0.45 = 1.55
```

LinkedIn went from last place to first without a single fact about LinkedIn changing. The objective changed. If Tomás had scored platforms once, "for Fernwood," LinkedIn would have been closed and the highest-margin objective in the business would have had no channel.

## Now Check Capacity, Because the Matrix Cannot

The scoring matrix ranks platforms. It does not tell you how many you can afford, and this is where plans die. Do the hours arithmetic explicitly.

Fernwood has 12 hours a week. The first thing that comes out is not content — it is the response obligation, because a comment left unanswered damages you whether or not you posted that week:

```txt
Weekly capacity                                        12.0 h
  Reply pass, twice daily, all live accounts           -3.5 h
  Monthly reporting, amortized (4 h / 4 weeks)         -1.0 h
  ------------------------------------------------------------
  Available for planning and creation                   7.5 h
```

Seven and a half hours. Now price the candidates at the cadence each realistically demands:

```txt
Instagram, 4 posts/wk + stories, primary            5.0 h
TikTok, 3 posts/wk (filming, scripting, editing)    4.0 h
Facebook, mirrored from Instagram + local events    1.0 h
LinkedIn, 2 posts/wk, drafted with June             1.5 h
```

Instagram plus TikTok alone is 9.0 hours against 7.5 available. The matrix's top two choices for the DTC objective do not both fit, and no amount of enthusiasm changes the arithmetic. Fernwood's defensible plan:

```txt
Instagram   PRIMARY     5.0 h   Objective A, plus café content for Objective B
Facebook    MIRROR      1.0 h   Objective B; local reach the graph gives cheaply
LinkedIn    SECONDARY   1.5 h   Objective C; owned by June, drafted by Tomás
                        -----
                        7.5 h   Slack remaining: 0.0 h
TikTok      DECLINED THIS QUARTER
```

Zero slack is a real risk, and a reviewer should say so. A more honest version cuts Instagram to 3 posts a week (4.0 h) and holds 1.0 hour of buffer for the week something breaks. Plans with no slack are plans that fail in week five.

**Write down what you declined and why.** The decline is the most valuable line in the memo, because it is the one people relitigate:

> TikTok is declined for Q3. It scored highest on audience presence for our DTC objective and would be the fastest route to reach outside our region. We are declining on capacity, not on merit: three posts a week requires four hours we do not have, and a TikTok account posting once a fortnight underperforms no account at all. **Revisit trigger:** if we hire or reassign four hours a week of production time, or if Instagram Reels performance shows our vertical video is working at a saved-per-view rate above our photo posts for two consecutive months, we run a six-week TikTok pilot with a stated target and kill it if unmet.

A revisit trigger converts an argument into a condition. Nobody has to win the TikTok debate; they only have to watch for the trigger.

## What Choosing a Platform Commits You To

Opening an account is signing three contracts, and you should sign them consciously:

1. **A cadence floor.** Below some frequency, a platform's ranking systems stop distributing you and an arriving visitor sees an abandoned storefront. Decide the floor at selection time and treat falling below it as a reason to close the account, not to feel guilty.
2. **A response SLA.** If people can message you there, you have promised to answer. Name the window (Fernwood uses four working hours) and name the owner. Lesson 04 builds this out.
3. **A measurement plan.** Before the first post, name the one number that tells you this platform is serving its objective. For Fernwood's Instagram that is not followers — it is subscription starts attributed to social. Lesson 06 builds this out.

If you cannot sign all three, do not open the account.

## Failure Modes Worth Naming

- **Competitor mimicry.** "Our competitor is on TikTok" tells you nothing about whether it works for them, and their capacity is not yours.
- **Scoring once, for the brand.** Produces one winner and orphans every objective that winner does not serve.
- **Confusing audience size with audience presence.** Reaching people who cannot buy is a cost, not a reach number.
- **Personal preference laundering.** If the person choosing loves the platform they chose, have someone else run the matrix.
- **Quitting at six weeks.** Most platforms need a full quarter of consistent output before the data means anything. Set the horizon at selection time so the quit decision is a rule, not a mood.
- **Ignoring the conversation cost.** The account nobody wanted to open is usually the one generating the complaints, and an unanswered complaint is more expensive than a missed post.

## Practice

**Part 1 — Extend the framework.** Choose one social platform this lesson did not cover. Using only the seven axes and thirty minutes of looking at the platform itself, write its row of the comparison table. For each axis, state the specific observation that produced your rating. Then name one objective that platform would be the best available choice for, and one it would be a poor choice for.

**Part 2 — Score a real business.** Take a small business you know (or Fernwood's Objective B: lift Tuesday-to-Thursday 9-11am café transactions 10% by end of Q3 among people within 3 miles).

1. Write the objective in the outcome/audience/horizon form.
2. Describe the audience as a situation, not a demographic, and say where they already are.
3. Set your four criteria weights and defend each one in a sentence.
4. Score at least four platforms 1-5 on each criterion and show the weighted arithmetic for every row.
5. State whether social media is the right channel for this objective at all, and how you know.

**Part 3 — The capacity check.** State the weekly hours available. Subtract the reply pass and reporting first, showing the subtraction. Price each candidate platform in hours at a realistic cadence. Produce a final allocation that leaves at least 10% slack, and say which platform you cut to get there.

**Part 4 — The decision memo.** In under one page, write: the platforms you are running and the objective each one serves, the cadence floor and response SLA you are committing to for each, the one number that will tell you each platform is working, the platform you deliberately declined with the reason, and the explicit trigger that would make you revisit the decline. A reviewer should be able to disagree with your conclusion and still be unable to find a step you skipped.
