## Why the indicators matter to you

AJCC certification is how your local board confirms that a center actually delivers what the workforce system promises: effective, accessible, customer-focused service. California's current guidance on certification is directive WSD25-05, and it organizes the review around seven certification indicators. Each indicator is a claim about your center. Your job, whether you sit on board staff, run the center as operator, or manage a team inside it, is to show that the claim is true with evidence a reviewer can check.

This lesson does not restate the seven indicators for you. The directive is the authority, and paraphrased indicator lists drift out of date quickly. Instead, you will learn a repeatable way to read each indicator, decide what evidence it calls for, and spot the gaps before a reviewer does. Have WSD25-05 open next to this lesson and copy each indicator's title and language directly from it.

The one indicator this course quotes is indicator #6, "high-quality, well-informed, and cross-trained staff," because the rest of the workshop builds on it.

## Read each indicator as a claim, a standard, and a proof

For every indicator, ask three questions:

1. **What is the claim?** Restate the indicator in one plain sentence about your center. "Our staff are high-quality, well-informed, and cross-trained" is the claim behind indicator #6.
2. **What standard does the directive set?** Look for the language in WSD25-05 that describes what meeting the indicator looks like, and any attachment, rubric, or tool the directive provides for scoring it. Note the exact words; reviewers score against them.
3. **What would prove it?** List the records, observations, and data that would convince a skeptical reviewer who has never visited your center.

If you cannot answer question 2 from the directive itself, write "check WSD25-05 / ask the state or board certification lead" rather than guessing. A guessed standard produces evidence aimed at the wrong target.

## Four kinds of evidence

Most certification evidence falls into four types. A strong indicator response usually uses at least two.

| Evidence type | What it looks like | Strength | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Documents | Policies, procedures, MOUs, training plans, org charts | Shows intent and design | A policy no one follows proves little |
| Records | Sign-in sheets, LMS completion reports, case file samples, calendars | Shows it actually happened | Must be dated and tied to named roles |
| Data | Customer counts, referral outcomes, customer satisfaction results, performance reports | Shows results over time | Needs a source and a date range |
| Observation and voice | Site visit notes, staff and customer interviews, mystery-shopper results | Shows what customers experience | Should be structured, not anecdotal |

A common weakness is a binder full of documents with no records showing the documents were used. Reviewers notice.

## Build an indicator crosswalk

The working tool for this lesson is a crosswalk: one row per indicator, filled in from the directive and from what you already have.

| # | Indicator (copy from WSD25-05) | Plain-language claim | Evidence we have | Evidence we need | Owner |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | [copy from directive] | | | | |
| 2 | [copy from directive] | | | | |
| 3 | [copy from directive] | | | | |
| 4 | [copy from directive] | | | | |

Keep the indicator column verbatim. Put your interpretation only in the claim column. That separation lets a reviewer see that you understood the directive rather than rewrote it.

## Worked example: Valley Regional Workforce Board

Valley Regional Workforce Board is preparing to certify its comprehensive center in a mid-sized county. Dana, the board's one-stop coordinator, sends the operator a blank crosswalk with the indicator titles pasted from WSD25-05. The operator manager, Luis, fills in "evidence we have" for each row in a week.

When Dana reviews it, she finds three patterns:

- Two rows list only documents. She marks them "needs records" and asks Luis what shows the policy is in use.
- One row cites "customer satisfaction is high" with no source. She asks for the survey, the date range, and the response count.
- The indicator #6 row lists a training calendar but no proof of who attended or what partner programs they learned. That row becomes the focus of the next lesson.

Dana's review takes an hour and saves a failed submission. Notice what she did not do: she did not invent a scoring threshold. Where she was unsure what "meets" looks like for an indicator, she flagged it for a call with the state contact.

## Common traps

- **Paraphrasing the indicator.** Always copy it; interpret beside it.
- **Evidence without dates.** Every record should show when it happened and who was involved.
- **One person's knowledge.** If only the operator manager can explain the evidence, it is not ready.
- **Treating local policy as state policy.** Your board may add local criteria on top of WSD25-05. Mark those rows clearly as local.

## Discussion prompt

In your breakout group, pick one indicator other than #6. Each person names the single piece of evidence they would be most worried about producing in two weeks. What does that tell you about where your center's record-keeping is thin?

## Practice

1. Open WSD25-05 and copy the title of each of the seven indicators into a blank crosswalk table like the one above.
2. For each indicator, write a one-sentence plain-language claim about your own center.
3. For at least three indicators, list one document, one record, and one data source you could produce today, and note where each one is stored.
4. Mark every row where you are unsure what the directive's standard is, and write the question you would send to your board or state certification contact.
