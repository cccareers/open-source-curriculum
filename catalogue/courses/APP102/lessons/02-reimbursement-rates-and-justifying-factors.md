## Why the rate is a decision, not a default

Every OJT contract answers one money question first: what share of the trainee's wage will WIOA reimburse the employer? The reimbursement is meant to compensate the employer for the extraordinary costs of training and extra supervision while the trainee gets up to speed. It is not a wage subsidy, and it is not a reward for hiring.

Many staff copy whatever rate the last contract used. In an apprenticeship that habit gets expensive or gets you an audit finding. Apprenticeship contracts are longer, the wages climb on a schedule, and the employer is often small. The rate you pick drives the total obligation more than any other number in the package, so you need to be able to say *why* you picked it, in writing, before the contract is signed.

This lesson teaches you to:

- name the three rate tiers this course works with (50, 75, and 90 percent) and what opens each one;
- apply the factors that 20 CFR 680.730 tells boards to consider when raising the rate;
- check your choice against your board's local OJT policy and the state guidance in WSD25-03;
- write a short rate justification memo that a monitor can follow.

## The three tiers

| Tier | What it is | What opens it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 50 percent | The general WIOA OJT reimbursement level | Nothing extra; this is the baseline |
| Up to 75 percent | An increased rate a Governor or local board may allow | Documented consideration of the 680.730 factors, within local policy |
| 90 percent | The small-employer rate the course catalog calls the "90 percent small-employer waiver" | Only where the waiver authority applies; confirm the current conditions and employer-size threshold in WSD25-03 and your board's policy |

Three points to keep straight:

1. **The tiers are ceilings.** "Up to 75 percent" means your board can set 60, 65, or 75. Many boards publish a matrix; some leave it to case-by-case approval.
2. **The 90 percent tier is not automatic.** It depends on waiver authority and on the employer meeting a size condition. Do not quote a size threshold or an effective date from memory. Pull the current text of WSD25-03 and your board's policy, and cite the exact section in your memo.
3. **Your board's policy can be stricter than the ceiling.** CCC's course catalog cites board policies that range from a 50 percent cap with a low dollar ceiling at one board to up to 90 percent with a much higher ceiling at another. Your job is to know which world your board lives in.

## The 680.730 factors

When a board raises the rate above 50 percent, 20 CFR 680.730 lists factors to consider. In plain language, they are:

- **Characteristics of the participant.** For example, whether the apprentice is an individual with barriers to employment, has limited work history, or is returning to work after a long gap.
- **Size of the employer.** Smaller employers carry training costs with fewer people to absorb them.
- **Quality of the employer-provided training and advancement opportunities.** Training that leads to an industry-recognized credential, or a clear wage and promotion path, weighs toward a higher rate. A Registered Apprenticeship, with its completion certificate and progressive wage schedule, is strong evidence here.
- **Other factors the Governor or board decides are appropriate.** These can include how many employees are in training, the wage and benefit levels of trainees now and after completion, and how the training relates to the participant's competitiveness in the labor market.

The regulation expects the factors to be *considered and documented*. You do not need every factor to point the same way. You need an honest record of which ones you weighed and how they led to the number.

### Apprenticeship-specific evidence

Apprenticeship gives you evidence that a one-off OJT usually lacks. Use it.

| Factor | Where the evidence lives in an apprenticeship |
| --- | --- |
| Quality of training | The registered standards, the work process schedule, the related technical instruction plan |
| Advancement | The progressive wage schedule in the standards |
| Credential | The certificate of completion issued on finishing the program |
| Employer size | Employer's payroll headcount (get it in writing, dated) |
| Participant characteristics | Eligibility and assessment records already in the case file |

## A decision path you can use on Monday

Work through these questions in order. Write the answer to each one down; together they become your memo.

1. **What does my board's local OJT policy allow?** Note the maximum rate, any per-trainee dollar cap, and who has to approve a rate above 50 percent.
2. **Does WSD25-03 or a current waiver open the 90 percent tier for this employer?** If you cannot confirm it from the current directive text and your board's policy, stop at 75.
3. **Which 680.730 factors apply to this apprentice and this employer?** List each with its evidence and source document.
4. **What rate do the factors support, inside the ceiling?** Pick the number and say which factors carried the most weight.
5. **What is the total obligation at that rate?** Multiply by the actual wage schedule, not a single starting wage, and compare with any dollar cap.
6. **Who signs off?** Get the approval your policy requires before the contract is executed, not after.

## Worked example

**The case.** Sierra Foothills Workforce Board (fictional) is writing an OJT contract with Delta Precision Machining, a shop with 38 employees on payroll. Delta has a registered machinist apprenticeship. The participant is Andre, a 24-year-old who was laid off from a warehouse job and has no machining experience.

**Fictional local policy for this exercise.** Sierra Foothills allows 50 percent by default, up to 75 percent with supervisor approval, and the small-employer rate where state guidance permits. It sets a per-trainee cap of $15,000. (This is a made-up policy for practice. Use your own board's numbers in real work.)

**The wage schedule.** Andre starts at $22.00 an hour. The draft contract covers 1,000 hours. (A later lesson shows how to set the hours properly; use 1,000 for now.)

| Rate | Reimbursed per hour | Total for 1,000 hours | Within $15,000 cap? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 50 percent | $11.00 | $11,000 | Yes |
| 75 percent | $16.50 | $16,500 | No, capped at $15,000 |
| 90 percent | $19.80 | $19,800 | No, capped at $15,000 |

Notice what the table tells you. At this board, the cap binds before the rate does. Choosing 90 percent does not buy the employer more than choosing 75 percent; it only reaches the cap sooner. That is worth saying out loud to the employer.

**The rate justification memo.**

> **OJT rate justification — Delta Precision Machining / Andre T. (fictional)**
>
> **Local policy:** Sierra Foothills OJT Policy, section on reimbursement levels (cite exact section). Maximum available: 75 percent with supervisor approval; small-employer rate only where state guidance applies. Per-trainee cap $15,000.
>
> **State guidance checked:** WSD25-03, reviewed [date]. Small-employer rate conditions: [quote the condition and section from the current directive]. Delta's headcount of 38 (payroll report dated [date]) [does / does not] meet it.
>
> **Factors considered (20 CFR 680.730):**
> - Participant: no prior machining experience; laid off from unrelated occupation; skill gap assessment shows gaps in every work process in the first year.
> - Employer size: 38 employees; one journeyworker mentor will supervise.
> - Training quality: registered standards; work process schedule; certificate of completion; progressive wage schedule.
> - Other: training leads to an in-demand occupation on the board's local sector list.
>
> **Rate selected:** 75 percent. Participant gap and training quality support the increase. The small-employer rate was not used because the cap binds at $15,000 under either rate and [state the reason if the employer does not qualify].
>
> **Obligation:** $15,000 (cap). Approved by [name, title, date].

Keep the memo to one page. Monitors read many of them; a clear one gets read correctly.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the starting wage for the whole contract.** If the apprentice gets a wage step during the contract period, the reimbursement per hour changes. Calculate each period at its own wage.
- **Justifying the rate after the fact.** A memo dated after the contract start date looks like what it is.
- **Citing a directive you have not opened.** Quote the section you actually read, and note the date you checked it. Directives are revised.
- **Treating "small business" as self-evident.** Get a dated headcount from payroll, not from the employer's website.
- **Forgetting the cap.** A high rate on a long apprenticeship can blow through a per-trainee cap in a few months.

## Discussion prompt

In your breakout group, compare your boards' OJT policies. What is the default rate, the maximum, and the dollar cap? Who approves an increase? If you could change one thing in your board's policy to fit apprenticeship better, what would it be?
