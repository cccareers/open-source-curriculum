## Why a routine beats a heroic cleanup

This year's refresh found citations to guidance that had been cancelled or rescinded. Next year there will be new ones. If your office only checks when a monitor is coming, you will always be catching up. A light, regular routine keeps the gap small and makes next year's clinic a short update instead of a rescue.

A good routine answers four questions: **where** changes are announced, **who** watches, **how often**, and **what happens** when something changes.

## Where changes are announced

Build a short list of the sources that matter to your work. For a California AJCC, that usually includes:

- **State directives and information notices.** The state's Workforce Services Directives (WSD) and Workforce Services Information Notices (WSIN).
- **Federal guidance.** The U.S. Department of Labor's Training and Employment Guidance Letters (TEGLs) and related notices, including cancellations and rescissions.
- **Your local board.** Board meeting agendas and approved policy updates.
- **System notices.** CalJOBS announcements and release notes, especially during the modernization.

Most of these sources offer email subscriptions or posted lists. Subscribing a shared team mailbox, rather than one person's inbox, keeps the routine alive when people change jobs.

## Who watches, and how often

Assign roles. A small office might use:

| Role | Task | Frequency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Policy watcher (rotating) | Scan new directives, notices, and cancellations; post a short summary | Weekly or every two weeks |
| Policy lead or supervisor | Decide which local documents are affected and who updates them | When the watcher flags something |
| Document owners | Update their desk guides and procedures | Within the timeline your board sets |
| Whole team | Hear a two-minute "what changed" item | Regular staff meeting |

Your board's local policy may already set a review cycle for board policies. Your routine covers everything else: desk guides, checklists, templates.

## What happens when something changes

Use a simple tracker. One row per new or changed piece of guidance:

| Date noticed | Guidance | Type of change | Affected local documents | Owner | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [date] | [WSD or TEGL number] | New / changed / cancelled / rescinded | Intake desk guide; youth checklist | [name] | Open |

The "affected local documents" column only works if you keep a **document inventory**: a list of every policy, desk guide, and template, with its owner and the directives it cites. You started one in the first lesson. Keep it current and the tracker almost fills itself, because you can search the inventory for the changed directive number.

## Build in an annual sweep

Even with a weekly watch, do one full sweep a year. Pair it with this clinic or the start of the program year:

1. Re-run the citation search across every document in the inventory.
2. Check the status of every directive cited.
3. Confirm each document's next review date is set.

## Keep it small

The routine fails when it is too heavy. Signs it needs trimming: summaries nobody reads, a tracker with dozens of rows stuck "open," or one person doing all of it. Shorter summaries, fewer sources, and a rotating watcher fix most of these.

## Discussion prompt

Who in your office currently hears about new directives first? Is that by design or by accident?

## Practice

Draft a one-page change-tracking routine for your team.

1. List your sources (state, federal, local board, system notices) and how you will receive each.
2. Assign the four roles from the table, using real job titles from your office.
3. Set frequencies that your team can actually keep.
4. Create a blank tracker with the columns above and enter one sample row using a 2026 change from this clinic.
5. Pick the date of your next annual sweep and write it down.
