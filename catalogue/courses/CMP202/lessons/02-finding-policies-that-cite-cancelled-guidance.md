## Why stale citations matter

A local policy is only as current as the guidance it rests on. When a federal TEGL is cancelled or a state directive is replaced, the local policy that cites it does not update itself. Staff keep following it, monitors keep reading it, and the gap between "what our policy says" and "what the rules say" quietly grows.

A stale citation causes three kinds of trouble:

- **Wrong practice.** Staff apply a flexibility or a requirement that no longer exists.
- **Weak files.** A case note that says "per TEGL [number]" points a monitor at guidance that is no longer in effect.
- **Lost trust.** When one citation is visibly out of date, reviewers start questioning the rest of the document.

Your job in this lesson is not to rewrite anything yet. It is to **find** the documents and passages that need attention.

## The words that signal a change

Guidance rarely just disappears. Look for these status words on the issuing agency's page or in the newer document:

| Status word | What it usually means | What you check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cancelled | The guidance is no longer in effect | Does anything replace it, or is the topic simply no longer covered? |
| Rescinded | The guidance has been withdrawn | Which earlier or state-level rule now governs the topic? |
| Superseded | A newer document replaces it | Cite the newer document instead |
| Change [number] | The original was amended | Are you citing the base document, the change, or both? |

The 2026 list for this clinic, as stated in the course catalog, includes two federal items that are no longer in effect: **TEGL 09-22** (youth flexibilities) has been cancelled, and **TEGL 10-23 Change 2** (work authorization) has been rescinded. It also includes new California directives, such as **WSD25-02** (ETPL) and **WSD25-05** (AJCC certification), that may replace or reshape what your local documents say. The next lesson covers what each change means. Here you only need the list so you know what to search for.

## Where stale citations hide

Policies are not the only place. Build an inventory of everything staff actually use:

- Board-approved local policies (eligibility, ITA, OJT, youth, supportive services)
- Desk guides, job aids, and "cheat sheets" taped to monitors
- Intake checklists and document-request lists
- Case-note templates and canned CalJOBS text
- Training slides and onboarding binders
- Contract templates for providers and employers

The informal documents are the riskiest. A board policy gets reviewed on a cycle; a desk guide written by a retired supervisor may never have been touched.

## A four-step sweep

1. **List the documents.** Use the inventory above. Record the owner and last revised date for each.
2. **Search for citations.** Open each file and search for "TEGL", "WSD", "WSIN", "CFR", and specific numbers like "09-22" and "10-23". In a shared drive, a folder-wide text search saves hours.
3. **Check each hit against the source.** Open the issuing agency's directive or guidance page and confirm the document's current status. Do not rely on memory or on another board's policy.
4. **Log what you find.** One row per citation, not per document.

A sample log entry:

| Document | Location | Citation | Current status | Action needed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Youth Services Desk Guide (rev. 2023) | Page 4, "Eligibility shortcuts" | TEGL 09-22 | Cancelled per 2026 refresh list | Remove the flexibility; confirm current youth eligibility rule with policy lead |
| Intake Checklist | Step 3 | TEGL 10-23 Change 2 | Rescinded | Replace with current work-authorization rule; check WSD18-03 |

Notice the second row does not guess the fix. It points to where the answer lives. That is the right level for this step.

## Silent dependencies

Some documents never cite a number but still depend on old guidance. Watch for phrases like "under the current flexibility," "per recent DOL guidance," or "as allowed during [year]." Flag these too. If no one can name the source, the passage needs review.

## Discussion prompt

In your breakout, name one document your team uses that has no revision date. Who would know where its rules came from?

## Practice

Choose one local policy or desk guide you use at least weekly.

1. Write down its title, owner, and last revised date.
2. Search it for every TEGL, WSD, WSIN, and CFR citation, plus any "silent dependency" phrases.
3. For each hit, look up its current status on the issuing agency's site.
4. Build a citation log using the table format above, with at least three rows.
5. Mark each row as **current**, **cancelled/rescinded**, **superseded**, or **unknown — needs policy lead**.

Keep this log. You will use it in the closing project.
