## What "new-collar" means

When most people hear "apprenticeship," they picture electricians, carpenters and plumbers. Those building-trades programs are still the backbone of apprenticeship in California. But a growing share of registered programs now train people for **new-collar** jobs: skilled roles in IT, healthcare, education and advanced manufacturing that often don't require a four-year degree.

CCC's catalog cites a 2023 practitioner survey (Safal/NAWDP) in which about 40 percent of respondents did not know the apprenticeship programs in their own area. This lesson is about closing that gap for your region.

## Common new-collar occupations by sector

The table lists occupations that are widely registered as apprenticeships across the country. It is a starting point, not a list of what exists near you. Whether a program is active in your region is something you have to check.

| Sector | Example apprenticeable occupations |
| --- | --- |
| Information technology | IT support specialist, network technician, cybersecurity analyst, software developer, data analyst |
| Healthcare | Medical assistant, pharmacy technician, certified nursing assistant, behavioral health worker, community health worker |
| Education | Teacher (through teacher residency or apprenticeship models), paraeducator, early childhood educator |
| Advanced manufacturing | CNC machinist, industrial maintenance technician, mechatronics technician, quality technician |

Two cautions:

- An occupation being *apprenticeable* only means a program *could* be registered for it. It doesn't mean anyone in your county is running one.
- A program being *registered* doesn't mean it is *recruiting now*. Many programs only hire when their employers have openings.

## Where to look

Use more than one source. Each one sees a different part of the picture.

1. **Apprenticeship.gov.** The federal site has a program and occupation search. It is a good first scan, but check results against California sources.
2. **DAS.** DAS is California's registration agency, so its program listings are the closest thing to an official in-state record. Search by county or occupation.
3. **Your board's business services team.** They often know which employers are building programs before those programs appear in any search.
4. **Community colleges and adult schools.** Many act as related instruction providers or intermediaries. Ask their workforce or apprenticeship coordinator.
5. **Industry associations and intermediaries.** Healthcare, manufacturing and tech groups often run multi-employer programs.
6. **Your board's ETPL listings.** Registered programs that have chosen to be listed will show up there. (You'll learn why in the next lesson.)

Search tools change. If a link or filter has moved, ask your supervisor or business services lead for the current source your board relies on.

## Worked example: a quick regional scan

Luis, a career specialist at a fictional *Foothill County AJCC*, spends 45 minutes building a scan for his office.

| Program (fictional) | Sector | Sponsor or intermediary | Status | Source | Next step |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| IT Support Apprenticeship | IT | Foothill Tech Partners | Registered; hiring in spring | DAS listing plus a call to the sponsor | Ask for the spring application date |
| Medical Assistant Apprenticeship | Healthcare | Central Valley Health Careers Consortium | Registered; rolling hiring | Business services team | Share the referral process with career staff |
| Paraeducator Pathway | Education | Foothill Unified School District | Planned, not yet registered | Community college coordinator | Check back next quarter |
| CNC Machinist | Manufacturing | Pinewood Machining | Registered; no openings | Apprenticeship.gov, confirmed with DAS | Keep on the list; recheck in six months |

Notice what Luis recorded: not just the occupation, but **status, source and next step**. A list without those goes stale in weeks.

## Talking about new-collar options with job seekers

When a participant hears "apprenticeship," they may think it means construction only. Try a short, concrete line:

> "There's a registered IT support apprenticeship in our county. You'd be hired and paid from the start, take classes one evening a week, and your pay goes up as you learn."

Then check fit honestly: the schedule, the entry requirements, and whether the program is hiring now.

## Discussion prompt

Name one new-collar employer in your region that you think *could* run an apprenticeship but doesn't yet. What would they need to hear to consider it?

## Practice

1. Using at least two of the sources above, find **three** apprenticeship programs active in your region, at least two of them outside the building trades.
2. For each, record: occupation, sector, sponsor or intermediary, whether it is registered, whether it is hiring, the source you used, and one next step.
3. Mark any entry you couldn't confirm with a second source.
4. Write the one-sentence description you'd use to introduce your strongest program to a job seeker.
