## Why a transition needs its own plan

A provider transition is not a contract end date followed by a contract start date. It is a period, often several weeks long, when two organizations, a board, and hundreds of participants are all in motion at once. If nobody writes down who does what, the default is that everyone assumes someone else is doing it.

Think of the transition plan as a handoff contract between three parties: the outgoing provider, the incoming provider, and the board that oversees both. It does not need to be long. It does need to cover four things completely: **caseloads, files, open contracts, and data entry.** Miss any one of them and participants feel it first.

## Set the frame first

Before you list tasks, agree on the basics in writing:

- **Key dates.** Notice date, last day the outgoing provider enrolls, last day it serves, first day the incoming provider serves. Leave a gap between "last enrollment" and "last service" so the outgoing team can close out cleanly.
- **Named leads.** One transition lead from each provider and one from the board. Names, not job titles.
- **Decision rule.** Who breaks a tie when the two providers disagree? Usually the board lead.
- **Meeting rhythm.** A short standing check-in, weekly at minimum, more often in the final two weeks.

Your board's local policy and the contract terms may already set some of these dates. Pull them in rather than inventing new ones.

## Part 1: Caseloads

Start with a full roster of every active participant, pulled from the state system of record (CalJOBS in California) and reconciled against the provider's own lists. The two rarely match exactly, and the differences are where people get lost.

For each participant, capture enough to triage:

| Field | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Program(s) and co-enrollments | Determines who must pick up which services |
| Current service and next step | Tells the new case manager what to do first |
| Last contact date | Flags people already drifting toward exit |
| Open training or work-based placement | Needs a named owner on day one |
| Supportive services pending | Payments that must not be dropped |
| Follow-up status (exited participants) | Follow-up is easy to forget in a handoff |

Then sort the roster into tiers. A simple version:

- **Tier 1 (warm handoff):** in training, in an OJT or work experience, or with a payment pending. Joint call or meeting with the outgoing and incoming case managers.
- **Tier 2 (introduction):** active, engaged, no time-sensitive item. Letter or call introducing the new case manager.
- **Tier 3 (re-engage):** no recent contact. Outreach plan, with notes on how they were last reached.

Participants should hear about the change from a person, in plain language, before their next appointment. Plan a short script so the message is consistent.

## Part 2: Files

Paper and electronic files both need an inventory and a chain of custody. Decide:

- What transfers (participant files, eligibility documentation, Individual Employment Plans, case notes) and what stays with the outgoing provider as its own business records.
- How files move, who signs for them, and how PII is protected in transit.
- Who reviews files for missing eligibility documents before they move, so gaps are fixed while the person who collected them is still on staff.

Record retention and confidentiality obligations come from federal grant rules, state guidance, and your contract. Confirm the current requirements with your board and your state's guidance rather than assuming the outgoing provider's practice is correct.

## Part 3: Open contracts

List every agreement the outgoing provider holds on behalf of participants or the board: OJT contracts, work experience worksite agreements, training provider vouchers or purchase orders, supportive service vendors, leases, and data-sharing agreements. For each, record the end date, remaining balance, and the decision: **assign, close out, or let expire.** Employers in the middle of an OJT should get a call, not a surprise invoice from a new organization. Check your board's policy for how agreements are assigned or reissued.

## Part 4: Data entry

Data entry is where transitions leak performance. Assign responsibility for:

- **Catch-up entry.** The outgoing provider enters all services, case notes, and outcomes delivered up to its last day.
- **System access.** When outgoing accounts are closed and incoming accounts are opened, so there is no day when nobody can enter data.
- **Ownership transfer.** How participant records move to the new provider's staff in the system.
- **A reconciliation check.** A board-run report comparing the roster before and after.

Your state's data-entry timeliness requirements still apply during a transition. Check the current requirement and build the schedule around it.

## A sample plan excerpt

> **Valley Regional Workforce Board: Eastside AJCC Transition Plan (excerpt)**
> Outgoing: Bridgeway Services. Incoming: Northstar Careers. Board lead: D. Okafor.
> Caseload: 312 active, 140 in follow-up. 46 Tier 1 participants; warm handoffs scheduled in weeks 2 to 4.
> Files: Bridgeway QA staff review all Tier 1 files for missing eligibility documents by week 2. Files transfer in sealed, logged boxes; both leads sign the transfer log.
> Open contracts: 9 OJT agreements (board to reissue under Northstar), 14 training vouchers (no change to provider), 1 lease (expires with contract).
> Data: Bridgeway completes catch-up entry by its last service day. Board pulls reconciliation report on day 1 and day 10 of Northstar service.

## Practice

Use a local area you know, or the fictional Eastside AJCC above.

1. Write the frame: four key dates, three named leads, and the tie-break rule.
2. Draft the caseload table headings and define your three tiers in one sentence each.
3. List at least five types of open agreements in your area and assign each a default decision (assign, close out, or let expire).
4. Write the data-entry section as four owner-and-deadline lines.
5. Trade with a partner. Find one thing in their plan that has no named owner.
