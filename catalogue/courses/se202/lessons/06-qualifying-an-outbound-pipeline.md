## The problem qualification solves

Outbound works and now you have a mess. Forty accounts in various states of contact, nine replies of wildly different quality, three meetings held, and a manager asking what your number looks like this quarter. Without a discipline for sorting that, two failure modes take over. The first is **happy ears**: every polite reply becomes "interested," the pipeline inflates, the forecast is fiction, and the quarter ends badly and suddenly. The second is **paralysis**: everything looks equally uncertain, so you work whatever is most recent instead of whatever matters most.

Qualification is the cure for both. It is a repeatable test that answers two questions about every opportunity: **is this real?** and **is this next?** A qualification framework does not make deals close. It makes you spend your hours on the ones that can.

The critical distinction for outbound: **interest is not qualification.** A prospect taking a meeting proves they were curious and polite. It proves nothing about budget, authority, timing, or whether the problem is worth money to them. Curiosity is free; qualification costs the prospect something to demonstrate.

## Frameworks: pick one and use it consistently

There are several standard frameworks and their differences matter less than consistency. Any of them, applied to every deal every week, beats the best one applied occasionally.

**BANT** — Budget, Authority, Need, Timeline. The oldest and the simplest. Strengths: fast, everybody understands it, easy to teach. Weakness: it is seller-centric and it asks about budget too early for a modern buying process, where budget often gets created *because* of the evaluation rather than existing before it.

**MEDDIC** — Metrics, Economic buyer, Decision criteria, Decision process, Identify pain, Champion. Built for complex technical sales with committees. Strengths: it forces you to know *how the buyer buys*, not just whether they like you, and the champion and economic-buyer fields catch the most common ways enterprise deals die. Weakness: heavy for small transactional deals.

**CHAMP** — Challenges, Authority, Money, Prioritization. A reordering of BANT that leads with the buyer's problem instead of your budget question, and replaces "timeline" with the sharper question of where this sits against everything else competing for the same attention.

For technical sales with a buying committee — the shape this course has been teaching — **MEDDIC is the best default**, because it is the only one of the three that explicitly asks how the decision gets made. This lesson uses it. If your employer mandates a different framework, port the scorecard below onto it; the mechanics are identical.

### MEDDIC, field by field

**Metrics.** What number does the buyer expect to change, and by how much? "Better visibility" is not a metric. "Raise technician utilization from 61% to 70%, worth about $1.4M annually" is. If you cannot state the metric in the buyer's own units, you do not have Metrics — you have enthusiasm.

**Economic buyer.** The person who can approve the spend without asking permission. Two tests: have you *met* them, and do you know what they personally care about? A named person you have never spoken to is a partial score, not a full one.

**Decision criteria.** What will they compare options against — formally and informally? Formal criteria are in the RFP or the evaluation grid. Informal criteria are things like "the COO will not approve anything that requires new hardware." The informal ones decide more deals.

**Decision process.** The steps and dates between now and a signature: who evaluates, who approves, whether security or procurement review is required, whether there is a board or budget cycle involved. Deals do not die of bad products nearly as often as they die of an unmapped step nobody planned for.

**Identify pain.** What is the cost of doing nothing? If the honest answer is "they carry on as they are and it is fine," you have a nice-to-have, and nice-to-haves lose to "no decision" almost every time. No decision is your biggest competitor, not the vendor down the road.

**Champion.** Someone inside who has power or influence, gains personally from the change, and will advocate when you are not there. The test is not whether they like you — it is whether they have *done something for you*: made an introduction, sent you internal material, brought a colleague to a call. A champion who has never taken a risk for you is a friendly contact.

## A qualification scorecard

Score each field 0–3 and total it. The scoring rule is the whole trick: **you only score what the prospect told you or did, never what you inferred.**

| Field | 0 — unknown | 1 — assumed | 2 — stated | 3 — confirmed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **M**etrics | No number discussed | You estimated the value | They named the metric and current baseline | They quantified the gap and its dollar value |
| **E**conomic buyer | Unknown | Named from research | Named by the champion | You have met them and know their priority |
| **D**ecision criteria | Unknown | Guessed from persona | They listed criteria | Criteria in writing, incl. informal constraints |
| **D**ecision process | Unknown | Assumed standard | They described the steps | Steps + owners + dates, confirmed back to them |
| **I**dentify pain | None found | Persona-level pain | They described their pain specifically | They stated the cost of doing nothing |
| **C**hampion | None | Someone friendly | Someone advocating internally | Advocated with a risk to themselves |

**Reading the total (out of 18):**

| Score | Stage | What it means | What you do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0–5 | Suspect | Interest, no evidence | Keep prospecting it; do not forecast it |
| 6–10 | Qualified lead | Real problem, unproven process | Work the lowest-scoring field next |
| 11–14 | Opportunity | Committee and process understood | Forecastable with a date |
| 15–18 | Strong opportunity | Everything confirmed | Protect the timeline; hand to the close motion |

Two hard rules make the scorecard honest.

**Rule one: no field above 1 without a source.** Write next to every 2 or 3 who said it and when. A score you cannot source is a guess wearing a number.

**Rule two: the lowest field is your next action.** Not the easiest field, not the one you enjoy. If Economic buyer is a 1 and everything else is a 3, your next action is an introduction request — not another demo for your champion. Deals fail at their weakest field, and reps naturally work their strongest one because it is more pleasant.

### A worked example

```text
ACCOUNT: Northwind Mechanical            Reviewed: 2026-07-27

M  Metrics             2  "Utilization is 61%, we've told the board 70%."
                          — Dana, discovery call 7/21
E  Economic buyer      1  Marcus Hale, COO — from a vendor case study.
                          Never contacted. NOT champion-confirmed.
D  Decision criteria   2  Dana: "must not require new tablets, must not
                          retrain the branch managers." — 7/21
D  Decision process    0  Unknown. No idea if IT security review applies.
I  Identify pain       3  "Every branch review turns into an argument
                          about whose numbers are right." Dana estimates
                          two days a month of her own time. — 7/21
C  Champion            2  Dana forwarded our summary to two branch
                          managers unprompted. — 7/23

TOTAL: 10 / 18  → Qualified lead. Not forecastable.

WEAKEST FIELD: Decision process (0)
NEXT ACTION:   On Thursday's call, ask Dana to walk through what
               happened the last time Northwind bought software of
               this size — who was involved, how long, what stopped it.
               Owner: me. Date: 2026-07-30.
```

Note the discipline. Economic buyer sits at 1 despite a confident name, because a name from a case study is research, not qualification. Pain is a 3 because the prospect quantified the cost herself. And Decision process at 0 is the finding that matters — an otherwise healthy deal with a completely unmapped buying process is a deal that will surprise you in six weeks.

Note also *how* the next action asks about process: "walk me through the last time you bought something like this" gets a far better answer than "what's your procurement process," because people can narrate a story they lived through and cannot recite an org chart on demand.

## Disqualifying on purpose

The most valuable output of qualification is a **fast, clean no**. A deal you disqualify in week one gives you back six weeks of capacity; a deal you carry to week twelve and lose costs you the six weeks *and* an inaccurate forecast.

Disqualify when: there is no pain beyond curiosity; there is no path to anyone who can approve spend; a hard technical or regulatory disqualifier from your ICP applies; the timeline is genuinely beyond your horizon with no interim step; or your champion cannot or will not introduce you to anyone else after repeated, reasonable asks.

Disqualifying is not abandoning. Move the account to a dated nurture — "revisit after their Q4 close, 2027-01-12" — and say so plainly to the prospect. Reps consistently report that being straightforward here (*"it doesn't sound like this is the quarter for it — should I check back in January?"*) preserves the relationship better than a slow fade, and it frequently produces the honest answer that a polite maybe was hiding.

## The weekly pipeline review

Qualification is a habit with a calendar entry, not a one-time act. Block thirty to sixty minutes at the same time every week and run every open opportunity through four questions:

1. **What changed since last week?** Nothing changed for two consecutive weeks is a signal in itself, not neutral.
2. **What is the current score, and which field moved?** A deal whose score has not moved in three weeks is stalling regardless of how good the last call felt.
3. **What is the weakest field, and what is the dated next action to raise it?** Every open deal leaves the review with an action and a date. No exceptions.
4. **Should this be disqualified?** Ask it out loud about every deal. Make yourself say no to something.

Then sort your week by expected value, not by comfort: high-score deals with near dates first, then low-score deals with high potential where the *next* action is cheap, then everything else. Anything that has not moved and has no action gets disqualified or nurtured. That sorted list is the answer to "what do I work next," and producing it is the whole point of the exercise.

## Practice

Use the accounts you have actually touched in lessons 3 through 5.

1. **Build the scorecard.** Create a spreadsheet with one row per account and one column per MEDDIC field, plus columns for total, stage, weakest field, next action, and next-action date.

2. **Score at least eight accounts** from your outreach — every account where you got any reply, plus enough others to reach eight. Apply rule one strictly: next to every score of 2 or 3, record who said it and on what date. Any score you cannot source drops to 1.

3. **Confront the total.** Count how many accounts scored 11 or higher. For most learners after two weeks of outbound this number is zero or one, and that is the correct and useful result — write one paragraph on what that says about the difference between activity and pipeline.

4. **Write the next action for each account** based on the weakest field, with an owner and a date. For the three lowest-scoring fields across your whole pipeline, write the exact question you will ask to raise the score — the actual sentence, not the topic.

5. **Disqualify at least two accounts.** Write, for each, the specific evidence that supports disqualifying, the dated nurture date if any, and the sentence you would say to the prospect. If you genuinely cannot disqualify two, your bar for "qualified" is too low — re-score with a stricter reading.

6. **Run one review out loud.** Present your three highest-scoring accounts to a partner or coach in under two minutes each: score, weakest field, next action, date. Have them challenge every 3 you claimed. Any score you cannot defend with a source and a quote gets lowered on the spot.
