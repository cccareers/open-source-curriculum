## Why a sequence, not an email

Almost nobody replies to the first cold email. Not because it was bad, but because it arrived on a Tuesday when the recipient had ninety unread messages and a budget review at eleven. The same email arriving eleven days later, when the budget review has just told them their utilization numbers are ugly, gets a reply. Timing is most of it, and you cannot know the timing, so you take several shots spread over time.

That is what a **sequence** is: a planned series of touches over a defined window, each one adding something rather than repeating the last, ending in a clean exit. Typical shape for outbound is **four to seven emails over two to three weeks**, interleaved with the calls from lesson 4 so that the two channels reinforce each other. The prospect who ignores your email may remember your name when you call, and the prospect who cut your call short may read the email that shows up an hour later.

Two things govern everything in this lesson. First, **every email in the sequence has to earn its own place** — if a touch says nothing new, delete it rather than sending it, because a sequence of restatements teaches the reader that your name is safe to ignore. Second, **every touch except the last asks for the same specific meeting**. The ask does not escalate, does not soften, and does not turn vague. Consistency is what makes the sequence read as a professional following up rather than a bot cycling.

## Anatomy of a cold email

A cold email has five parts, and each has a job.

**The subject line** decides whether the message is opened. Three to six words, lowercase or sentence case, no punctuation theatrics. It should read like an internal email from a colleague, not like marketing. Good: `dispatch across 4 branches`. `question about Phoenix`. `Northwind + utilization`. Bad: `Revolutionize Your Field Service Operations Today!` — that one is not opened by anyone, ever. Avoid ALL CAPS, exclamation marks, "Re:" on a message that is not a reply (it is a lie and it is transparent), and the word "free".

**The first line** decides whether they keep reading, and it is the most-wasted real estate in cold email. It must be about *them*, not about you. Delete "I hope this email finds you well." Delete "My name is Alex and I work at Northstar." They can see your name; nobody has ever cared where you work in line one. Open with the trigger, the observation, or the question.

**The body** is two to four sentences. State the problem you believe they have, in their language, and give one piece of evidence that you are not guessing. Not three pieces. One.

**The ask** is a single specific question with a low cost to answer. Either a direct meeting request with a time, or a question that is easy to answer with one line.

**The signature** is short: name, company, phone. No banner image, no ten-line quote block, no "sent from" gimmicks.

Length target: **50 to 125 words for the whole email**. A cold email longer than a phone screen is a document, and documents do not get read by people who do not know you yet. If you cannot make the case in 125 words, the case is not clear enough yet — that is a thinking problem, not a writing problem.

### Personalization that actually scales

There are three tiers of personalization, and knowing which one you are doing prevents both wasted effort and generic spam.

**Tier 1 — account-specific.** Something true only of this company: a job posting, an acquisition, a press release, a number of branches. Reserve for tier-A accounts. Highest reply rate, highest cost.

**Tier 2 — segment-specific.** True of every multi-branch HVAC contractor between 120 and 1,500 employees. Written once, used across a whole segment, and it still reads as relevant because the segment is narrow. This is where most of your volume should live, and it is only possible because you did lesson 2 properly.

**Tier 3 — generic.** True of anyone. This is the tier that produces the emails everyone deletes.

The scalable pattern is a **tier-2 skeleton with one tier-1 line**. The problem statement, the proof, and the ask are written once for the segment; the first line is swapped per account. That is roughly two minutes of work per prospect for something that reads as genuinely researched, and it is how a rep sends volume without sending slop.

## A complete four-touch sequence

Here is a full sequence for the field-service example, aimed at Dana Ruiz, newly hired VP of Operations at Northwind Mechanical. Read it as a whole first: notice that each touch changes the angle, and every one of the first three asks for the same twenty minutes.

### Touch 1 — Day 1: the trigger email

```text
Subject: dispatch across the 4 branches

Dana — congrats on the Northwind move three weeks ago.

Every ops leader I talk to who inherits a multi-branch contractor
runs into the same thing in month two: each branch dispatches its own
way, so utilization isn't comparable across them and you can't tell
whether Phoenix is actually underperforming or just counting
differently.

Meridian Mechanical had the same four-board problem and got to one
cross-branch view without changing what each branch already used.

Worth 20 minutes Thursday at 2:00 to see if it's the same shape at
Northwind?

Alex Chen
Northstar | 555-0142
```

Ninety-four words. The first line is the trigger. The problem is in operational language, not product language. One piece of proof. One specific ask with a time.

### Touch 2 — Day 4: the different-angle email

```text
Subject: re: dispatch across the 4 branches

Dana — following up, but with a different question.

When you look at first-time-fix rate by branch, do you trust the
numbers? The contractors I work with usually find the gap isn't
performance, it's that each branch logs a "completed" job under
different rules.

If that's already solved at Northwind, tell me and I'll stop.
Otherwise — Thursday 2:00, or Friday morning?

Alex
```

Fifty-nine words. It threads on the original subject so it reads as a genuine follow-up. Critically, it does not say "just bumping this to the top of your inbox" — it introduces a new, more specific symptom. And it gives an explicit out, which raises reply rates because "no, we're fine" is an easy message to send.

### Touch 3 — Day 9: the value / social proof email

```text
Subject: how Meridian handled the 4-board problem

Dana — short version, no ask attached to this part.

Meridian ran four branch boards on three different systems. Rather
than consolidating, they left the boards alone and put a read-only
utilization layer over the top. Took about six weeks. The useful part
wasn't the dashboard — it was that branch managers stopped arguing
about whose numbers were right.

Happy to walk through what they did wrong first, if it's useful.
20 minutes Thursday?

Alex
```

Seventy-five words. This one leads with substance the reader can use even if they never buy — including the honest note about what went wrong, which is more credible than a clean success story. The ask is still there and still the same meeting.

### Touch 4 — Day 16: the close-out

```text
Subject: closing the loop

Dana — I've reached out a few times about cross-branch dispatch
visibility and haven't heard back, which usually means it's not a
priority this quarter or I've aimed at the wrong person.

Either is fine. If it's the second, who should I be talking to?

If it's the first, I'll leave you alone — happy to check back after
Q4 if that's more useful.

Alex
```

Sixty-three words. The close-out is the highest-reply email in most sequences, because it is easy to answer and because it removes the pressure. Note what it is *not*: it is not passive-aggressive ("I guess you're not interested"), it does not manufacture urgency, and it does not threaten to stop as a manipulation. It genuinely offers three doors — a referral, a later date, or the end — and any of the three is a useful outcome.

### Where the calls go

The sequence above is the email half. Interleaved with lesson 4's calling, a full two-week cadence looks like this:

| Day | Channel | Touch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Email | Touch 1 — trigger |
| 2 | Call | Dial + voicemail referencing the email |
| 4 | Email | Touch 2 — different angle |
| 6 | Call | Dial, no voicemail |
| 9 | Email | Touch 3 — value / proof |
| 10 | Call | Dial + voicemail |
| 12 | Other | Engage with their posted content, or reach a second contact |
| 16 | Email | Touch 4 — close-out |

Two design notes. The **voicemails reference the emails and the emails reference the calls** — that is what makes multi-channel work rather than just doubling the noise. And day 12 is a **second contact** in the same account: multi-threading is not optional, and a champion's peer or their direct report will often reply when they will not.

## Writing rules that hold across every touch

**Write like a person emailing a colleague.** Read every draft out loud. If you would not say the sentence to someone at a coffee counter, rewrite it. "Leverage", "synergies", "cutting-edge", "best-in-class", "reach out to touch base", and "circle back" all fail this test.

**Lead with them, not you.** Count the words before the first "you" or their company name. If it is more than eight, restructure.

**One idea per email.** If you find yourself using "additionally" or a bulleted feature list, you are writing a brochure.

**Never send an attachment or an unexplained link in a cold email.** Both hurt deliverability and both read as risky to the recipient. If you have something to send, offer it and let them ask.

**Ask for something specific.** "Let me know if you'd like to learn more" is not an ask. "Thursday at 2:00?" is.

**Proofread the merge fields.** "Hi {{first_name}}" is the single most reputation-destroying typo available to you. Before any send, spot-check five rendered messages, not the template.

**Follow the rules.** Include a genuine way to opt out, use a real physical address where required, never disguise who you are in the header or subject line, and honor an opt-out request immediately and permanently across every list you own. Requirements differ by jurisdiction — including consent-based regimes stricter than the US default — so follow your employer's policy and legal guidance for the markets you sell into.

## Deliverability, briefly

None of this matters if the message lands in spam. Three things you control:

**Volume per mailbox.** Sending hundreds of cold emails a day from one address is the fastest way to get that address filtered. Keep per-mailbox daily volume modest and consistent rather than spiky.

**Bounce rate.** This is lesson 3's verification work paying off. Sustained bounces from unverified addresses damage your sending domain, and that damage follows you into every future send.

**Reply rate and complaints.** Filters weight engagement. Emails people reply to help you; emails people mark as spam hurt you badly. This is one more reason narrow targeting and short, relevant copy are technical necessities and not just good manners. Ask your operations team whether your domain has authentication records configured — it is table stakes, and it is not something you should assume.

## Measuring the sequence

Track by touch, not just in aggregate:

- **Open rate** reads the subject line — though open tracking has become unreliable as mail clients pre-fetch images, so weight it lightly.
- **Reply rate** is the number that matters. Track total replies and positive replies separately.
- **Meeting rate** is the outcome.
- **Bounce rate** is a data-quality alarm, not a copy metric.

Change **one variable at a time**, and give it enough volume to mean something — a subject line tested on twelve sends has told you nothing. Test subject lines first (they are cheapest to change and highest leverage), then first lines, then the ask.

## Practice

Build the sequence you will actually run in the final project.

1. **Write a complete four-touch sequence** for your primary persona and product, following the structure above: trigger, different angle, value/proof, close-out. Every email gets a subject line. Every one of the first three asks for the same specific meeting.

2. **Enforce the word count.** No email over 125 words; touches 2 and 4 under 80. Count them. Cut until you are under.

3. **Mark your personalization tiers.** In each draft, highlight the tier-1 (account-specific) content and the tier-2 (segment) content. If touch 1 has no tier-1 line, add one. If touches 2 through 4 are all tier-1, you have built something you cannot scale — rewrite them as segment skeletons.

4. **Read every email out loud** and delete any sentence you would not say. Then delete the first sentence of each email and check whether it still works; often it does, and the email is better.

5. **Build the cadence table** for your own two-week campaign, interleaving your calls from lesson 4. Specify the day, the channel, and the touch, and state explicitly which day you attempt a second contact in the account.

6. **Peer review.** Swap sequences with a partner. As the reviewer, answer three questions in writing for each email: What is the ask? What would make me delete this in the first two seconds? Which sentence is doing the least work? Revise from their answers.

7. **Send touch 1 to ten real prospects** from your target list — verified addresses only, from lesson 3's clean list. Record opens, replies, and bounces. If your bounce rate is above a couple of percent, stop sending and go fix the data before you continue.
