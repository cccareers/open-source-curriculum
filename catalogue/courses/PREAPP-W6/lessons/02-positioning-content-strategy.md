## Why positioning comes before posting

You have been posting for five weeks without a written position, and it has probably shown. Some posts sounded like a student, some sounded like a job seeker, some sounded like a developer. Each one was fine on its own, but a reader scrolling your profile got three different answers to the same question: who is this person and why should I care?

A positioning statement fixes that. It is one sentence you write for yourself — not a headline, not a bio, not something you paste anywhere — that decides in advance who you are talking to and what you are offering them. Every post, comment, and connection request this week gets checked against it. If a draft does not serve the audience in your statement, you either rewrite the draft or you consciously decide this one is off-message. Both are fine. Drifting without noticing is not.

Marketers write these for products. You are writing one for the professional you are becoming, which is harder, because you are inside the product and cannot see it.

## The four parts

A working positioning statement names four things:

1. **Audience** — who specifically. Not "companies." Not "recruiters." A person with a job title and a problem.
2. **Value** — what they get. Stated as an outcome for them, not as a list of what you know.
3. **Differentiator** — why you and not the next person with the same certificate.
4. **Evidence** — the thing a skeptic can go look at.

The template, which you will fill in and then throw away once the sentence reads naturally:

```text
For [audience], I [do this valuable thing], unlike [the alternative],
which you can see in [evidence].
```

A first draft usually comes out like this:

```text
For companies, I am a motivated self-taught developer passionate about
leveraging cutting-edge technologies to deliver innovative solutions.
```

That sentence names no audience, offers no outcome, and could describe forty thousand people. Compare a second pass:

```text
For engineering managers at small nonprofit tech teams, I ship small
internal tools end to end — intake form to deployed app — so their one
overloaded developer stops being the bottleneck. Unlike most career
changers, I come from six years of case management, so I can interview
the staff who will actually use the tool. Three of them are in my
portfolio, with the intake conversations documented.
```

Longer, and much narrower — which is the point. Narrow is not a limitation you accept; it is the mechanism. The manager of a five-person nonprofit team reads that and thinks *this was written for me*, because it was.

## The jargon pass

Once you have a draft, run it through three filters out loud.

**The opposite test.** Would any sane person claim the opposite? "Passionate about technology," "hard-working," "fast learner," "detail-oriented" — nobody advertises being unmotivated and sloppy. If the opposite is absurd, the phrase carries no information. Cut it.

**The plain-language test.** Read it to someone outside tech. Every word they stumble on — *leverage, synergy, solutions, robust, scalable, end-to-end,* even *stack* — either gets replaced with the plain version or gets cut. Jargon feels like credibility from the inside and reads as camouflage from the outside.

**The swap test.** Put a classmate's name on your statement. If it is still true, you have described the cohort, not yourself. The differentiator has to be something only you can say: your prior industry, the specific problem you keep solving, the population you actually understand.

Expect three or four rounds. The sentence is short; getting it short is what takes the time.

## Anatomy of a post that worked

Now the second half of the day. Positioning decides what you say; content structure decides whether anyone reads it. Every post that performs well is doing three separable jobs, and when you deconstruct someone else's post you should be able to point at each one.

**The hook** is the first line or two — everything visible before the "see more" cut. Its only job is to buy the next line. Hooks that work do one of a few things: state a specific result, name a mistake, or open a loop the reader wants closed.

```text
Weak:  Excited to share that I've been learning about APIs this week!
Better: I deleted 300 lines of code today and the app got faster.
Better: Nobody told me the hard part of a first dev job is reading,
        not writing.
```

The weak one announces a feeling. The strong ones create a question — *how? why?* — and the reader scrolls to get the answer. Note also that the strong hooks are one line. A hook that runs three lines gets truncated mid-thought and loses its own tension.

**The structure** is what keeps them reading after the cut, and it is mostly formatting plus sequence. On a phone, a five-line paragraph is a wall. Short paragraphs, one idea each, with white space between them. Sequence usually follows one of a few shapes: problem then attempt then result; before then after; or a numbered list of things learned. Specific beats general everywhere — "cut the load time from 4 seconds to under 1" outperforms "improved performance," because the number proves you were actually there.

**The call to action** is the last line, and it is where most novice posts collapse. The most common CTA in the cohort's weeks 1 through 5 posts is silence: the post simply ends. The second most common is too big an ask from a stranger — *DM me if you're hiring.* A good CTA is small, specific, and easy to answer from a phone: a question about the reader's own experience, an invitation to disagree, or a single concrete next step. "What did your first month on a dev team actually look like?" costs a reader eight seconds and gives them a reason to type. That is what turns a view into a conversation, which is the thread you will pick up in the next lesson.

## Deconstructing, in practice

When you take apart a post, be a mechanic, not a critic. Read it once as a reader. Then mark where the "see more" fold falls, and write down what question the hook opened. Then label the structure — how many paragraphs, how long, what shape. Then find the CTA and name what it is asking for. Finally, look at the visible engagement and try to explain it in terms of the parts: a post with high reactions and no comments usually has a strong hook and a weak or absent CTA. A post with fewer views but a long comment thread usually asked a real question.

## Common failure modes

- **The résumé post.** A list of technologies with no reader in it. Nothing to react to.
- **The vague win.** "Learned so much this week!" No specifics means no evidence and no reason to comment.
- **The buried lede.** The interesting sentence is in paragraph four, below the fold.
- **The mismatched CTA.** A gentle reflective post that ends with a hiring ask; the tone whiplash kills the reply.
- **Position drift.** A post that is well made but speaks to an audience your positioning statement does not claim.

## Practice

Work in your own account and in your cohort's shared doc. Everything here feeds this week's milestone.

1. **Draft your positioning statement.** Fill in the four-part template, then run the opposite test, the plain-language test, and the swap test. Bring three versions to the floor. Pair up, read them aloud to a partner who is not in your pathway, and have them say back what they think you do and who for. Revise until their answer matches your intent. Post the final sentence in the cohort doc.
2. **Deconstruct three posts.** Pick one high-performing post from someone in the field you want to enter, one from a peer, and one of your own from weeks 1 through 5. For each, write down: the hook and the question it opens, where the fold falls, the structural shape, the CTA and what it asks for. One paragraph per post.
3. **Rewrite your worst post.** Take the weakest of your own weeks 1 through 5 posts and rewrite it three ways — a new hook only, a new structure only, a new CTA only. Compare all four side by side and write two sentences on which single change did the most work.
4. **Set up the analysis you will finish tomorrow.** List your weeks 1 through 5 posts in a table with columns for date, topic, hook type, structure shape, and CTA type. Leave the metrics columns empty; you will fill them in during the analytics lesson, and this table becomes the spine of your best-performing post analysis.
