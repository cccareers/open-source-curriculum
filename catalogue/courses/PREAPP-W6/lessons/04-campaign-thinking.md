## A campaign is a decision, not a stream of posts

For five weeks you have posted when you had something to say. That is a habit, and habits are good, but it is not a campaign. A campaign has a finish line and a date, and every piece of content in it exists to move somebody one step closer to that finish line. When the date passes, you can say plainly whether it worked.

Your finish line is Demo Day. You want particular people in the room — or on the call — on a specific date, and you have roughly two weeks to make that happen. That constraint is what turns marketing from self-expression into planning, because you cannot post whatever you feel like on a Tuesday if Tuesday's slot has a job to do.

## The five elements

A simple campaign is fully specified by five answers. Write them down before you draft a single post; if you cannot answer all five, you do not have a campaign yet.

**Audience.** Who, specifically, do you want to show up? Not "my network." Name the segments: hiring managers at the kind of employer in your positioning statement, alumni of the program who are now employed, engineers who have commented on your posts before, your current professional contacts from your prior industry. Three segments is plenty; one is often enough. Be honest about size — if the realistic target is twelve people attending, say twelve. Small and named beats large and vague.

**Message.** What one idea should stick? Not the event logistics — the reason to care. "Come see what a career-changer can build in eight weeks" is a message. "Demo Day is May 30 at 5pm" is a detail. Your message should be a direct descendant of the positioning statement you wrote at the start of the week; if it is not, one of the two is wrong.

**Channel.** Where does it get delivered? LinkedIn feed posts are your main channel, but a campaign is more than the feed: direct messages to named individuals, comments on other people's posts, and the one-to-one email or text to a former manager are all channels, and the personal ones convert far better than the public ones. Public content warms; private messages invite. You need both.

**Cadence.** How often, and in what order. Two weeks is short enough that you should be able to see every touch at once. A workable shape is three feed posts a week, front-loaded with story and back-loaded with the ask, plus a fixed number of personal invitations each day. Cadence is also about spacing: three posts on Monday and silence for six days reaches fewer people than three posts spread across the week, because the feed shows each one to a different slice of your network.

**Metric.** How you will know. Pick one primary number that is tier 3 — the conversation tier from the last lesson — and let the rest be diagnostics. For Demo Day the primary metric is people who said yes and then attended. Diagnostics might be reach on the announcement post, replies to invitations, and profile views in the week before. If your primary metric is impressions, you have designed a campaign to be seen rather than a campaign to be attended.

## Worked example

Here is a specification that fits on one card:

```text
GOAL      12 of my target audience attend Demo Day on the 30th
AUDIENCE  (1) 20 nonprofit ops/eng managers in my city
          (2) 8 program alumni now employed
          (3) 15 former colleagues from case management
MESSAGE   "In eight weeks I built the internal tools a small team
           actually needs. Come see the intake app run live."
CHANNEL   LinkedIn feed (public warm-up) + LinkedIn DM and email
           (named invitations) + comments on segment 1's posts
CADENCE   3 feed posts/week for 2 weeks; 5 personal invitations/day
          on weekdays; one reminder to every yes, 24h before
METRIC    primary: attendees from the named list (target 12)
          diagnostics: invitation reply rate, post engagement rate,
                       profile views week of the event
```

Notice what the metric does to the plan. Because success is counted in attendees from a *named list*, the plan has to include personal invitations — you cannot hit a named-list target with feed posts alone. If the metric had been "reach 5,000 people," the same two weeks would have produced a completely different and much less useful calendar. Choose the metric early; it designs everything downstream.

## Building the two-week calendar

Now convert the specification into dated slots. The calendar is the deliverable, and it has to be executable by a person who is also finishing a capstone project — which is to say, realistic.

Sequence the two weeks with a shape. A reliable one:

- **Days 1 to 4 — story.** Content about the work, not the event. Behind the scenes of what you are building, a problem you hit, a decision you made. This warms the audience and gives the algorithm a reason to show your later posts to more of them.
- **Days 5 to 8 — proof.** The result. A screenshot, a short clip, a number, a before-and-after. This is where the invitation gets its credibility.
- **Days 9 to 12 — invitation.** Explicit asks, in public and in private. The event, the date, the link, and why the reader specifically would want to be there.
- **Days 13 to 14 — reminder.** Short, low-effort, aimed only at people who already said yes or engaged.

Every row of the calendar names six things: date, channel, audience segment, hook, CTA, and the diagnostic you will check. A row that omits the CTA is a post that will not convert, and you will know that before you write it.

```text
Date  | Ch.  | Segment | Hook                          | CTA              | Check
5/18  | feed | all     | "The bug that cost me a day"  | ask their worst  | comments
5/20  | feed | 1       | "Intake used to take 40 min"  | ask their process| profile views
5/21  | DM   | 1 (x5)  | personal, references their post| invite to 5/30  | replies
5/22  | feed | all     | 20-second clip of the app     | "want the link?" | comments
```

Two rules keep this from turning into noise. First, **the personal invitations are non-negotiable and they are dated.** Five a day is fifty over two weeks, and fifty personalized invitations reliably outperform a feed post that reaches two thousand strangers. Second, **do not repeat a hook.** If two rows have the same hook, one of them is filler; cut it and post less. A thin calendar you execute beats a dense one you abandon on day four.

## Checking mid-flight

Do not wait until Demo Day to find out. At the end of week one, look at your diagnostics and change exactly one thing. If invitation replies are low, the problem is the message or the personalization, not the volume — rewrite the invitation before you send fifty more. If reach is fine but nobody clicks through, the CTA is the weak link. If a segment is silent, either the channel is wrong for them (managers who never use LinkedIn need email) or they were never really your audience.

Change one variable at a time. Change three and you will not know which one moved the number, which puts you right back where you started five weeks ago.

## Practice

The two-week content calendar you build here is this week's milestone deliverable. Build it for real; you will execute it in weeks 7 and 8.

1. **Write the campaign card.** On one page, specify audience (with named segments and realistic counts), message (one sentence, traceable to your positioning statement), channel, cadence, and metric. Bring it to the floor and defend the metric in particular — expect to be asked why it is not impressions.
2. **Build the named list.** Produce an actual list of the specific people in each audience segment, with the channel you will reach each one on. If a segment has fewer than five real names, either research more or cut the segment.
3. **Build the two-week calendar.** Every dated row carries channel, segment, hook, CTA, and the diagnostic you will check. Follow the story–proof–invitation–reminder shape, and use your best-performing post analysis from the previous lesson to choose the hook types that actually worked for your audience — not the ones you wish had worked.
4. **Draft the first three posts and one invitation in full.** Hook, body, CTA, written out and ready to publish. Have a partner check each one against your positioning statement and against the campaign message; anything that does not serve both gets cut or rewritten.
5. **Pre-register your result.** Write down, before you start, the number you expect to hit and how you will count it. At the end of week 8 you will compare the prediction to the outcome — the habit of committing to a number before the data arrives is what separates someone who runs campaigns from someone who explains results afterward.
