## Your data is small, and that is fine

Open LinkedIn, go to your profile, and find the analytics for your own posts. Whatever you see there is the dataset for today. It is going to look modest — a few hundred impressions on a normal post, maybe a couple of thousand on the one where you announced you were changing careers. That is exactly the right size to learn on. The reasoning you practice on 600 impressions is the same reasoning a marketer applies to 600,000, and you have an advantage they usually do not: you know the story behind every single post, so you can actually explain the numbers instead of guessing at them.

Today's job is not to make the numbers bigger. It is to learn what each number does and does not tell you, and to find the one post out of your weeks 1 through 5 that actually did something for you.

## Three tiers of metric

Every metric on the platform belongs to one of three tiers, and the tiers are ranked. Higher tiers cost the reader more, which is exactly why they mean more.

**Tier 1 — reach.** *Impressions* (how many times your post appeared on a screen) and *unique views*. This tier measures distribution, not approval. An impression costs the reader nothing; it may mean they scrolled past your post at speed. Reach mostly reflects who your connections are, when you posted, and whether the feed decided to keep showing it — it is only loosely about quality.

**Tier 2 — engagement.** *Reactions, comments, reposts, and profile views*. Here the reader spent something. A reaction is a tap; a comment is thirty seconds and a public association with your name. Treat comments as worth far more than reactions, and treat *profile views from a post* as one of the most useful numbers on the whole platform — that person left the feed to go look you up, which is the behavior immediately before a message.

**Tier 3 — conversation.** *Messages received, replies to your outreach, calls booked.* The platform does not report this tier next to your post. You have to track it yourself. It is the only tier that maps onto anything you actually want.

Write the three tiers down in that order, because for the rest of the week you will read every post bottom-up: what conversation did this cause, then what engagement, then what reach.

## Rate, not raw count

Raw counts punish small posts and flatter big ones. Convert to a rate before you compare anything:

```text
engagement rate = (reactions + comments) / impressions
comment rate    = comments / impressions
profile-view rate = profile views / impressions
```

For an account in your range — a few hundred connections, posting a couple of times a week — an engagement rate of 3 to 5 percent is healthy, under 2 percent is quiet, and anything above 6 percent means you hit a nerve worth studying. Do not compare your rate to a creator with 40,000 followers; large accounts reach cold audiences and their rates are structurally lower. Compare your posts to your own other posts. That is the only fair benchmark you have.

## A worked read

Here is a realistic five-week table for a participant with about 600 connections.

| # | Week | Post | Impr. | React. | Comm. | Profile views | Messages | Meetings |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| P1 | 1 | "I'm changing careers into tech" announcement | 1,840 | 74 | 9 | 38 | 2 | 0 |
| P2 | 2 | "5 things I learned about Git" | 620 | 11 | 1 | 6 | 0 | 0 |
| P3 | 2 | Question: how did you handle the pay cut? | 410 | 8 | 14 | 12 | 3 | 1 |
| P4 | 3 | Portfolio project with screenshot | 1,120 | 46 | 6 | 27 | 1 | 0 |
| P5 | 4 | Reshared an industry article, no comment | 240 | 3 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 0 |
| P6 | 4 | "What I got wrong in my first mock interview" | 780 | 22 | 19 | 31 | 4 | 2 |
| P7 | 5 | Shipped a tool, here is the repo | 950 | 35 | 5 | 18 | 1 | 0 |

Read it in tiers.

*Reach* says P1 won: 1,840 impressions, more than double most others. But P1 is an announcement to your own network, and announcements get a courtesy surge — friends, former colleagues, family. It measures how many people already knew you, not how well the post worked.

*Engagement* reorders things. P1 is 83 interactions on 1,840 impressions, a 4.5 percent rate. P3 is 22 on 410 — 5.4 percent — and P6 is 41 on 780, 5.3 percent. P2 is 1.9 percent and P5 is 1.3 percent, both quiet. Now look only at comments: P6 has 19 and P3 has 14, while P1 — the reach winner — has 9, most of which are one-word congratulations. P4 got 46 reactions and only 6 comments: people approved of the project and had nothing to say about it, which is the signature of a strong hook with a weak or missing call to action.

*Conversation* settles it. P6 produced four inbound messages and two booked calls; P3 produced three messages and one call. P1, the post that reached the most people, produced two messages and zero meetings. P4 and P7 — the two proudest posts, the ones with real work in them — produced one message each and nothing on the calendar.

The best-performing post of the five weeks is P6, and it is fourth by impressions.

## Why P6 won

This is the part that belongs in your write-up, because "P6 got the most meetings" is a finding, not an analysis. Look back at the structure from the previous lesson. P6 named a specific mistake in the hook, so it opened a loop. It told a short story with a real, slightly embarrassing detail, which invites a reader to answer with their own. And it ended with an actual question rather than an announcement. Compare P4: an accomplishment post, hook fine, no question at the end — nothing for a reader to do except tap a reaction. High profile-view rate on P6 (31 on 780, exceeding P1's 38 on 1,840) is the mechanical explanation: people left the feed to look at who wrote it, and four of them kept going into the inbox.

Notice too that vulnerability plus specificity beat polish. That is not a universal law of the internet; it is what your data says about your audience this month. State it that way.

## Attribution, and how it lies to you

Attribution is connecting an outcome back to the thing that caused it, and it is genuinely hard because the honest answer is often "several things did." Three traps to avoid.

**The vanity trap.** Impressions are the number that feels best and means least. A post can reach 3,000 people and change nothing. If you optimize for reach, you will drift toward broad, agreeable, forgettable content, because that is what gets scrolled past politely by the most people.

**The last-touch trap.** Someone messages you the day after P6, so P6 gets the credit. But they also saw P1 five weeks ago, and you sent them a connection request in week 3. P6 was the trigger; it was not the whole cause. Record the trigger, and note the prior touches next to it.

**The coincidence trap.** A meeting that came from a classmate's referral, in the same week as a good post, is not attributable to the post. Ask the person. "What made you reach out?" is a normal question and the answer is usually specific and generous.

The practical instrument is a small log you keep by hand, because the platform will not keep it for you:

```text
date | who | what they did | trigger post | prior touches | outcome
2026-05-14 | ops mgr, mid-size nonprofit | sent DM | P6 | commented on P1;
  connected wk3 | 30-min call booked 5/19
```

Two weeks of that log is worth more than any dashboard you will be given in a first job.

## Practice

Use your real account and your real numbers. This produces the best-performing post analysis due at the end of the week.

1. **Build your table.** Take the table you started in the previous lesson and fill in impressions, reactions, comments, and profile views for every post you made in weeks 1 through 5. Pull them from your own post analytics, not from memory. Add a row per post even if the numbers embarrass you.
2. **Compute rates.** Add engagement rate, comment rate, and profile-view rate columns. Rank your posts three times — once by impressions, once by engagement rate, once by comment rate — and write one sentence about how the rankings disagree.
3. **Reconstruct the conversation tier.** Go through your LinkedIn inbox and your calendar for the last five weeks. For every inbound message, reply, or booked call, write a log line in the format above, including prior touches. Where you genuinely cannot tell what triggered it, message the person and ask.
4. **Name your best-performing post and defend it.** Pick one post. In 300 to 400 words, state which post won, on which tier, and then explain the *mechanism* using hook, structure, and CTA — why this post caused conversations when a higher-reach post did not. Include the table and at least two rate comparisons as evidence.
5. **Find your worst false positive.** Identify the post you felt best about that produced no tier-3 outcome, and write two sentences on what you would change. Bring both write-ups to the floor; you will need this evidence tomorrow when you choose what to put on the calendar.
