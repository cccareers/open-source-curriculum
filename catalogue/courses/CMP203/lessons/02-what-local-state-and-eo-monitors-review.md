## Three monitors, three questions

Your operation can be reviewed by more than one monitor, and each one arrives with a different question. If you know the question, you know what they will pull.

| Monitor | Core question | Typical focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Local (your board or its monitoring unit) | Is this provider doing what the contract and local policy say? | Participant files, contract deliverables, local policy compliance, subrecipient procedures |
| State | Is the local area administering WIOA Title I correctly, and is the local board monitoring its own providers? | A sample of participant files, local monitoring records, policies, fiscal and program systems |
| Equal opportunity (EO) | Is every person served without discrimination, and can the office prove it? | Nondiscrimination notices, accessibility, complaint processes, data on who is served, staff awareness |

The local monitor is usually the one you see most. In California, WSD24-11 sets out the standards the state expects for local monitoring, so your board's local monitoring tool is built to meet it. Read the current WSD24-11 directly, and get a copy of your board's monitoring tool — that tool is the closest thing you have to the monitor's script.

The state monitor reviews the local area, which includes looking at whether local monitoring is actually happening and whether findings get resolved. That means your board's monitoring reports about you can themselves end up in front of a state reviewer.

The EO monitor works under WIOA Section 188 and its implementing regulations at 29 CFR Part 38. EO review is not limited to the EO Officer's office; it reaches the front desk, the intake packet, and the case notes.

## What they pull from a participant file

Most monitors sample participant files and trace each one from application to exit. Expect them to look for:

- **Eligibility.** Is there acceptable documentation for each eligibility element, dated on or before the eligibility determination? Check your state's current acceptable documentation guidance and your board's policy for what counts.
- **Enrollment and data.** Do the dates and values in CalJOBS match the source documents in the file? This is where your CMP 201 data validation work pays off.
- **Assessment and planning.** Is there an objective assessment and an individual employment plan (or ISS for youth) that the services actually follow?
- **Service justification.** For training, is there a documented reason the participant needs training, a link to in-demand occupations, and the ETPL or contract basis your local policy requires?
- **Case notes.** Are notes timely, specific, and signed or attributed? Do they explain gaps in service?
- **Supportive services.** Is each payment tied to need, policy limits, and a receipt or approval as your board's policy requires?
- **Exit and follow-up.** Is the exit date consistent with the last service, and is follow-up documented?
- **EO items in the file.** Is there a signed acknowledgment of the EO notice ("Equal Opportunity Is the Law") and, where your procedures require it, a record of any accommodation offered?

## What they review in your procedures

Monitors also look past the file to how you work. They commonly ask for:

1. Written procedures for intake, eligibility, case management, and file maintenance.
2. Evidence that staff know those procedures (training records, sign-in sheets, acknowledgments).
3. Your internal quality review records — what you checked, what you found, and what you fixed.
4. Prior findings and the status of each corrective action.
5. For EO: posted notices, the complaint log and procedure, accessibility arrangements, language access, and EO training for staff.

A procedure that exists only in a supervisor's head will not satisfy a monitor. A procedure that is written but never followed is often worse, because the file evidence will contradict it.

## Worked example: reading a sample file like a monitor

Valley Regional Workforce Board's monitor pulls the file for Maria, a laid-off medical billing clerk enrolled as a dislocated worker and placed in a medical coding ITA.

| Item checked | What the monitor sees | Likely result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dislocated worker eligibility | Layoff notice in file, dated before eligibility date | Meets |
| Right to work | Documentation in file, dated before eligibility date | Meets |
| CalJOBS enrollment date | Matches signed application | Meets |
| IEP | Present, but training goal is "office work" | Observation: goal does not support coding ITA |
| Training justification | No case note explaining need for training | Finding |
| EO acknowledgment | Unsigned | Finding |

Notice the pattern: nothing here required the monitor to know Maria. Every result came from comparing what the file says with what policy requires.

## Discussion prompt

In your breakout group, list the last three monitoring findings your office received. For each one, decide: was the monitor looking at the file, at a procedure, or at EO compliance? Which monitor type is your office least prepared for?

## Practice

1. Get your board's current local monitoring tool and the current WSD24-11. Highlight every item in the tool that touches a participant file and every item that touches a written procedure.
2. Pull one closed, de-identified participant file from your own caseload. Using the "What they pull" list above, mark each item **Meets**, **Observation**, or **Finding**, and write one sentence of evidence for each mark.
3. Walk your front lobby and intake area as an EO monitor would. Note where the EO notice is posted, whether it is available in the languages your customers use, and whether a person using a wheelchair could reach the intake desk.
