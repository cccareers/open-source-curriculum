## Purpose

After watching, a learner can build a post table, compute engagement, comment, and profile-view rates, and pick the best-performing post by conversation tier rather than impressions.

## Audience and prerequisites

Week 6 participants with five weeks of LinkedIn posts. Basic spreadsheet use (typing a formula) is helpful but demonstrated.

## Script

Note to producer: use the lesson's worked P1-P7 table as the demo data so numbers match the lesson. Do not record a real participant's analytics without consent. Platform analytics screens change often; if you show the real LinkedIn UI, re-record when it changes, or use a neutral mock.

## On-screen assets and B-roll

- Neutral mock of a post analytics panel (or real UI recorded with consent and a re-record note).
- Spreadsheet with the lesson's P1-P7 data; formulas visible in the formula bar.
- Three-tier band graphic; trap cards.

## Accessibility

- Captions; every formula is read aloud ("open parenthesis C2 plus D2, close parenthesis, divided by B2").
- Sorting results are announced in narration, not only shown by row movement.
- Highlights use a box and label, not only fill color.
- Spreadsheet zoomed to at least 150%; transcript includes the full table with computed rates.

## Check for understanding

1. A post has 2,000 impressions, 40 reactions, 0 comments, and produced no messages. Another has 500 impressions, 10 reactions, 12 comments, and produced one call. Which performed better, and on which tier? **Answer:** The second, on the conversation tier (one call), and it also has a far higher comment rate (2.4% vs. 0%).
2. What is the engagement rate for 620 impressions, 11 reactions, 1 comment? **Answer:** (11 + 1) ÷ 620 ≈ 1.9%.
3. Someone books a call the day after your best post. Why is it risky to credit the post alone? **Answer:** Last-touch trap: earlier touches (prior posts, a connection request, a referral) may have contributed. Record the trigger and prior touches, and ask them.
