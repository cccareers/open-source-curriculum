## Purpose
After watching, a learner can run the six named editing passes in order on their own draft, record a word count after each, and explain what each pass is allowed to change.

## Audience and prerequisites
Apprentices who have read lesson 03 through "The editing pass" and have a first draft from lesson 02's brief. No tools beyond a text editor with a word counter.

## Script
| Time | Visual / On screen | Narration |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | Cold open: the 127-word "BEFORE" blog intro from lesson 03 fills the screen. A word counter in the corner reads 127. | "This paragraph is a hundred and twenty-seven words long, and when you reach the end of it you still do not know how deep to build a raised bed. Let us fix it, one pass at a time, and watch the counter." |
| 0:18 | Title card: "The Six-Pass Edit". Six numbered chips: Promise, Structure, Sentence, Evidence, Accessibility, CTA and voice. | "Six passes, in order. The rule that makes this work: in each pass, you only fix that pass's problem. If you spot a typo during pass one, leave it." |
| 0:35 | A sticky note at the top of the editor: "PROMISE: You will know exactly how deep to build your bed, and why, in under a minute." | "Before editing anything, paste the promise above the draft. Every sentence has to earn its place against this line." |
| 0:50 | Pass 1 chip lights. Highlight sentences 1-3 ("Raised bed gardening has exploded...", "Raised beds offer a host of benefits...") in a strikethrough style with the label "not the promise". | "Pass one, promise. Read each sentence and ask: does this help the reader know how deep to build? The popularity of raised beds: no. The benefits of raised beds: no, they already chose one. Cut." |
| 1:30 | Counter drops. Remaining text: from "how deep should your raised bed actually be?" to "Let's get started!". Counter shows the new count. | "This is the only pass where the count should drop a lot. Write the number down." |
| 1:45 | Pass 2 chip. Show the lesson 03 heading outline beside the draft: "How deep for most crops", "How deep for root crops", "Patio vs grass". | "Pass two, structure. Read only the headings and the first sentence. Is the answer up top? No: the draft still announces a question the reader already asked. Move the answer to the first line." |
| 2:15 | Type the new first sentence: "Ten to twelve inches is deep enough for almost everything most people grow — tomatoes, beans, lettuce, peppers, herbs." | "Answer first. Detail second. The people who need carrot depths will scroll." |
| 2:40 | Pass 3 chip. Highlight phrases in yellow with a text tag beside each: "comprehensive guide" (filler), "deep dive" (filler), "everything you need to know" (filler), "isn't always as straightforward as you might think" (throat-clearing). | "Pass three, sentences. Front-load, cut throat-clearing, kill intensifiers. Every one of these phrases promises an answer later instead of giving one now. Delete them, and notice the sentence almost never needs repair." |
| 3:20 | Add: "Go to eighteen if you want long carrots, parsnips or a decent potato crop." Counter updates. | "Expect this pass to remove fifteen to twenty-five percent of what is left. If it removed less, run it again." |
| 3:45 | Pass 4 chip. Margin comment bubble addressed to Elena: "10-12 in for tomatoes, beans, lettuce, peppers, herbs; 18 in for long carrots, parsnips, potatoes — please initial." | "Pass four, evidence. Every number is a claim. Route it to the subject-matter expert. Nothing ships that Elena has not initialled. Do not change a fact silently; ask in a comment." |
| 4:20 | Pass 5 chip. Show the rendered draft with a heading list panel. A bolded line "How deep for carrots" is flagged "fake heading"; replaced with a real H3. A link reading "click here" is flagged and rewritten to "see the crop-by-crop depth table". | "Pass five, accessibility. Real headings in order, not bold text pretending. Link text that makes sense on its own, because screen-reader users can list every link out of context. Alt text on the depth photo that says what the photo shows: a ruler at twelve inches." |
| 5:10 | Pass 6 chip. Highlight two CTAs at the bottom: "Sign up for our newsletter" and "See the 12-inch cedar kit". Strike the newsletter. | "Pass six, call to action and voice. One next step. The brief said the kit, so the newsletter goes. Then read the whole thing aloud." |
| 5:35 | Play an audio waveform; on-screen text: "Read aloud." The phrase "Let's get started!" is struck. | "Anything you would not say to a customer standing in front of you comes out." |
| 5:50 | Final text, the lesson 03 AFTER version. Counter shows the final count. A log table appears: Pass, Words after, What changed. | "Here is the after. Fifty-something words, the answer in the first sentence, every number checkable. And here is the log: six rows, a word count after each, one sentence on what changed. That log is a deliverable in Practice Part 3." |
| 6:30 | Split screen: before and after. | "Nothing in the cut half was false. It was optional, and optional prose is where readers leave." |
| 6:50 | Recap card: "One pass, one problem. Count after each. Promise first, voice last." | "Your turn. Take your lesson 02 draft and run the six passes in order. Pause the video and start with the promise." |

## On-screen assets and B-roll
- Text editor with a visible live word counter (any editor; set font to at least 18pt).
- Pass chips as a persistent top bar so the current pass is always visible as text, not only colour.
- Downloadable edit-log template (Pass | Words after | What changed).

## Accessibility
- Captions burned in and as a file; the narration reads out each phrase that is highlighted.
- Highlights use a text tag ("filler", "throat-clearing", "fake heading") as well as colour.
- Final before/after texts provided in the transcript in full.
- Keyboard-only editing shown (selection with shift-arrows) so the demo is reproducible without a mouse.

## Check for understanding
1. During pass 1 you notice "it's" should be "its". What do you do? *Answer: leave it; fix it in the sentence pass. Mixing passes is how edits shuffle text without improving it.*
2. Which pass is the only one where the word count should drop sharply? *Answer: the promise pass, because whole sections that do not serve the promise are cut there.*
3. A sentence reads "Generally quite shallow is fine." Which pass catches it and what do you do? *Answer: the evidence pass (and sentence pass); ask the SME for the number in inches and replace the abstraction with it.*
