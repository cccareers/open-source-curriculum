## Purpose

After watching, a learner can write peer feedback in the where / what / what-instead form against the five criteria, receive feedback without defending, and sort received notes into repeated, single-but-structural, and parked.

## Audience and prerequisites

Week 8 participants before pass three of rehearsal. They have a draft deck and a timed solo run.

## Script

Fictional presenter **Marcus** gives a 40-second excerpt of a results talk. Three peer reviewers: **Ana**, **Dev**, **Sam**.

| Time | Visual / On screen | Narration / Dialogue |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | Instructor talking head. | "You're about to give and get ten to fifteen pieces of feedback before Demo Day. Most of it will be useless — unless everyone uses the same structure. Here's the structure, and what it does to a real talk." |
| 0:20 | Marcus presents (classroom, slide behind him reads "Results"). | **Marcus:** "So after I changed my approach in week four, my reply rate improved a lot — it was way better. And then I did a lot of informational interviews, which really helped me figure out my pathway. Anyway, I learned a ton. Next slide." |
| 1:00 | Freeze. Ana's note card appears: "Great job! You seemed really confident." Label: "Generic praise." | "Here's the most common feedback. 'Great job, you seemed confident.' Kind — and Marcus can't change a single thing because of it." |
| 1:15 | Dev's note card: "Be more specific." Label: "No action." | "Better instinct, still not usable. Specific where? About what?" |
| 1:25 | Sam's note card builds in three labeled parts: WHERE "At the Results slide," WHAT "you said reply rate 'improved a lot' but never gave the number," WHAT INSTEAD "say '7% to 19% across 214 contacts' and leave the slide up for three seconds." | "This is the form. Where — a moment in the talk. What — what happened. What instead — the specific alternative. Marcus can fix that tonight." |
| 2:00 | Five-criterion sheet on screen: Problem clarity, Decision visibility, Evidence strength, Ask specificity, Delivery and time; each 1-4. | "Every reviewer scores all five criteria and writes one where/what/what-instead note per row. The score starts the conversation; the note is the deliverable." |
| 2:20 | Ana rewrites her note live: "At 'I changed my approach in week four' (where), we didn't hear what you changed or why (what); name the one change — 'I started opening with a detail from their recent work' — and why you chose it (what instead)." Label: "Decision visibility." | "Ana tries again — this time on decision visibility. 'Changed my approach' hides the decision. Employer partners listen hardest for decisions." |
| 2:55 | Marcus receiving. He starts: "Well, I didn't have time to—" Instructor freeze-frame, text: "Presenter does not defend." Marcus restarts: "Got it. When you say 'the change,' do you mean the hook or the targeting?" | "Rule one for receiving: don't defend. Take the note, write it down, ask only clarifying questions. If you explain why the reviewer misunderstood, you'll find out on Demo Day that the room misunderstands too." |
| 3:30 | Rule two card: "Point at the talk, not the person." Example crossed out: "You're not a confident speaker." Replacement: "At the ask (where), your voice dropped and you looked at the slide (what); say the ask to the back row, then stop (what instead)." | "Rule two for giving: point at a moment in the talk, never at a trait of the person." |
| 4:00 | Marcus's twelve note cards spread on a table. They slide into three columns: REPEATED (3 cards: missing number ×2, unclear change), SINGLE BUT STRUCTURAL (2 cards: ask too vague, problem buried), PARKED (7 cards: slide colors, talking speed, joke placement…). | "Then sort. Notes that two or more reviewers gave independently: signal — fix first. Single notes about the problem or the ask: fix next. Everything about style or taste: parked." |
| 4:40 | Marcus re-delivers the excerpt. | **Marcus:** "In week four I changed one thing: I started every first message with a specific detail from the person's recent work. Over the next three weeks, my reply rate went from 7% to 19%, across 214 contacts. *(pause, slide stays)* 7 to 19." |
| 5:05 | Side-by-side: original vs. revised transcript lines, with changes marked by underline. | "Three fixes. A named decision. A number with a denominator. A pause. That's the difference between 'it went way better' and something an employer can repeat to a hiring manager." |
| 5:30 | Task card: "Pass three: present to 3+ peers. Every reviewer: 5 scores + 5 where/what/what-instead notes. Then sort, fix the top three, re-time." | "Your turn. Run pass three, review two peers, sort your notes, fix three things well." |
| 5:50 | End card. | — |

## On-screen assets and B-roll

- Classroom recording of the excerpt (actor or consenting participant) with slide visible.
- Note cards with WHERE / WHAT / WHAT INSTEAD labels.
- Five-criterion scoring sheet graphic (matching the lesson's table).
- Three-column sorting animation.

## Accessibility

- Captions with speaker labels.
- Note-card parts labeled with words, not color-coded only.
- Before/after transcript lines provided as text; changes shown by underline, not only by color.
- Audio description for the sorting sequence ("three cards move to Repeated…").

## Check for understanding

1. Rewrite "Your evidence was weak" as where / what / what instead. **Answer (example):** "At the slide showing your automation (where), you said it 'saves a lot of time' (what); state the measured minutes per week and how you timed them (what instead)."
2. Two reviewers independently said your problem statement was unclear; one said your slides were too dark. Which do you fix first? **Answer:** The problem statement. It is a repeated note about a core section; the slide color is a parked style note.
3. A reviewer misunderstood your approach. What do you say? **Answer:** Nothing in defense. Ask a clarifying question, write the note down, and fix the talk so the room cannot make the same mistake.
