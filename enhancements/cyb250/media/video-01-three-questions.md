## Purpose

After watching, the learner can apply the three structural questions (what does it want, what pressure is applied, what would it cost to verify out of band) to an unfamiliar interaction and name each lever operating in it.

## Audience and prerequisites

Apprentices beginning lesson 02, and reusable as a 6-minute segment in all-staff sessions. No technical background assumed.

## Script

| Time | Visual / On screen | Narration |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | Dramatized reconstruction: a service desk at 4:40 p.m., a technician packing up; phone rings. Caption: "Fictional composite." | "Tuesday, 4:40 in the afternoon. You're about to log off. The phone rings." |
| 0:10 | Caller audio (actor), friendly, apologetic. On-screen transcript. | Caller: "Hi — so sorry to call this late. I'm covering for Dee at the facilities supplier. I know you just did the office move on the third, and Maria in the office said you'd be the right person…" |
| 0:30 | Freeze frame. Presenter steps into frame beside the frozen technician. | "Pause. Nothing has been asked yet. But two things have already happened. They used a name and a date — both from a press item on our own website. And they've been warm, apologetic, a little self-deprecating. That's rapport, built before the ask." |
| 0:55 | Lever card appears: "LIKING / RAPPORT" with the quoted line. | "This is the liking lever. People say yes to people they like. A friendly first contact that asks for nothing is a technique, not a coincidence." |
| 1:10 | Resume. Caller: "…the portal's rejecting my login, and finance need the maintenance schedule before tomorrow's invoice run. Could you just confirm which address the invites go to?" | — |
| 1:25 | Freeze. Two lever cards: "AUTHORITY BY ASSOCIATION" (finance, supplier), "URGENCY" (tomorrow's invoice run, 4:40 p.m.). | "Now the ask — small, and tied to finance and a deadline. Authority by association, and urgency. And look at the clock: late-day fatigue is part of the plan." |
| 1:45 | Resume. Caller: "Great — and could you resend it to my personal address? Our mail's still being migrated." | — |
| 1:55 | Freeze. Lever card: "COMMITMENT & CONSISTENCY" with a staircase graphic: step 1 "confirm address", step 2 "redirect invite". Also "HELPFULNESS". | "And there's the real ask. It only works because you already answered the first one. That's commitment and consistency — small step, then bigger step. Plus helpfulness: solving a stranger's problem is literally your job." |
| 2:25 | Presenter to camera. Title: "Red-flag lists don't catch this." Checklist with misspellings, odd greeting, wrong domain — all unticked. | "Run your red-flag checklist. Spelling? Perfect. Greeting? Personal. Suspicious link? There isn't one. Every box clean. Checklists train you on yesterday's lures. What still works is three questions about the shape of the interaction." |
| 2:50 | Card 1: "What does this want me to do?" | "One: what does this want me to do? Strip the story away. It wants an authentication invitation sent to an address the caller controls. That's on the short list of terminal actions — credentials, approvals, payments, files, information, access." |
| 3:15 | Card 2: "What pressure is making me do it now?" | "Two: what pressure is being applied to make me do it now? Say it out loud: rapport, urgency, authority, and a staircase of small asks. Naming it is the interruption. Once it has a name, it loses most of its grip." |
| 3:40 | Card 3: "What would it cost to verify through a channel they didn't give me?" | "Three: what would it cost to verify through a channel the caller didn't supply? Here: one phone call to the supplier's main number from our vendor record — not the caller ID, which is trivial to fake." |
| 4:05 | Replay with the better outcome: technician, calm and courteous. | Technician: "Happy to help — redirecting portal invites is something I confirm through the vendor's registered contact. Let me take your details and call you straight back on the number we have." |
| 4:20 | Caller: "Oh, there's no need for that, honestly, I'm about to leave…" Highlight: "RESISTANCE TO VERIFICATION". | "Listen to that. If the caller is genuine, the callback costs them four minutes. If they're not, they'll try to talk you out of it. Resistance to out-of-band verification is a strong warning sign. It isn't proof: a genuine caller under time pressure may push back too, and a prepared attacker may happily agree to a callback. So the rule doesn't change with the caller's reaction: you verify either way." |
| 4:45 | Presenter to camera. | "Notice what the right answer wasn't. It wasn't hanging up rudely. It wasn't 'I don't trust you.' It was procedure: I verify every one of these, including my own manager's. That makes verification socially free — and that's a design job for the organization, not a personality trait." |
| 5:15 | Recap screen: three questions + "Verify out of band, using contact details you already had." | "Three questions. What does it want? What's the pressure? What would it cost to verify another way? And the one habit underneath them: verify out of band, using contact details you already had." |
| 5:40 | End card: "Now: Practice Part 1 — annotate five interactions." | "Now try it on five interactions you haven't seen. Go to Practice Part 1." |

## On-screen assets and B-roll

- Two actors (technician, caller voice). Reconstruction must be labelled fictional.
- Lever cards (one design system across the course): rounded card, lever name in caps, quoted line beneath.
- Staircase graphic for commitment and consistency.
- No real company names, real supplier brands, or real phone numbers on screen.

## Accessibility

- Captions and full transcript; caller audio is also shown as on-screen text during freezes so meaning does not rely on tone of voice.
- Lever cards use text and icons, not color, to distinguish levers.
- Audio description track for the freeze-frame visuals (e.g., "the technician is packing a bag; the clock reads 4:40").

## Check for understanding

1. In the call, which ask came first, and why does the order matter? *Answer: confirming the address came first; it creates commitment that makes the second, riskier ask (redirect to a personal address) feel consistent.*
2. Why is calling back on the number shown in caller ID not out-of-band verification? *Answer: caller ID is attacker-controllable; the number must come from a record you already had, such as the vendor record or directory.*
3. Name the terminal action this interaction was driving toward. *Answer: redirecting an authentication artifact (portal invitation) to an attacker-controlled address — effectively account access.*
